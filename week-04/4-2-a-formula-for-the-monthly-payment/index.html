A formula for the monthly payment

1. The payment from lecture 4.1

The median sale price of a home in King County in August 2026 was $845,000 [1]. With 20% down, the buyer borrows 80% of the price:

0.80 × 845,000 = 676,000 dollars.

On October 1, 2026 the average rate for a 30-year fixed mortgage was 7.28% a year [2]. The bank charges interest every month at the monthly rate

r = 0.0728 ÷ 12 = 0.0060666…

In lecture 4.1 the spreadsheet ran the balance rule

new balance = old balance × (1 + r) − payment

for 360 months, and we changed the payment by hand until the balance at month 360 came out as close to zero as whole cents allow. The payment that did it was $4,625.28. Taxes, insurance and fees are left out here, as they were in lecture 4.1, so this is the payment of loan and interest only.

Trial and error worked, but it took a 360-row spreadsheet and many guesses. Every new loan, rate or length means starting over.

Task 1 (about 3 minutes, spreadsheet)

The same survey gives 6.60% a year for a 15-year fixed loan [2]. Open your spreadsheet from lecture 4.1, change the annual rate to 0.066, and look at the balance in the row for month 180 (15 years). Change the payment by trial and error until that balance is close to zero. Stop after 2 minutes and write down your best bracket: one payment, to the dollar, that leaves money owing and one that overpays.

Solution to Task 1

A payment of $5,925 leaves $279.10 owing at month 180, and $5,926 overpays by $27.07. So the 15-year payment is between $5,925 and $5,926. Pinning it down to the cent takes several more guesses. Task 6 finds it in one step: $5,925.91.

The question is whether one calculation gives the payment for any loan amount, rate and length, and whether that calculation gives $4,625.28 for the 30-year loan.

We write f = 1 + r for the number the balance is multiplied by each month. For this loan f = 1.0060666…

2. The balance after n months

Write L for the loan amount and P for the monthly payment. Apply the balance rule one month at a time and multiply out the brackets each time. At month 3, the last step takes out the common factor P from the payment terms.

After 1 month: L f − P
After 2 months: (L f − P) f − P = L f2 − P f − P
After 3 months: (L f2 − P f − P) f − P = L f3 − P f2 − P f − P = L f3 − P (f2 + f + 1)

Each month adds one more power of f to the loan and one more term to the bracket of payments. The payment made in the last month has not grown at all (factor 1), the payment made one month earlier has grown once (factor f), and so on. After n months

balance = L fn − P (1 + f + f2 + … + fn−1).

For the 30-year loan, n = 360 and the bracket is the sum 1 + f + f2 + … + f359 from the end of lecture 4.1.

3. Summation notation

Writing 1 + f + f2 + … + f359 takes space, and the “…” asks the reader to guess the pattern. Summation notation states the pattern exactly. The symbol Σ is the capital Greek letter sigma, for “sum”.

4Σk=1 k = 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 = 10

Read it as “the sum of k, for k from 1 to 4”. The letter k is a counter. The number under Σ is where the counter starts, the number above is where it stops, and the expression to the right of Σ is the term you write down for each value of the counter. Two more examples:

3Σk=1 10k = 10 + 20 + 30 = 60

3Σk=0 2k = 20 + 21 + 22 + 23 = 1 + 2 + 4 + 8 = 15

The last one starts at k = 0. Any number to the power 0 is 1, so starting at 0 makes the first term 1. The sum of payments in Section 2 is written this way:

1 + f + f2 + … + f359 = 359Σk=0 fk

The counter runs from 0 to 359, which is 360 values, one term for each monthly payment.

4. Geometric sequences

The terms 1, f, f2, f3, … have a pattern: to get from one term to the next you always multiply by the same number, f. A list of numbers with this property is a geometric sequence, and the number you multiply by is its ratio. You find the ratio by dividing any term by the term before it.

SequenceEach term ÷ the one beforeGeometric?
1, 2, 4, 8, 162, 2, 2, 2yes, ratio 2
1000, 1010, 1020.10, 1030.3011.01, 1.01, 1.01yes, ratio 1.01
2, 4, 6, 82, 1.5, 1.33…no; it adds 2 each time

The second row is $1,000 left alone at 1% a month: the balance is multiplied by 1.01 every month. A loan balance with no payments is a geometric sequence with ratio f, and so is the list of factors 1, f, f2, … in the payment sum. Adding up the terms of a geometric sequence gives a geometric series.

5. The sum of a geometric series

A small case: 1 + 2 + 4 + … + 29

Call the sum S. It has ten terms, from 20 to 29. Multiply every term by the ratio 2. Each term turns into the next one:

S =1+2+4+…+28+29
2S =2+4+…+28+29+210
2S − S =−124…2829+210

The two lines share every term from 2 to 29, and each shared term sits in the same column. Subtract the first line from the second and those terms cancel (crossed out in the third line). Only the 210 at the end of the second line and the 1 at the start of the first are left:

2S − S = 210 − 1, so S = 1024 − 1 = 1023.

Adding the ten terms 1 + 2 + 4 + 8 + 16 + 32 + 64 + 128 + 256 + 512 also gives 1023.

The general case

The same steps work for any ratio f and any number of terms n:

S =1+f+f2+…+fn−1
f S =f+f2+…+fn−1+fn
f S − S =−1ff2…fn−1+fn

The shared terms cancel, so f S − S = fn − 1. Take out the common factor S on the left:

S (f − 1) = fn − 1

Divide both sides by f − 1. This needs f ≠ 1, which holds whenever the interest rate is not zero.

1 + f + f2 + … + fn−1 = n−1Σk=0 fk = fn − 1f − 1

In words: raise the ratio to the number of terms, subtract 1, and divide by the ratio minus 1. With f = 2 and n = 10 it gives (1024 − 1)/(2 − 1) = 1023, as above. With the mortgage factor f = 1.0060666… and n = 360 it gives (8.82335226 − 1)/0.0060666… = 1289.56.

6. A formula for the monthly payment

Section 2 gave the balance after n months. Replace the sum by the formula from Section 5:

balance after n months = L fn − P · fn − 1f − 1

The loan is paid off when this balance is zero. That gives an equation with one unknown, P, which we solve as in week 2.

L fn − P · (fn − 1)/(f − 1) = 0
L fn = P · (fn − 1)/(f − 1)   (add the payment term to both sides)
L fn (f − 1) = P (fn − 1)   (multiply both sides by f − 1)
L (f − 1) fn / (fn − 1) = P   (divide both sides by fn − 1)

Since f = 1 + r, the factor f − 1 is just r. The monthly payment is

P = L · r · fnfn − 1 with r = annual rate ÷ 12, f = 1 + r, n = number of months.

The formula also says how big the answer should be. L · r is the first month’s interest, $4,101.07 for our loan. The fraction fn/(fn − 1) is a little more than 1 when fn is large (here about 1.13). So for a 30-year loan the payment is a bit more than the first month’s interest, which matches what lecture 4.1 found: early payments are mostly interest.

Task 5 (about 5 minutes, calculator)

Use the boxed formula for the loan of $676,000 at 7.28% for 30 years. Write down each of these, keeping every digit your calculator shows:

  1. r, f and n;
  2. fn;
  3. the top of the fraction, L · r · fn, and the bottom, fn − 1;
  4. P, rounded to the cent. Does it match $4,625.28?
Solution to Task 5
  1. r = 0.0728 ÷ 12 = 0.006066666…, f = 1.006066666…, n = 30 × 12 = 360.
  2. f360 = 8.82335226 (to eight decimal places). Without any payments the balance would grow to almost 9 times the loan in 30 years.
  3. Top: 676,000 × 0.006066666… = 4,101.0666…, and 4,101.0666… × 8.82335226 = 36,185.1558. Bottom: 8.82335226 − 1 = 7.82335226.
  4. P = 36,185.1558 ÷ 7.82335226 = 4,625.2750…, which rounds to $4,625.28. This is the spreadsheet’s answer from lecture 4.1, to the cent.

On most calculators the whole formula fits in one line. The brackets around the bottom are needed, because without them the calculator divides by f360 first and subtracts 1 afterwards:

676000 × (0.0728 ÷ 12) × (1 + 0.0728 ÷ 12)^360 ÷ ((1 + 0.0728 ÷ 12)^360 − 1)

This line is for a calculator. The spreadsheet version is cell B9 in Section 7.

Rounding too early changes the answer. The exact payment is $4,625.275…, only a few thousandths of a cent above the point where it would round down. Even rounding f360 to 8.8234 gives $4,625.27, and rounding r to 0.0061 gives $4,643.65, which is $18.37 too much. Keep all the digits until the last step. A bank charges whole cents, so the payment is rounded to $4,625.28, the amount lecture 4.1 found.

7. The payment in one spreadsheet cell

Put the three inputs (loan, rate, years) in their own cells and the formula in one more cell. Then a new loan only means typing new inputs. Each formula refers to other cells in column B, and nothing is copied down, so no fixed references are needed.

AB
1ItemValue
2Loan amount L676000
3Annual rate0.0728
4Years30
5Monthly rate r=B3/12
6Number of months n=B4*12
7f = 1 + r=1+B5
8Monthly payment P=B2*B5*B7^B6/(B7^B6-1)
9Payment, all in one cell=B2*(B3/12)*(1+B3/12)^(B4*12)/((1+B3/12)^(B4*12)-1)
10Total paid=ROUND(B8,2)*B6
11Total interest=B10-B2

The symbol ^ means “to the power”. Cell B9 does the whole calculation from the three inputs, so B8 and B9 should show the same number. ROUND(B8,2) rounds the payment to the cent, the amount actually paid each month, before multiplying by the number of months. On a phone, scroll the table sideways to see the whole formula in B9.

These formulas and files were not tested in Excel or Google Sheets. The copied layout is tab-separated, so pasting it into cell A1 should fill columns A and B. If a formula shows as text, retype it in the cell starting with the = sign.

8. What-if questions: price and down payment

The payment is the loan amount times a number that depends only on the rate and the length:

P = L × r · fnfn − 1

Doubling the loan doubles the payment. At 7.28% for 30 years that number is 0.006842, which means about $6.84 a month for every $1,000 borrowed (4,625.28 ÷ 676 = 6.84). That is a quick way to estimate a payment before computing it exactly.

The price only enters through L. A smaller down payment raises L, and so raises the payment in the same proportion.

Task 7 (about 4 minutes, spreadsheet or calculator)
