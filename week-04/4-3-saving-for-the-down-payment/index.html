Saving for the down payment

1. The down payment and saving with no interest

In lecture 4.1 we used the King County median sale price for August 2026, $845,000 [1]. A 20% down payment on that price is

0.20 × $845,000 = $169,000,

which left a loan of $676,000. At the 30-year fixed rate of 7.28% [2], the monthly payment on that loan is $4,625.28.

Before any of that, the buyer needs $169,000 in the bank. Three questions follow from that: how long it takes to save $169,000, how much interest on the savings helps, and how much house a monthly budget pays for if prices keep rising.

For the examples we use a savings budget of $2,000 a month. This amount is made up for the example.

Task 1 (about 3 minutes, calculator)

Put $2,000 a month into an account that pays no interest.

  1. How many months until the account holds $169,000? Write the answer in years and months.
  2. Write the balance S after n months as a formula. What is its slope, with units?
Solution to Task 1

1. 169,000 ÷ 2,000 = 84.5. After 84 months the account holds $168,000, which is short. After 85 months it holds $170,000. So it takes 85 months, which is 7 years and 1 month.

2. S = 2000n. This is a line from week 2, y = mx + b, with the number of months n in place of x, slope m = 2000 and intercept b = 0. The slope is $2,000 per month: each extra month adds the same $2,000.

2. Interest on savings as a growth factor

Banks pay interest on savings accounts. Two rates from this fall:

  • The national average savings rate was 0.37% per year in September 2026 [3]. This is an average over US banks and credit unions that report rates, weighted by their deposits.
  • One online bank, Ally Bank, paid 3.10% per year on its savings account as of October 1, 2026 [4]. This is one bank's rate, chosen as an example of a high-yield account. The rate can change at any time.

Banks state these rates as an annual percentage yield (APY): the percent the balance grows in one year. To work month by month, as in lecture 4.1, we divide the yearly rate by 12. For Ally that gives a monthly rate of

r = 0.031 ÷ 12 = 0.0025833…

This is a simplification. Ally adds interest daily, and the APY already includes that. Dividing by 12 makes the balance grow by about 3.14% in a year instead of 3.10%. The difference is small for most of our questions, but it is not always zero: Task 6 shows a case where it moves the answer by two months.

The balance rule with no payments

In lecture 4.1 the loan balance followed the rule

new balance = old balance × (1 + monthly rate) − payment.

A savings account follows the same rule with no payment taken out. If you leave the money alone,

new balance = old balance × (1 + r).

As in lecture 4.2, r is the monthly rate and the number f = 1 + r is the growth factor: each month the balance is multiplied by f. For Ally, f = 1.0025833…. Keep all the digits in your calculator. Suppose you have $20,000 saved already (a made-up amount). After one month the balance is 20,000 × 1.0025833… = $20,051.67.

Task 2 (about 4 minutes, calculator)

Start with $20,000 at Ally's rate, f = 1 + 0.031/12, and add no more money.

  1. Find the balance after 1, 2 and 3 months by multiplying by f again each month.
  2. Find the balance after 12 months and after 120 months. Use the power key: after 12 months the balance is 20,000 × f12.
  3. Find the balance after 120 months at the national average rate, f = 1 + 0.0037/12.
Solution to Task 2

1. Month 1: 20,000 × f = $20,051.67. Month 2: 20,051.67… × f = 20,000 × f2 = $20,103.47. Month 3: 20,000 × f3 = $20,155.40.

2. After 12 months: 20,000 × f12 = $20,628.89. After 120 months (10 years): 20,000 × f120 = $27,257.60.

3. With f = 1.00030833…: 20,000 × f120 = $20,753.74. In ten years the national average rate earns $753.74. Ally's rate earns $7,257.60.

On a calculator, type 20000*(1+0.031/12)^120. The parentheses make the calculator add before it raises to the power, by the order of operations from week 1.

3. The function A(t) = A0(1 + r)t and its graph

Task 2 multiplied by the same factor again and again. After n months the starting amount has been multiplied by f a total of n times. Written as a function of the time t:

A(t) = A0(1 + r)t = A0 · ft.

Here A0 is the starting amount, r is the monthly rate and the time t is counted in months, so A(n) is the balance after n months. This is a function: a rule that takes an input and gives exactly one output. The input is the time t. The output is the balance A(t). For example, with A0 = 20,000 and r = 0.031/12, the input t = 12 gives the output A(12) = $20,628.89. The exponent t counts how many times we multiply by f. A function of this form is called exponential because the input sits in the exponent.

Comparing linear and exponential growth

Interest is easier to see over many years, so here we count in years. Since the APY is the yearly growth, the balance is multiplied by 1.031 each year. We call this yearly function E:

E(t) = 20,000 × 1.031t, with t in years.

In its first year this account earns 20,000 × 0.031 = $620. For the linear comparison, put the same $620 into an account yourself every year, with no interest, as in Task 1. That balance is the line

K(t) = 20,000 + 620t,

with slope $620 per year. Both start at $20,000 and both grow by $620 in the first year, so any gap between them comes from multiplying instead of adding. Task 1's line S = 2000n appears in Figure 2 in section 7, next to a savings plan that earns interest. Both functions here assume the rate stays at 3.10% for 40 years, which no bank promises.

From year 39 to year 40 the line grows by $620, the same as in every year. The exponential grows by 67,822.94 − 65,783.65 = $2,039.29, because it gains 3.1% of a balance that is now much larger than $20,000.

E(10) = $27,140.43 is $117.17 less than Task 2’s 10-year balance of $27,257.60 for the same $20,000. Task 2 divided the rate by 12, which makes the balance grow about 3.14% a year (section 2). The factor 1.031 a year is the bank’s actual yearly growth.

This is the difference between the two kinds of growth:

  • Linear growth adds the same amount each period. That amount is the slope.
  • Exponential growth multiplies by the same factor each period. Here every year's balance is 1.031 times the year before, so the amount added keeps getting larger.

4. Monthly deposits and the geometric sum

A real savings plan adds money every month and earns interest. Put $2,000 into Ally's account at the end of each month, starting from $0. The balance rule becomes

new balance = old balance × f + 2,000.

Write out the first three months with f = 1 + 0.031/12:

MonthBalance as a sumBalance
12,000$2,000.00
22,000f + 2,000$4,005.17
32,000f2 + 2,000f + 2,000$6,015.51

Each deposit grows by its own factor: the first deposit has had two months of interest by month 3, the second has had one, the newest has had none. After n months the balance is

2,000 × (1 + f + f2 + … + fn−1).

The bracket is the geometric series from lecture 4.2, with ratio f and n terms:

1 + f + f2 + … + fn−1 = n−1Σk=0 fk = fn − 1f − 1

Since f = 1 + r, the bottom f − 1 is just r. So a deposit D at the end of each month gives a balance after n months of

B(n) = D × fn − 1r.

Example. After 84 months (7 years): f84 = 1.2419…, so

B(84) = 2,000 × (1.2419… − 1) / 0.0025833… = $187,352.15.

The deposits add up to 84 × 2,000 = $168,000, so interest contributed $19,352.15. Without interest, 84 months fell short of $169,000. With Ally's rate, 84 months is more than enough.

The deposit needed for a deadline

The formula also runs the other way. To have $169,000 after 60 months (5 years), we need D with

D × (f60 − 1) / r = 169,000.

Compute the number that multiplies D first: (f60 − 1)/r = 64.8095… Then divide both sides by it: D = 169,000 ÷ 64.8095… = $2,607.64 a month. With no interest the deposit would be 169,000 ÷ 60 = $2,816.67 a month.

6. The loan a monthly payment supports

Lecture 4.2 gave the monthly payment P on a loan amount L:

P = L · r · fnfn − 1 with r = annual rate ÷ 12, f = 1 + r, n = number of months.

In this section r and f belong to the mortgage, r = 0.0728 ÷ 12, not to the savings account of sections 2 to 5.

A buyer usually knows the payment they can afford and wants the loan. Solve for L: multiply both sides by fn − 1, then divide both sides by r · fn:

L = P · fn − 1r · fn.

Check. At 7.28% for 30 years, r = 0.0728 ÷ 12 = 0.0060666…, f = 1.0060666… and n = 360, so f360 = 8.8233… Then

f360 − 1r · f360 = 146.153…

Each dollar of monthly payment supports $146.15 of loan. The payment from lecture 4.1 gives 4,625.28 × 146.153… = $676,000.73. That is the $676,000 loan, off by 73 cents because the payment was rounded to the cent.

Task 5 (about 4 minutes, calculator)

A buyer can pay $4,000 a month (a made-up budget) on a 30-year loan at 7.28%. As in lectures 4.1 and 4.2, the whole $4,000 goes to loan and interest; property tax and insurance are left out.

  1. Find the largest loan this payment supports.
  2. The loan is 80% of the price when the buyer puts 20% down. Find the price of the home and the down payment.
Solution to Task 5

1. L = 4,000 × 146.153… = $584,613.88.

2. Price × 0.80 = 584,613.88, so the price is 584,613.88 ÷ 0.80 = $730,767.36. The down payment is 0.20 × 730,767.36 = $146,153.47. A $4,000 budget does not reach the $845,000 median price at today's rate.

7. Home price growth as another exponential

While the buyer saves, home prices change. The Federal Housing Finance Agency publishes a house price index for the Seattle-Bellevue-Kent metropolitan division, which is King County [5]. An index is a number that tracks prices relative to a starting point; this one is set to 100 in early 1995. Only ratios of index values matter. If the index goes from 297.78 to 554.91, prices were multiplied by 554.91 ÷ 297.78.

The yearly factor g is the number that, multiplied 10 times, gives the 10-year factor: g10 = 1.8635. It can be found by trial, as we found the mortgage payment in lecture 4.1:

Try gg10Compared with 1.8635

So prices grew about 6.42% a year on average over the 10 years. Over the last 5 years the average was 4.49% a year, and over the last year 0.33%. A different measure points the other way: the median sale price over the whole NWMLS area (27 counties) fell 2.3% from August 2025 to August 2026 [1]. Past growth rates differ a lot depending on the period, and none of them tells us the future. One more simplification: the index tracks price changes of the same homes over time, including appraisals for refinancing, and we apply its growth to the King County median sale price.

If prices grow by a factor g each year, the price after t years is another exponential function:

H(t) = 845,000 × gt.

Example. At the 10-year average, g = 1.0642. After 7 years the price is 845,000 × 1.06427 = $1,306,229.51. The 20% down payment becomes $261,245.90, and the loan is $1,044,983.61. If the mortgage rate is still 7.28% then (a simplification), the payment on that loan is 1,044,983.61 ÷ 146.153… = $7,149.91 a month.

At the end of year 12 the goal is larger; at the end of year 13 the savings are larger. The savings are still short at month 144 and ahead at month 156, so they catch the goal during year 13, between months 144 and 156. Part 1 gives month 152.

The wait nearly doubles, from 77 to 152 months, because the goal is an exponential that starts large. In the first year at 6.42% the goal rises by 169,000 × 0.0642 = $10,849.80, while the savings rise by 24,000 in deposits plus $343.95 in interest. The savings gain 24,343.95 − 10,849.80 = $13,494.15 on the goal that year. Each year the goal’s rise is 6.42% of a larger amount: from year 12 to year 13 it rises by $22,892.90.

Both ranges compared here run between real rates from the sources. Moving the savings rate from the national average (0.37%) to Ally’s 3.10% saved 7 months (from 84 to 77, Task 4 and Figure 2). Moving price growth from last year’s 0.33% to the 10-year average of 6.42% adds 152 − 79 = 73 months. Over these ranges, price growth changes the answer far more than the savings rate does.

The months in the table come from dividing 3.10% by 12 (section 2). With the bank’s actual growth of 3.10% a year, a monthly factor of 1.0311/12, the 0%, 0.33% and 4.49% months stay the same, but the 6.42% case reaches the goal in month 154 instead of 152. So read month 152 as an answer within this model, about 12½ to 13 years, not as an exact date.
