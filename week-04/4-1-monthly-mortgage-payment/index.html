What will the monthly payment be?

1. Mortgages

In August 2026, the median sale price of homes and condominiums in King County was $845,000.[1] About half of the homes sold that month went for more, and about half for less.

Most buyers pay part of the price up front and borrow the rest. The part paid up front is the down payment. Here the buyer puts down 20% of the price. Twenty percent is a common benchmark. It is not a requirement.

The borrowed amount is a mortgage. It is repaid in equal monthly payments over 30 years, which is 360 payments. On October 1, 2026, the average interest rate on a 30-year fixed-rate mortgage was 7.28% per year.[2]

A real monthly housing bill also includes property taxes, insurance, and fees. These are left out here, as a simplification, and only the loan payment is computed.

Task 1 (about 3 minutes, calculator)

Write down four numbers:

  1. the down payment, 20% of $845,000;
  2. the amount borrowed;
  3. the interest rate per month, as a decimal (the yearly rate is 7.28%);
  4. the interest charged for the first month on the amount borrowed.
Solution to Task 1

(a) A percent is a number of hundredths: 20% = 20/100 = 0.20. To take 20% of a number, multiply by 0.20.

Down payment = 0.20 × 845,000 = $169,000

(b) The buyer borrows the rest: 845,000 − 169,000 = $676,000. The same answer comes in one step from the 80% that is left: 0.80 × 845,000 = 676,000.

(c) Mortgage rates are quoted per year. For a fixed-rate mortgage like this one, the monthly rate is the yearly rate divided by 12.

Monthly rate as a percent: 7.28% ÷ 12 = 0.60667% per month
Monthly rate as a decimal: 0.0728 ÷ 12 = 0.0060667…

The decimal goes on forever (0.006066666…), so 0.0060667 is rounded.

(d) Interest for a month is the balance times the monthly rate. Type the whole calculation in one go, so the calculator keeps every digit of the rate:

676000 × 0.0728 ÷ 12 = 4,101.0667, so the first month's interest is $4,101.07

Typing the rounded rate gives a different cent: 676,000 × 0.0060667 = 4,101.0892, or $4,101.09. A rounded rate is multiplied by a large balance, so a small rounding error in the rate becomes cents in the answer.

The answer to (d) sets a floor. If the monthly payment were $4,101.07, it would only cover the interest, and after a month the buyer would still owe $676,000. The payment has to be larger than that. The question is how much larger.

2. Small example

The real loan runs 360 months. To see how the balance changes, start with a short loan. The numbers in this section are made up for practice. Someone borrows $1,200 at 12% per year and repays it in 3 equal monthly payments. The monthly rate is 12% ÷ 12 = 1% = 0.01.

The balance is the amount still owed. Each month, the lender adds interest to the balance and the borrower's payment is subtracted:

interest = old balance × monthly rate
new balance = old balance + interest − payment

The new balance becomes the old balance for the next month.

Month 1: interest = 1,200 × 0.01 = 12.00, and new balance = 1,200 + 12 − 400 = 812. Month 2 starts from 812. In month 3, 420.12 × 0.01 = 4.2012, which rounds to 4.20.

After three payments the borrower still owes $24.32, so $400 is too small. Three payments of $400 add up to exactly the $1,200 borrowed, and nothing is left over for the interest.

A percentage as a multiplier

The interest step and the adding step can be done as one. Adding 1% interest to a balance is the same as multiplying it by 1.01, because

balance + 0.01 × balance = (1 + 0.01) × balance = 1.01 × balance

So the rule for the small loan becomes

new balance = 1.01 × old balance − payment

Month 1 is 1.01 × 1,200 − 400 = 812, as in the table. In general, adding a percentage to a number means multiplying by 1 plus that percentage as a decimal. The 1.01 is called the growth factor for one month.

On a calculator, multiplication is done before subtraction, so 1.01 × 1200 − 400 gives 812 typed in that order. A calculator that works strictly left to right would turn 1200 + 1200 × 0.01 into (1200 + 1200) × 0.01 = 24 in place of 1,212. Typing 1.01 × 1200 first, or using parentheses, gives the right result on either kind.

Task 3 (about 4 minutes, calculator)

Use new balance = 1.01 × old balance − payment.

  1. Find the balance after month 3 with a payment of $410. Is $410 too small or too large?
  2. Do the same for $405, halfway between $400 and $410.

Write down the three balances after month 3, for $400, $405 and $410.

Solution to Task 3

(a) With $410: 1.01 × 1,200 − 410 = 802.00, then 1.01 × 802.00 − 410 = 400.02, then 1.01 × 400.02 − 410 = −5.98. A negative balance means the borrower paid $5.98 too much. So $410 is too large, and the right payment is between $400 (owes $24.32) and $410 (overpays $5.98).

(b) With $405: 1,200 → 807.00 → 410.07 → 9.17. The borrower still owes $9.17, so $405 is too small, and the right payment is now between $405 and $410. One halfway try cut the search range in half. Section 4 turns this into a method.

Getting closer in one jump. Going from $400 to $410, ten dollars more per month, moved the final balance from 24.32 down to −5.98, a change of 24.32 + 5.98 = 30.30. To remove the 24.32 still owed at $400, the payment needs about this much more:

24.32 ÷ 30.30 × 10 = 8.03 dollars, so try 400 + 8.03 = $408.03

Three rows by hand take a few minutes. The house loan needs 360 rows, and every guess for the payment needs all 360 again. That job belongs to a spreadsheet.

The house loan follows the same rule with a different growth factor. Its monthly rate is 0.0728 ÷ 12, so each month the balance is multiplied by 1 + 0.0728 ÷ 12, about 1.0060667, and then the payment is subtracted. Columns C and E split that into two steps so the interest can be seen; =B7*(1+$B$3)-D7 in E7 would give the same new balance.

Fixed cell references

When a formula is copied down one row, the spreadsheet shifts every cell reference in it down one row. That is what makes =B7*$B$3 in row 7 turn into =B8*$B$3 in row 8: each month uses its own old balance.

The monthly rate should not shift. It lives in B3 and only in B3. A dollar sign in front of the column letter and the row number, as in $B$3, makes a fixed cell reference: it stays the same when the formula is copied. The payment $B$4 is fixed for the same reason.

Without the dollar signs, row 7 would hold =B7*B3 and row 8 would get =B8*B4. B4 is the payment, so the month-2 interest would be the balance times 4,000, a number in the billions. A column of absurd numbers after copying is often a sign of a missing $.

Building it

  1. Type the labels and numbers in A1:B4 and the formula =B2/12 in B3. Type the headings in row 6.
  2. Type the five formulas of row 7, then the five formulas of row 8.
  3. Select A8 through E366 and press Ctrl+D (Cmd+D on a Mac). This fills the row-8 formulas down. Dragging the small square at the corner of the selection down to row 366 does the same thing.
  4. Click a cell in row 366 and check that A366 shows 360.

4. Finding the right monthly payment

The right payment is somewhere above $4,500. Use the slider or the payment box to set a payment, and watch where the balance curve ends at month 360. The slider moves in whole dollars; the box and the buttons reach the cent.

The second graph splits each payment into two parts. The interest part is that month's interest. The rest of the payment reduces the balance, and is called the principal part, or principal. With a payment of $4,625.28, month 1 has an interest part of $4,101.07 and a principal part of 4,625.28 − 4,101.07 = $524.21.

Task 5 (about 5 minutes, payment box)

With the 30-year loan selected, find the payment to the cent that leaves the balance after 360 months closest to zero. Use the payment box and the −$1, +$1, −1¢ and +1¢ buttons. Write each payment you try and the balance it leaves in a two-column list, and count your tries.

Solution to Task 5

To the dollar: $4,625 leaves $354.67 owed, and $4,626 overpays by $934.90. To the cent: $4,625.27 leaves $6.48 owed, and $4,625.28 overpays by $6.41. The overpayment is the smaller of the two, so $4,625.28 is the answer.

The number of tries depends on the path. Starting from $4,625.00 and pressing +1¢ until the balance turns negative takes 28 presses.

A systematic search: bracketing

Pressing +1¢ until the balance changes sign is a search done by feel. The number of tries depends on where it starts, and in a spreadsheet every try means typing a new number in B4. Bracketing is a search with a fixed rule, and Task 3 already took one step of it.

  1. Start with two payments: a low one that leaves money owed and a high one that overpays. The right payment lies between them.
  2. Try the payment exactly halfway between low and high.
  3. If it leaves money owed, it becomes the new low. If it overpays, it becomes the new high.
  4. Repeat from step 2.

The right payment always stays between low and high, and each step cuts the gap between them in half. Starting from $4,500 (owes $161,550.11) and $5,000 (overpays), the first four steps are:

The total cost

The buyer makes 360 payments of $4,625.28, with the last one $6.41 smaller:

Total paid = 360 × 4,625.28 − 6.41 = $1,665,094.39
Total interest = 1,665,094.39 − 676,000 = $989,094.39

The total interest is also the sum of the interest column; in the spreadsheet, =SUM(C7:C366) gives 989,094.39 (with B4 set to 4625.28). The buyer repays about 2.46 times the amount borrowed. Interest is charged every month on whatever is still owed, and for most of 30 years a large amount is still owed.

6. A formula

Bracketing reaches the payment to the cent in 16 tries, and each try runs through 360 months of the rule. A lender quoting payments all day, or a buyer comparing many prices and rates, needs something faster.

Is there a formula that takes the loan amount, the monthly rate, and the number of months, and gives the payment in one calculation?

Here is where such a formula has to come from. Write f for the growth factor 1 + 0.0728 ÷ 12 (about 1.0060667) and P for the payment. The rule is new balance = f × old balance − P, so

after month 1: f × 676,000 − P
after month 2: f × (f × 676,000 − P) − P = f2 × 676,000 − f × P − P
after month 3: f3 × 676,000 − f2 × P − f × P − P

Each month adds one more payment term, and each earlier term picks up one more factor of f. After 360 months the payment is multiplied by the sum 1 + f + f2 + … + f359: 360 numbers, each f times the one before. Adding up numbers like these in one step is the subject of lecture 2. Whatever formula comes out must give $4,625.28 for this loan.

Task 6 (after class)

The same October 1, 2026 survey reports an average rate of 6.60% for a 15-year fixed-rate mortgage.[2] For the same $676,000 loan repaid over 15 years (180 payments):

  1. Find the monthly payment to the nearest dollar. Use the slider with the 15-year loan selected, or change B2 to 0.066 in the spreadsheet and read cell E186, the balance after month 180.
  2. Estimate the total interest, using total paid = 180 × payment.
  3. Compare with the 30-year loan: how much more per month, and how much less interest in total?
Solution to Task 6

(a) $5,900 leaves $7,933.40 owed and $6,000 overpays by $22,683.77. Narrowing down gives $5,926 to the nearest dollar, and $5,925.91 to the cent. With $5,925.91, E186 shows 0.49, so the last payment is 49 cents larger.

(b) Total paid = 180 × 5,925.91 + 0.49 = $1,066,664.29. Total interest = 1,066,664.29 − 676,000 = $390,664.29. Using $5,926 and no cent adjustment gives about $390,680, which is close enough for a comparison.

(c) The 15-year payment is 5,925.91 − 4,625.28 = $1,300.63 more per month. The total interest is 989,094.39 − 390,664.29 = $598,430.10 less. Two things cause the savings: the rate is lower, and the balance shrinks faster, so there is less to charge interest on. In month 1 of the 15-year loan, interest is 676,000 × 0.066 ÷ 12 = $3,718.00, which is 62.7% of the payment, and the balance falls below half the loan after month 112.
