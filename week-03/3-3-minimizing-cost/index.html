2. Cost as a linear function and level lines

The cost of a breakfast with e eggs and m cups of milk is

cost = 0.19e + 0.24m  (dollars).

This is a linear function of the two amounts: each amount is multiplied by a fixed number, its price, and the results are added. For 1 egg and 3 cups, cost = 0.19 × 1 + 0.24 × 3 = $0.91, as in Task 1.

Level lines

Fix a cost, for example $1.00. All breakfasts that cost exactly $1.00 satisfy

0.19e + 0.24m = 1.00.

This is the equation of a line, as in week 2. Its intercepts are e = 1.00 ÷ 0.19 = 5.26 eggs with no milk, and m = 1.00 ÷ 0.24 = 4.17 cups with no eggs. Breakfast (b) from Task 1, (4, 1), is on it. A line on which the cost has one fixed value is called a level line of the cost. The level line for $0.80 has intercepts 4.21 and 3.33. The level line for $1.25 has intercepts 6.58 and 5.21: it is the budget line from lecture 3.1, since the budget requirement says the cost is at most $1.25.

Solving for m gives m = −(0.19 ÷ 0.24)e + (cost ÷ 0.24). The slope is −0.19 ÷ 0.24 = −0.79 for every cost, so all level lines are parallel. Only the intercept changes: a lower cost gives a line closer to the origin. The slope is a trade-off rate. Giving up 1 egg saves $0.19, which pays for 0.79 cup of milk.

To find the cheapest feasible breakfast, start with a level line that crosses the feasible region and slide it toward the origin, keeping it parallel. Each step lowers the cost. Stop at the last position where the line still touches the region. The point it touches there is the cheapest feasible breakfast.

3. The corner-point method

The feasible region is a polygon: a shape bounded by straight edges on all sides. When a straight line slides across such a polygon, the last point it touches is a corner. The one exception is when the line is parallel to an edge. Then it leaves along that whole edge at once, and every point of the edge, including the two corners at its ends, has the same cost. In both cases some corner has the lowest cost. Our region is bounded (it does not go on forever), and for a bounded polygon this always works. An unbounded region needs a check of which way the cost goes. Without the Calorie and budget maximums, the region from lecture 3.2 would continue forever up and to the right. A line sliding that way would never leave it, so there would be no most expensive breakfast. The cheapest breakfast would still be at a corner, because a line sliding toward lower costs still leaves the region at a corner. For some other unbounded regions the cost can drop forever, and then there is no minimum at all.

Corner-point method. When the cost is a linear function and the feasible region is a bounded polygon:

  1. Find every corner of the feasible region. Each corner solves a pair of boundary equations (lecture 3.2, Section 5).
  2. Compute the cost at each corner.
  3. The corner with the smallest cost is the cheapest feasible breakfast. If two corners tie for the smallest cost, every point on the edge joining them has that same smallest cost.

Lecture 3.2 already found the corners. With four decimals they are 1: (4.7619, 0), 2: (6.5789, 0), 3: (1.4407, 4.0678), 4: (0, 4.9180) and 5: (0, 3.7258). Corner 3 was the one that needed two equations solved together, in Task 4 of lecture 3.2.

Corners 2 and 3 both lie on the budget line 0.19e + 0.24m = 1.25, which is the level line for $1.25. Every breakfast on the edge between them costs exactly $1.25, the most any feasible breakfast can cost.

Why the cheapest breakfast is all milk

Both foods are sources of protein, and the requirement is a minimum amount of protein. A dollar buys 6.3 ÷ 0.19 = 33.2 g of protein as eggs and 8.052 ÷ 0.24 = 33.55 g as milk, as lecture 3.2 found. Milk is the slightly cheaper source, so the cheapest breakfast takes all of its protein from milk, and only as much as the requirement asks: exactly 30 g, which is 30 ÷ 8.052 = 3.7258 cups. That breakfast has 455 Calories and costs $0.89, so neither the Calorie limit nor the budget affects the answer. Lecture 3.2 (Section 6) found the same $0.894 as the cheapest way to get 30 g of protein; the corner-point method shows that no mix of eggs and milk does better.

A budget of at least $0.8942 keeps corner 5 feasible, and it stays the cheapest. With a budget below $0.8942, no breakfast meets every requirement, and the feasible region is empty, as in lecture 3.2.

To pour it, round the milk in the safe direction from lecture 3.1. A minimum is rounded up: 3.73 cups gives 8.052 × 3.73 = 30.03 g of protein, 455 Calories and $0.8952. Rounding down to 3.72 cups gives 29.95 g, which fails the protein requirement. In a kitchen measuring cup, 3¾ cups is the nearest mark; it gives 30.2 g, 457.5 Calories and $0.90.

What if the breakfast must include eggs

Almost 4 cups of milk and nothing else is a breakfast few people want. Suppose a person adds a requirement of their own: at least 2 eggs, e ≥ 2. This requirement is made up for this example. Its boundary is the vertical line e = 2, and the side to keep is the right side. Lecture 3.1 (Section 4) already worked with 2 eggs and found that the milk must be from 2.17 to 3.625 cups.

4. When the price of eggs changes

Egg prices have moved a lot. A dozen cost $6.227 in March 2025 and $2.272 in August 2026 [3]. Let p be the price of one egg, with milk at $0.24 a cup. The cost is pe + 0.24m, and the level lines have slope −p ÷ 0.24. A higher egg price makes them steeper.

The egg price is also in the budget requirement, pe + 0.24m ≤ 1.25, so the budget line turns too, and corners 2 and 3 move. Corners 1, 4 and 5 stay where they are, because the protein and Calorie lines and the axes do not involve prices. Only corners 1 and 5 can ever be cheapest. Corners 2 and 3 lie on the budget line and always cost exactly $1.25. Corner 4 has more milk than corner 5 and no eggs either, so it costs more. When eggs cost more than $1.25 ÷ 4.7619 = $0.2625 (a dozen above $3.15), 4.76 eggs alone are over budget, so corner 1 leaves the region. A new corner, labeled 6 on the graph, appears where the protein line meets the budget line, and it also costs $1.25.

On the graph below, the egg-price slider starts at $2.28 a dozen, which is $0.19 an egg, as in Table 1. Moving it turns both the dashed cost line and the budget line.

Solution to Task 5

(1) At $3.59 a dozen one egg costs $0.2992. The cheapest corner is still 5, all milk, at $0.8942; the milk corners do not depend on the egg price. Corner 1 has left the region: 4.76 eggs would cost 4.7619 × 0.2992 = $1.42, over the budget. Corner 6 is at (3.19, 1.23) and costs $1.25.

(2) At $2.26 a dozen ($0.1883 an egg), corner 1 costs $0.8968 and corner 5 $0.8942, so corner 5 is cheapest. At $2.25 ($0.1875 an egg), corner 1 costs $0.8929, so corner 1 is cheapest. The switch is between $2.25 and $2.26.

Algebra gives the exact price. The two corners cost the same when

4.7619p = 0.8942,   so p = 0.8942 ÷ 4.7619 = $0.1878 per egg,

or 12 × 0.1878 = $2.2534 a dozen. At this price the level lines have slope −0.1878 ÷ 0.24 = −0.782, the same as the protein line. The cost line is parallel to the edge from corner 1 to corner 5, and every breakfast on that edge costs $0.8942. It is also the price at which both foods cost the same per gram of protein: 0.1878 ÷ 6.3 = 0.24 ÷ 8.052 = $0.0298.

(3) One egg: 2.141 ÷ 12 = $0.1784, which is 0.1784 ÷ 6.3 = $0.0283 per gram of protein. One cup: 3.750 ÷ 16 = $0.2344, which is 0.2344 ÷ 8.052 = $0.0291 per gram. Eggs were the cheaper source, so the cheapest breakfast was corner 1, all eggs: 4.76 eggs for 4.7619 × 0.1784 = $0.8496, against 3.7258 × 0.2344 = $0.8732 for corner 5. In whole eggs it is closer: 5 eggs cost $0.892, more than the 3.73 cups of milk at $0.874.

The answer switched to eggs in June 2026 and back to milk in August 2026. In those months egg prices fell while milk rose, and the two foods are so close in protein per dollar that a few cents a dozen decide it. The cost of the cheapest breakfast hardly changes at a switch: at the switch price the two corners cost the same. This table uses unrounded prices; rounding each price up to the cent, as in Table 1, can move a close month such as July to the other side.

A price change can only move the answer from one corner to another. The answer jumps from corner 5 to corner 1 when the level lines become less steep than the protein edge, and back when they become steeper. Between switches the cheapest breakfast stays at the same corner, even though its cost changes.

5. Checking the answer with Solver

Excel has an add-in called Solver that finds the smallest (or largest) value of a formula while other formulas meet requirements [6]. Google Sheets has no built-in Solver; a free Solver add-on from Frontline Systems has been offered for it [8]. With the Simplex LP method, the method for linear problems [6], Solver should return corner 5. Solver may have to be turned on or installed first, so do that before class if you can.

Running Solver in Excel

  1. On the Data tab, in the Analysis group, click Solver [6]. If there is no Solver command, the Solver add-in has to be activated first [6].
  2. In Set Objective, enter E4, the total cost. Click Min [6].
  3. In By Changing Variable Cells, enter B2:B3 [6].
  4. Click Add for each requirement [6], one at a time: C4 >= C5 (protein), D4 <= D5 (Calories), E4 <= E5 (budget), and B2:B3 >= 0 (no negative amounts).
  5. In Select a Solving Method, choose Simplex LP [6].
  6. Click Solve, then Keep Solver Solution [6].

Solver shows cell addresses with $ signs, such as $E$4. Leave them as Solver writes them; these fixed references are explained in lecture 4.1.

Running Solver in Google Sheets

  1. Click Extensions > Add-ons > Get add-ons [7].
  2. Search for "Frontline Systems" and choose the Solver add-on [8]. Click Install, then Continue, and click Allow when it asks for access [7].
  3. Open the add-on. Frontline's 2018 instructions say Add-ons > Solver > Start [8]; Google now lists add-ons under the Extensions menu [7], so look for Solver there.
  4. Enter the same things as in Excel: objective E4, set to minimize; variable cells B2:B3; the constraints C4 >= C5, D4 <= D5, E4 <= E5 and B2:B3 >= 0; a linear (simplex) method if the panel offers a choice.
  5. Click Solve.

The add-on's current panel could not be checked for this lecture, so its labels may differ from these steps.

Solver is not needed, because the corner-point method already guarantees that the cheapest breakfast is one of the corners.
