Systems of inequalities

1. The breakfast requirements from lecture 3.1

The breakfast is the one from lecture 3.1: large eggs and 2% milk. Table 1 repeats the values from lecture 3.1. One egg has 50 × 12.6 ÷ 100 = 6.3 g of protein and 71.5 Calories, rounded up to 72 [1]. One cup of 2% milk has 244 × 3.30 ÷ 100 = 8.052 g of protein and 122 Calories [2]. In August 2026 a dozen large eggs cost $2.272 on average in U.S. cities [3], so one egg costs $0.1893, rounded up to $0.19. A gallon of reduced-fat milk cost $3.715 [4, 5], so one cup costs $3.715 ÷ 16 = $0.2322, rounded up to $0.24.

The three limits are a choice made for this example. They are not official recommendations. The egg's Calories and both prices are rounded up, so a breakfast that meets the requirements with Table 1 also meets them with the unrounded values. Let e be the number of eggs and m the number of cups of milk. A breakfast is a point (e, m); for example, (2, 3) means 2 eggs and 3 cups of milk. Lecture 3.1 wrote the three requirements as inequalities:

Each check settles the question for one breakfast. There are infinitely many breakfasts, such as 4.5 eggs with 1.25 cups of milk, and checking them one at a time never ends. A graph can show every acceptable breakfast at once.

2. Systems of inequalities and non-negative amounts

The three inequalities accept some breakfasts that cannot exist. For example, (8, −2) passes all three checks: protein 50.4 − 16.104 = 34.296 g, Calories 576 − 244 = 332, cost 1.52 − 0.48 = $1.04. Nobody can drink −2 cups of milk. Two more inequalities rule this out:

e ≥ 0,   m ≥ 0no negative amounts

These are called non-negativity conditions.

A system of inequalities is a list of inequalities that must all be true at the same time. A solution of the system is a pair (e, m) that makes every one of them true. The breakfast system is:

6.3e + 8.052m ≥ 30protein
72e + 122m ≤ 600Calories
0.19e + 0.24m ≤ 1.25cost
e ≥ 0,   m ≥ 0no negative amounts

From Task 1, (2, 3) is a solution of this system. (6, 0.5) is not, because it fails the cost inequality.

3. Graphing the system: the feasible region

In lecture 3.1 the solutions of one inequality in e and m filled a half-plane: a boundary line and all the points on one side of it. With eggs across and cups of milk up, two of the half-planes are already known from lecture 3.1:

  • protein: the line 6.3e + 8.052m = 30, through (4.76, 0) and (0, 3.73), and the side above it, away from (0, 0). In slope-intercept form the line is m = −0.782e + 3.73.
  • cost: the line 0.19e + 0.24m = 1.25, through (6.58, 0) and (0, 5.21), and the side below it, the side with (0, 0).

Graphing one inequality (from lecture 3.1)

  1. Replace the inequality symbol by = to get the boundary line. Draw it through its two intercepts. Draw it solid for ≥ or ≤.
  2. Pick a test point that is not on the line. (0, 0) is easiest when the line misses it. Put it into the inequality.
  3. If the result is true, shade the side with the test point. If it is false, shade the other side.

The non-negativity conditions need no test point. The boundary of e ≥ 0 is the line e = 0, the vertical axis, and e ≥ 0 keeps the side to its right. The boundary of m ≥ 0 is the horizontal axis, and m ≥ 0 keeps the side above it.

The feasible region of a system is the set of all its solutions: the points that satisfy every inequality at once. On a graph it is the overlap of all the half-planes. Here each point in the feasible region is a breakfast that meets every requirement. "Feasible" means "possible": these are the breakfasts you are allowed to choose.

Task 2 (about 5 minutes, calculator and paper)

Work with the Calorie inequality 72e + 122m ≤ 600.

  1. Find both intercepts of its boundary line, to two decimals.
  2. Write the boundary line in slope-intercept form, m = (slope)e + (intercept), as lecture 3.1 did for protein.
  3. Test (0, 0) and say which side of the line to keep.
  4. On one set of axes, with eggs from 0 to 9 across and cups of milk from 0 to 6 up, draw all three boundary lines. Shade the region that is on the correct side of all three and has e ≥ 0 and m ≥ 0.

Produce the two intercepts, the equation, the side to keep, and your sketch.

Solution to Task 2
  1. No milk: 72e = 600, so e = 600 ÷ 72 = 8.33; the point (8.33, 0). No eggs: 122m = 600, so m = 600 ÷ 122 = 4.92; the point (0, 4.92).
  2. Subtract 72e from both sides: 122m = −72e + 600. Divide by 122: m = −0.590e + 4.92. With Calories fixed at 600, each extra egg replaces 0.590 cups of milk.
  3. 72 × 0 + 122 × 0 = 0, and 0 ≤ 600 is true. Keep the side with (0, 0), below the line.
  4. Compare your sketch with Figure 1 below.

An acceptable breakfast is on or above the protein line, on or below the Calorie line and on or below the cost line, with neither amount negative. The feasible region is the band between the protein line and the Calorie line, cut off on the right by the cost line.

protein = 30 gCalories = 600cost = $1.25

B at (2, 3) is inside the region. A at (2, 2) is below the protein line. D at (2, 4) is above both the Calorie line and the cost line. C at (6, 0.5) is just above the cost line, 1 cent over budget. The points with 2 eggs, from 2.17 up to 3.625 cups, are the part of the vertical line e = 2 inside the region, the interval found in lecture 3.1.

The system contains only these three requirements and non-negativity, so its region includes breakfasts that few people would eat, such as almost 5 cups of milk with no eggs, or six and a half eggs with no milk. A person can add a requirement of their own, such as at most 2 cups of milk (m ≤ 2). Each new inequality adds one boundary line and can cut off part of the region. This lecture and lecture 3.3 keep the three requirements of Table 1.

5. Corner points of the feasible region

A corner point of the feasible region is a point on its edge where two boundary lines meet. The region in Figure 1 has five corners, and its edges are the straight segments joining them in order. Lecture 3.3 uses the corners to compare breakfasts.

Four corners are on the axes, so they are intercepts already found: (4.76, 0) on the protein line and (6.58, 0) on the cost line, along the egg axis; (0, 4.92) on the Calorie line and (0, 3.73) on the protein line, along the milk axis. The fifth corner is where the Calorie line meets the cost line, on neither axis. Finding it means solving two equations in two unknowns, as in week 2.

Finding the corner points of a feasible region

  1. Draw the region and see which two boundary lines meet at each corner.
  2. A corner on an axis is an intercept. For a corner off the axes, solve the equations of its two lines together.
  3. Check each point against every inequality in the system.

Task 4 (about 6 minutes, calculator)

Find the point where the Calorie line 72e + 122m = 600 meets the cost line 0.19e + 0.24m = 1.25.

  1. Multiply the cost equation by 100, so it is in cents: 19e + 24m = 125. Solve it for e, then substitute into the Calorie equation and solve for m. Keep four decimals until the end.
  2. Check that the point meets the protein requirement and has e ≥ 0 and m ≥ 0.

Produce the point to two decimals and one sentence saying what breakfast it is.

Solution to Task 4

From 19e + 24m = 125: 19e = 125 − 24m, so e = (125 − 24m) ÷ 19.

Substitute into the Calorie equation:

72 × (125 − 24m) ÷ 19 + 122m = 600.

Multiply both sides by 19 to clear the division. On the left, 122m × 19 = 2318m; on the right, 600 × 19 = 11400.

72 × (125 − 24m) + 2318m = 11400 9000 − 1728m + 2318m = 11400 590m = 2400 m = 2400 ÷ 590 = 4.0678

Then e = (125 − 24 × 4.0678) ÷ 19 = (125 − 97.6272) ÷ 19 = 27.3728 ÷ 19 = 1.4407. The lines meet at (1.44, 4.07).

Check: protein 6.3 × 1.4407 + 8.052 × 4.0678 = 9.08 + 32.75 = 41.83 g, which is at least 30. Both amounts are positive. So (1.44, 4.07) is a corner. It is a breakfast of about one and a half eggs and just over 4 cups of milk that uses exactly 600 Calories and exactly $1.25.

Crossings that are not corners

Five lines (protein, Calories, cost and the two axes) give 10 pairs, and only the five pairs in Table 2 meet at corners. The picture shows which pairs to solve, so the other five need no algebra. Two of them are worth a look.

The Calorie line meets the egg axis at (8.33, 0). That breakfast costs 0.19 × 8.33 = $1.58, so it breaks the cost inequality and is not a corner.

The protein line and the cost line have slopes −0.782 and −0.792, almost the same. In week 2, parallel lines never cross. These two are nearly parallel, so they cross very far away, at about (160, −122), a breakfast with negative milk. They are nearly parallel because an egg and a cup of milk give almost the same protein per dollar: 6.3 ÷ 0.19 = 33.2 g per dollar for eggs and 8.052 ÷ 0.24 = 33.6 g per dollar for milk.

6. Empty feasible regions

Suppose the Calorie limit drops from 600 to 300, a stricter limit made up for this example. The protein and cost requirements stay the same.

Task 5 (about 4 minutes, calculator and interactive graph)

Use the requirements at least 30 g of protein, at most 300 Calories and at most $1.25.

  1. Find both intercepts of the new boundary line 72e + 122m = 300, to two decimals.
  2. Compare them with the intercepts of the protein line, (4.76, 0) and (0, 3.73). Decide whether any breakfast with e ≥ 0 and m ≥ 0 is both on or above the protein line and on or below the new Calorie line.
  3. Check your answer by moving the Calorie slider in the interactive graph to 300.

Produce the two intercepts and a yes or no answer with one sentence of reason.

Solution to Task 5
  1. No milk: 300 ÷ 72 = 4.17, so (4.17, 0). No eggs: 300 ÷ 122 = 2.46, so (0, 2.46).
  2. No. On both axes the new Calorie line is closer to the origin than the protein line (4.17 < 4.76 and 2.46 < 3.73). Both are straight lines, so between the axes the whole Calorie line lies below the protein line. A breakfast on or below the Calorie line is then below the protein line, so it has less than 30 g of protein.
  3. With the slider at 300, nothing is shaded, and the panel says the feasible region is empty (Figure 3).

protein = 30 gCalories = 300cost = $1.25

When the requirements conflict, the system has no solutions. Its feasible region is empty: no point is on the correct side of every boundary line at once.

Protein per Calorie explains the conflict. An egg gives 6.3 ÷ 72 = 0.0875 g of protein per Calorie, and a cup of milk gives 8.052 ÷ 122 = 0.066 g per Calorie. Eggs give more protein per Calorie, so the most protein that 300 Calories can buy comes from eggs alone: 300 ÷ 72 = 4.17 eggs, which give 6.3 × 300 ÷ 72 = 26.25 g. That is less than 30 g, and any mix with milk gives even less.

An empty feasible region means at least one requirement has to change. With these two foods, 30 g of protein takes at least 30 ÷ 6.3 = 4.76 eggs, which have 4.762 × 72 = 342.9 Calories. Any Calorie limit below 342.9 leaves no acceptable breakfast. On the interactive graph, set the Calorie slider to 350 and switch on "Show corner points": a small triangle remains next to (4.76, 0), with corners (4.76, 0), (4.86, 0) and (4.46, 0.24). At 340 the panel reports an empty region.

The budget can conflict with protein in the same way. The cheapest 30 g of protein is milk alone: 3.726 cups × $0.24 = $0.894 (eggs alone cost 4.762 × $0.19 = $0.905). A budget below $0.894 leaves no acceptable breakfast; with the Calorie limit back at 600, try the cost slider at $0.85.
