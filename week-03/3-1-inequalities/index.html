Inequalities

1. Breakfast requirements: eggs and milk

In week 2 the target was exact: a breakfast with 30 g of protein. Real requirements are usually minimums and maximums. In this module the breakfast is eggs and 2% milk, two of the foods from lecture 2.1, with the same nutrition values. It has three requirements:

  • at least 30 g of protein,
  • at most 600 Calories,
  • at most $1.25 for the food.

These three limits are a choice made for this example. They are not official recommendations. Calories here are food Calories, which the USDA database lists as kcal.

Nutrition and prices

One large egg weighs 50 g, and whole egg has 12.6 g of protein and 143 Calories per 100 g [1]. So one egg has 50 × 12.6 ÷ 100 = 6.3 g of protein and 50 × 143 ÷ 100 = 71.5 Calories, rounded up to 72.

One cup of 2% milk weighs 244 g, and 2% milk has 3.30 g of protein and 50 Calories per 100 g [2]. So one cup has 244 × 3.30 ÷ 100 = 8.052 g of protein and 244 × 50 ÷ 100 = 122 Calories. These are the values in Table 1 of lecture 2.1.

The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) publishes the average price that stores in U.S. cities charge for common foods. In August 2026, the latest month published, a dozen grade A large eggs cost $2.272 [3], and a gallon of low-fat, reduced-fat or skim milk cost $3.715 [4]. BLS does not price 2% milk on its own; it is one of the reduced-fat milks in this average [5]. Per egg, the price is $2.272 ÷ 12 = $0.1893. A gallon is 16 cups, so per cup of milk it is $3.715 × (1 gallon ÷ 16 cups) = $0.2322.

With a minimum and two maximums, many breakfasts are good enough. The question is how to describe all of them at once, instead of checking breakfasts one at a time.

2. Inequality symbols

An inequality is a statement that one amount is larger or smaller than another. It uses one of four symbols.

SymbolRead asEveryday wordsExample
≥is greater than or equal toat least, no less than, a minimum of33.252 ≥ 30 is true; 30 ≥ 30 is true
≤is less than or equal toat most, no more than, a maximum of, up to410 ≤ 600 is true
>is greater thanmore than, over30 > 30 is false
<is less thanless than, under, below0.81 < 1.25 is true

The narrow end of the symbol points to the smaller amount. The symbols with a bar underneath, ≥ and ≤, allow the two sides to be equal. Requirements that say "at least" or "at most" use these.

Let e be the number of eggs and m the number of cups of milk. With the values in Table 1, the three requirements become three inequalities:

protein: 6.3e + 8.052m ≥ 30
Calories: 72e + 122m ≤ 600
cost: 0.19e + 0.24m ≤ 1.25

A solution of an inequality is a value, or a pair of values, that makes it true. Breakfast C, with e = 4 and m = 1, is a solution of all three. Breakfast A, with e = 3 and m = 1, is a solution of the Calorie and cost inequalities, and it is not a solution of the protein inequality, because 26.952 ≥ 30 is false.

4. Solving a linear inequality in one unknown

Start with one unknown. Fix the eggs at 2 and ask which amounts of milk work. With e = 2, the eggs give 2 × 6.3 = 12.6 g of protein, so the protein requirement becomes

12.6 + 8.052m ≥ 30

To solve an inequality means to find all of its solutions. Inequalities are solved with the same moves as equations in week 2: do the same thing to both sides.

12.6 + 8.052m ≥ 30
8.052m ≥ 17.4 subtract 12.6 from both sides
m ≥ 2.1609… divide both sides by 8.052

When an answer is rounded, a minimum is rounded up and a maximum is rounded down. This is the safe direction: the rounded end still meets the requirement. Here 2.1609 rounds up to 2.17, because 2.16 cups gives 12.6 + 8.052 × 2.16 = 29.992 g, which falls short of 30. With 2.17 cups the protein is 30.073 g. So with 2 eggs, 2.17 cups of milk or more is enough.

A hundredth of a cup is about half a teaspoon, much finer than anyone measures milk. The answers keep two decimal places so that each step can be checked.

The cost requirement with 2 eggs is solved with the same steps. The eggs cost 2 × 0.19 = $0.38, so 0.38 + 0.24m ≤ 1.25 gives 0.24m ≤ 0.87 and m ≤ 3.625, with no rounding needed.

Multiplying or dividing by a negative number

The Calorie requirement with 2 eggs is 144 + 122m ≤ 600. Another way to state it is "the Calories left over from 600 are zero or more." After 2 eggs and m cups of milk, the Calories left are 600 − 144 − 122m, which is 456 − 122m.

456 − 122m ≥ 0
−122m ≥ −456 subtract 456 from both sides
m ≤ 3.737… divide both sides by −122, and reverse the symbol

Rounded down, m ≤ 3.73 (3.74 cups would give 144 + 122 × 3.74 = 600.28 Calories). Solving 144 + 122m ≤ 600 directly gives the same answer: 122m ≤ 456, so m ≤ 3.737…. Keeping the symbol as ≥ would give m ≥ 3.74, and that is wrong. Test m = 4: the Calories left are 456 − 122 × 4 = 456 − 488 = −32, which is not zero or more. So 4 cups is not a solution, and m ≥ 3.74 would include it.

The reason is visible with small numbers. 2 is less than 5: 2 < 5. Multiply both by −1 to get −2 and −5. On a number line −2 is to the right of −5, so −2 > −5. Multiplying by a negative number flips the number line around 0, and the order of every pair of numbers reverses. Dividing by a negative number does the same, since dividing by −122 is multiplying by −1/122.

The rules for solving a linear inequality

  • Adding or subtracting the same number on both sides keeps the symbol.
  • Multiplying or dividing both sides by a positive number keeps the symbol.
  • Multiplying or dividing both sides by a negative number reverses the symbol: ≥ becomes ≤, < becomes >, and so on.

Example without food: solve 5 − 2x > 11.

5 − 2x > 11
−2x > 6 subtract 5 from both sides
x < −3 divide both sides by −2, and reverse the symbol

Check with a number on each side of −3. For x = −4: 5 − 2 × (−4) = 5 + 8 = 13, and 13 > 11 is true. For x = 0: 5 − 0 = 5, and 5 > 11 is false. The solutions are the numbers less than −3.

5. Number lines and interval notation

The solutions of an inequality in one unknown are usually a whole stretch of numbers. A number line shows them. Shade the solutions, and mark each endpoint with a dot: a filled dot when the endpoint is a solution (≥ or ≤) and an open dot when it is not (> or <). An arrow means the shading continues forever.

Interval notation writes the same stretch with its two ends. A square bracket [ or ] means the endpoint is included, and a round bracket ( or ) means it is not. The symbol ∞ (infinity) means "no end in that direction"; it always gets a round bracket, because no number reaches it.

All requirements together, for 2 eggs

Milk cannot be negative, so m ≥ 0 as well. With 2 eggs, Section 4 found:

  • protein: m ≥ 2.17, the interval [2.17, ∞)
  • Calories: 0 ≤ m ≤ 3.73, the interval [0, 3.73]
  • cost: 0 ≤ m ≤ 3.625, the interval [0, 3.625]

A breakfast must meet all three, so the good amounts of milk are the numbers shaded on all three lines. That overlap is 2.17 ≤ m ≤ 3.625, the interval [2.17, 3.625]. With 2 eggs, every amount of milk from 2.17 to 3.625 cups meets all three requirements. Amounts below 2.1609… cups or above 3.625 cups fail at least one. The amounts from 2.1609… up to 2.17 also work; rounding the lower end up left them out. The spreadsheet in Task 3 agrees: with 2 eggs, the rows with 2.5, 3 and 3.5 cups passed.

The lower end was rounded up and the upper end is exact, so every number in [2.17, 3.625] is a solution of the three inequalities. The values in Table 1 are exact or rounded in the safe direction, so these breakfasts also meet the requirements at the unrounded USDA and BLS values.

6. Linear inequalities in two unknowns: half-planes

Sections 4 and 5 fixed the eggs. To describe every good breakfast, both amounts must vary. As in week 2, a breakfast is a point (e, m), with eggs across and cups of milk up.

Simplification: in this section the number of eggs can be any number, such as 2.5, the same way the milk can be any number of cups. Think of 2.5 eggs as half of a five-egg scramble.

The boundary line

The breakfasts with exactly 30 g of protein satisfy the equation 6.3e + 8.052m = 30. Week 2 showed that its graph is a line. Its intercepts are

  • no milk: 6.3e = 30, so e = 4.76; the point (4.76, 0),
  • no eggs: 8.052m = 30, so m = 3.73; the point (0, 3.73).

In slope-intercept form, m = −0.782e + 3.73: each extra egg replaces 0.782 cups of milk while protein stays at 30 g.

This line splits the graph into two sides, called half-planes. Start at a point on the line and move straight up. The eggs stay the same and the milk increases, so the protein goes above 30 g. Every point above the line has more than 30 g, and every point below it has less. The solutions of 6.3e + 8.052m ≥ 30 are the line together with the half-plane above it.

Solving for m gives the same answer with algebra. Subtract 6.3e and divide by 8.052, a positive number, so the symbol stays: m ≥ −0.782e + 3.73. The milk must be at or above the line's height.

Amounts cannot be negative, so the graph has only the part with e ≥ 0 and m ≥ 0. The half-plane itself continues past the axes.

Choosing the side with a test point

A quicker way to pick the side works for every linear inequality in two unknowns.

  1. Draw the boundary line. Draw it solid if the symbol is ≥ or ≤, because points on the line are solutions. Draw it dashed for > or <. For example, "the food costs under $1.25" is 0.19e + 0.24m < 1.25, and its line is dashed, because a breakfast that costs exactly $1.25 is not under $1.25.
  2. Pick a test point that is not on the line. (0, 0) is easiest when the line misses it.
  3. Substitute the test point into the inequality. If the result is true, shade the side containing the test point. If it is false, shade the other side.

For protein, the test point (0, 0) gives 6.3 × 0 + 8.052 × 0 = 0, and 0 ≥ 30 is false. A breakfast of nothing does not have 30 g of protein. So the shaded side is the one away from (0, 0), above the line. A second check: breakfast C, (4, 1), has 33.252 g and lies in the shaded half-plane.

Drag the dot, tap the graph, or type the amounts. The dot moves in steps of 0.1. The shaded half-plane and the solid line are the solutions of the chosen requirement. Task 6 adds the cost requirement.
