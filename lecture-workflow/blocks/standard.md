The team's standard for lecture material

Who it is for. Most Math 111 students are not majoring in a STEM field, and many take the course because their major requires one mathematics class. Many dislike mathematics or doubt they can do it. They meet this material in lecture with the instructor present, and some will read it again later on their own. The instructor supplies the warmth and the reassurance in person. The material earns students' trust another way: by being clear, by being correct, and by being worth their time.

Start from a real situation. Each lecture opens with the situation in the brief, using real numbers: an actual loan amount and interest rate, the nutrition labels of actual foods, the drink options on an actual menu. Students should be able to start working on it with arithmetic, a calculator, or a spreadsheet before any new notation appears.

Let the mathematics earn its place. Push the situation until trial and error becomes tedious or stops working, and introduce the model or method at that point, as the tool that answers a question the students already have. Then go back to the situation and use the method on it.

Students work during lecture. Include tasks for students to do in class, with specific numbers, placed where trying them helps. Say plainly what they should produce. Give them time with a task before the material shows the solution.

Explain completely once students have tried. Lecture material explains: worked examples, the reason for each step, and the general method. A student who missed class should be able to follow it.

Be concrete and correct. Every number in the material is computed, and the computation can be checked. Facts about the world (rates, prices, nutrition values, statistics) come with their source. Simplifications are named as simplifications, briefly, at the point where they matter.

Plain language. Use short sentences and everyday words. Introduce a term or symbol when students need it, define it in words with an example, and then use it the same way every time. Assume only the arithmetic and algebra the course objectives say students will have.

Visuals and interaction. Use a figure, table, spreadsheet, or interactive element when it does real work: it lets students see a pattern, try values, or check an answer. Refer to it by what students do with it.

Length. Fit one lecture period, including time for the in-class tasks. The brief gives the period length.
