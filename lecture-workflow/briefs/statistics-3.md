Module: Statistics and regression (week 8)   Lecture: 3 of 3   Title: Misleading graphs and analyses

Situation: Students meet statistics in the news, in ads and in research summaries. The question students will care about: how can a graph or a statistic that is technically correct still mislead, and what questions should you ask before believing it?

Mathematics: Truncated or stretched axes and how they change the visual size of a difference; area and 3D graphics that exaggerate; correlation versus causation, with a confounding variable; small samples and how much a mean can move by chance (a quick simulation is welcome); cherry-picked time windows; base rates: a test that is 90% accurate can still be wrong most of the time it says yes, worked with a table of counts for 10,000 people (probability from week 7).

Learning objectives addressed: interpreting analyses and recognizing misleading ones; reading graphs; probability; percentages.

Students already know: Week 1 (lectures 1.1 to 1.3): rates written as fractions and unit conversion by multiplying by a fraction equal to 1; order of operations on a calculator; estimating the size of an answer; converting between fractions, decimals and percentages; concentrations and dilution; Spreadsheet skills so far (lecture 1.3): columns with a header row, formulas that refer to other cells, copying a formula down with relative references. Fixed cell references ($A$1) are not taught until lecture 4.1; do not use them. Week 2 (lectures 2.1 to 2.3): linear equations from nutrition labels; solving equations in one unknown; coordinate pairs; graphing lines; intercepts; slope from two points as a trade-off rate; y = mx + b; solving two equations in two unknowns by graphing and substitution; parallel and identical lines. Week 3: inequalities, feasible regions, corner points, minimizing a linear cost. Week 4 (Buying a house): percentages as multipliers; a balance rule in a spreadsheet with fixed cell references; summation notation; geometric sums; the mortgage payment formula; compound interest and exponential growth; linear versus exponential growth. Week 5: scale drawings, area, the Pythagorean theorem, square roots, volume, surface area, scaling. Week 6: the multiplication principle, sets, subsets, Venn diagrams, permutations and combinations. Week 7: probability by counting, independence, simulation in a spreadsheet, expected value, returns as multipliers. Lecture 8.1: summary statistics. Lecture 8.2: scatterplots, correlation, regression lines, prediction and extrapolation.

Data and sources (the writer must find, verify and cite each; save the source text in draft/src/):
- Real examples of misleading graphs or claims, each with a citation to where it appeared (for example a documented case discussed by a statistics source); recreate graphs from the underlying numbers rather than copying images.
- Real sensitivity, specificity and prevalence for one screening test from a medical source (for example the National Cancer Institute or USPSTF), for the base-rate example.
- A real confounding example with a source, or a clearly labeled made-up one.

In-class tools: calculators (phones are fine), laptops with Excel or Google Sheets, projector, whiteboard.

Format and length: a single self-contained HTML page (inline CSS and JavaScript, no external dependencies, no MathJax; math in plain HTML) that the instructor projects and students read afterwards, and that works on a phone. Header line "Math 111 · Module: Statistics and regression (week 8) · Lecture 3 of 3", then the lecture title. Numbered sections with plain titles that name their content. In-class tasks are boxed and numbered with time and tool in the label, for example "Task 2 (about 4 minutes, calculator)", each saying exactly what to produce, with its solution in a collapsed box right after it; task times add up to about half the period. A Sources list at the end, cited in the text with bracketed numbers, each source with its date and the figure quoted. Include an interactive element (for example a slider or a draggable point that redraws a graph) only if students use it in a task; when the lecture has graphs, draw them with inline SVG or canvas. Where students use a spreadsheet, give the layout as a CSV download and a copy-to-clipboard layout, with formulas, and say they were not tested in Excel or Google Sheets unless you tested them. The lecture period is 50 minutes, including in-class tasks.

Note on network access: direct curl/wget to outside sites is blocked in this environment; use the WebFetch tool to read sources, and save the relevant source text in draft/src/. A number that stands for the world is real and sourced, or labeled as made up.
