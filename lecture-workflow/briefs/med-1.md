Module: Titrating medicine (week 1)   Lecture: 1 of 3   Title: Weight-based doses

Situation: Children's liquid acetaminophen (the drug in Children's Tylenol) is sold in the United States at one concentration, 160 mg per 5 mL. Doses for children are commonly figured from body weight, in milligrams per kilogram. A parent or nurse knows the child's weight in pounds. The question students will care about: how many milliliters should this child get, and how do you check that the answer is not off by a factor of ten?

Mathematics: Rates and ratios written as fractions (mg per kg, mg per mL, kg per lb); converting units by multiplying by a fraction equal to 1, so units cancel; order of operations when the whole calculation is entered on a calculator in one line; rounding to a measurable amount; estimating the size of an answer before computing it, and comparing a computed dose with a maximum daily amount. Start with a calculation students can do by hand with friendly numbers (labeled as made up), then the real one. This is the first lecture of the course, so it also rebuilds fraction arithmetic (multiplying and dividing fractions, simplifying) at the moment it is needed.

Learning objectives addressed: confident arithmetic with fractions and decimals; order of operations; calculator use; extracting the relevant information from a word problem.

Students already know: arithmetic with whole numbers and decimals from high school, though many are unsure of it. Nothing earlier in this course. Lecture 1.2 covers percentages and concentrations; lecture 1.3 builds a dosage table in a spreadsheet; do not teach those here.

Data and sources (the writer must find, verify and cite each; save the source text in draft/src/):
- The single U.S. concentration of children's liquid acetaminophen, 160 mg/5 mL: FDA or the product label on DailyMed.
- A commonly published weight-based dose of acetaminophen for children (for example 10 to 15 mg/kg per dose, at most 5 doses in 24 hours) and its source, such as the product label, the American Academy of Pediatrics (healthychildren.org), or a hospital formulary. Use only what a source says, quoted.
- The pound-kilogram conversion (1 lb = 0.45359237 kg exactly): NIST.
- If useful for a second example, one adult weight-based order from a real label, cited. Otherwise label made-up numbers as made up.

In-class tools: calculators (phones are fine), laptops available, projector, whiteboard.
