Module: Titrating medicine (week 1)   Lecture: 2 of 3   Title: Concentrations and percentages

Situation: An IV bag of "0.9% sodium chloride" (normal saline) is one of the most common things in a hospital. The question students will care about: what does 0.9% actually mean, how much salt is in a 1-liter bag, and how do you make a weaker solution from a stronger one? A second real case shows why the way a concentration is written matters: epinephrine used to be labeled with ratios such as 1:1,000 and 1:10,000, and that labeling was dropped in favor of mg per mL after dosing errors.

Mathematics: Percent as "per hundred"; converting between fractions, decimals and percentages in all directions; percent weight per volume (grams per 100 mL) for solutions; ratio strength (1:1,000 means 1 g in 1,000 mL) converted to mg/mL; diluting a solution by proportional reasoning (the amount of drug stays the same while the volume grows), leading to the rule concentration × volume before = concentration × volume after, introduced only after students have done a dilution by reasoning. Use friendly made-up numbers for a first dilution done by hand, labeled as made up.

Learning objectives addressed: conversion between fractions, decimals, and percentages; arithmetic with fractions; calculator use; extracting information from word problems.

Students already know: lecture 1.1, which covered rates written as fractions (mg per kg, mg per mL), converting units by multiplying by a fraction equal to 1, order of operations on a calculator, and estimating the size of an answer. Do not re-teach those beyond a one-line reminder. Lecture 1.3 builds a spreadsheet; do not teach spreadsheets here.
