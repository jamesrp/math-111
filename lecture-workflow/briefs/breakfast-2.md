Module: What's for breakfast? (week 2)   Lecture: 2 of 3   Title: Graphing lines and slope

Situation: Lecture 2.1 found many combinations of two breakfast foods that give 30 g of protein. Plotted as points (amount of food 1, amount of food 2), they fall on a line. The question students will care about: what does that line say, and how many grams of one food do you give up for each extra serving of the other?

Mathematics: Coordinate pairs and plotting points; the graph of a linear equation as the set of all its solutions; intercepts and what they mean (all of one food, none of the other); slope from two points as rise over run, read as a trade-off rate with units; negative slope; slope-intercept form y = mx + b written from the situation and from two points; checking a point by substituting it. An interactive graph where students drag a point along the line and read the amounts is welcome if a task uses it.

Learning objectives addressed: number lines and coordinate pairs; graphing lines; calculating slope from two points; negative numbers; reading graphs.

Students already know: Week 1 (lectures 1.1 to 1.3): rates written as fractions and unit conversion by multiplying by a fraction equal to 1; order of operations on a calculator; estimating the size of an answer; converting between fractions, decimals and percentages; concentrations and dilution; Spreadsheet skills so far (lecture 1.3): columns with a header row, formulas that refer to other cells, copying a formula down with relative references. Fixed cell references ($A$1) are not taught until lecture 4.1; do not use them. Lecture 2.1: reading nutrition labels as rates; writing and solving linear equations in one unknown; tabulating combinations of two foods that meet a protein target (all of them hit 30 g).

Data and sources (the writer must find, verify and cite each; save the source text in draft/src/):
- Reuse the same foods and nutrition values as lecture 2.1 (rolled oats, eggs, Greek yogurt, milk or peanut butter, from USDA FoodData Central or product labels); cite them again.

In-class tools: calculators (phones are fine), laptops with Excel or Google Sheets, projector, whiteboard.

Format and length: a single self-contained HTML page (inline CSS and JavaScript, no external dependencies, no MathJax; math in plain HTML) that the instructor projects and students read afterwards, and that works on a phone. Header line "Math 111 · Module: What's for breakfast? (week 2) · Lecture 2 of 3", then the lecture title. Numbered sections with plain titles that name their content. In-class tasks are boxed and numbered with time and tool in the label, for example "Task 2 (about 4 minutes, calculator)", each saying exactly what to produce, with its solution in a collapsed box right after it; task times add up to about half the period. A Sources list at the end, cited in the text with bracketed numbers, each source with its date and the figure quoted. Include an interactive element (for example a slider or a draggable point that redraws a graph) only if students use it in a task; when the lecture has graphs, draw them with inline SVG or canvas. Where students use a spreadsheet, give the layout as a CSV download and a copy-to-clipboard layout, with formulas, and say they were not tested in Excel or Google Sheets unless you tested them. The lecture period is 50 minutes, including in-class tasks.
