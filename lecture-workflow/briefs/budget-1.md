Module: Breakfast on a budget (week 3)   Lecture: 1 of 3   Title: Inequalities

Situation: Real breakfasts have requirements that are minimums or maximums: at least 30 g of protein, at most 600 calories, at most a few dollars. The question students will care about: how do you describe every breakfast that is good enough, not just the ones that hit a target exactly?

Mathematics: Inequality symbols and reading them in words; solving a linear inequality in one unknown, including why multiplying or dividing by a negative number flips the sign (with a numeric check); number lines and interval notation; a linear inequality in two unknowns as a half-plane bounded by the line from week 2, with a test point to choose the side.

Learning objectives addressed: number lines and intervals; linear inequalities; basic algebra; graphing lines; word problems.

Students already know: Week 1 (lectures 1.1 to 1.3): rates written as fractions and unit conversion by multiplying by a fraction equal to 1; order of operations on a calculator; estimating the size of an answer; converting between fractions, decimals and percentages; concentrations and dilution; Spreadsheet skills so far (lecture 1.3): columns with a header row, formulas that refer to other cells, copying a formula down with relative references. Fixed cell references ($A$1) are not taught until lecture 4.1; do not use them. Week 2 (lectures 2.1 to 2.3): linear equations from nutrition labels; solving equations in one unknown; coordinate pairs; graphing lines; intercepts; slope from two points as a trade-off rate; y = mx + b; solving two equations in two unknowns by graphing and substitution; parallel and identical lines.

Data and sources (the writer must find, verify and cite each; save the source text in draft/src/):
- The same breakfast foods and nutrition values used in week 2 (USDA FoodData Central or labels), cited.
- Current prices for those foods: prefer the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics Average Price Data (for example eggs per dozen, milk per gallon), naming the series, area and month; for foods BLS does not track, a dated retailer price labeled as one store's price on that date.

In-class tools: calculators (phones are fine), laptops with Excel or Google Sheets, projector, whiteboard.

Format and length: a single self-contained HTML page (inline CSS and JavaScript, no external dependencies, no MathJax; math in plain HTML) that the instructor projects and students read afterwards, and that works on a phone. Header line "Math 111 · Module: Breakfast on a budget (week 3) · Lecture 1 of 3", then the lecture title. Numbered sections with plain titles that name their content. In-class tasks are boxed and numbered with time and tool in the label, for example "Task 2 (about 4 minutes, calculator)", each saying exactly what to produce, with its solution in a collapsed box right after it; task times add up to about half the period. A Sources list at the end, cited in the text with bracketed numbers, each source with its date and the figure quoted. Include an interactive element (for example a slider or a draggable point that redraws a graph) only if students use it in a task; when the lecture has graphs, draw them with inline SVG or canvas. Where students use a spreadsheet, give the layout as a CSV download and a copy-to-clipboard layout, with formulas, and say they were not tested in Excel or Google Sheets unless you tested them. The lecture period is 50 minutes, including in-class tasks.

Note on network access: direct curl/wget to outside sites is blocked in this environment; use the WebFetch tool to read sources, and save the relevant source text in draft/src/. A number that stands for the world is real and sourced, or labeled as made up.
