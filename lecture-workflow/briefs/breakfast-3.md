Module: What's for breakfast? (week 2)   Lecture: 3 of 3   Title: Two equations in two unknowns

Situation: Protein is not the only goal: the student also wants a calorie limit, or a second nutrient target, from the same two foods. The question students will care about: is there a breakfast that meets both targets exactly, how do you find it, and why is there sometimes no answer?

Mathematics: Two linear equations in two unknowns as two lines; the solution as the intersection point; solving by graphing and by substitution (elimination optional); checking the answer in both equations; parallel lines (no solution) and identical lines (infinitely many) from real food pairs where possible; a third food and a third target as a brief first look: three foods give more freedom, and some targets still cannot be reached (an introductory idea of span, in words only).

Learning objectives addressed: basic algebra; equations; graphing lines; slope; reading tables and graphs; word problems.

Students already know: Week 1 (lectures 1.1 to 1.3): rates written as fractions and unit conversion by multiplying by a fraction equal to 1; order of operations on a calculator; estimating the size of an answer; converting between fractions, decimals and percentages; concentrations and dilution; Spreadsheet skills so far (lecture 1.3): columns with a header row, formulas that refer to other cells, copying a formula down with relative references. Fixed cell references ($A$1) are not taught until lecture 4.1; do not use them. Lecture 2.1: linear equations from a nutrition target, solving for one unknown. Lecture 2.2: coordinate pairs, graphing a line, intercepts, slope as a trade-off rate, y = mx + b.

Data and sources (the writer must find, verify and cite each; save the source text in draft/src/):
- The same foods and nutrition values as lectures 2.1 and 2.2 (USDA FoodData Central or labels), plus calories or a second nutrient per serving; cite them.
- A sourced calorie or nutrient reference only if you use one; otherwise present the targets as the student's own choice.

In-class tools: calculators (phones are fine), laptops with Excel or Google Sheets, projector, whiteboard.

Format and length: a single self-contained HTML page (inline CSS and JavaScript, no external dependencies, no MathJax; math in plain HTML) that the instructor projects and students read afterwards, and that works on a phone. Header line "Math 111 · Module: What's for breakfast? (week 2) · Lecture 3 of 3", then the lecture title. Numbered sections with plain titles that name their content. In-class tasks are boxed and numbered with time and tool in the label, for example "Task 2 (about 4 minutes, calculator)", each saying exactly what to produce, with its solution in a collapsed box right after it; task times add up to about half the period. A Sources list at the end, cited in the text with bracketed numbers, each source with its date and the figure quoted. Include an interactive element (for example a slider or a draggable point that redraws a graph) only if students use it in a task; when the lecture has graphs, draw them with inline SVG or canvas. Where students use a spreadsheet, give the layout as a CSV download and a copy-to-clipboard layout, with formulas, and say they were not tested in Excel or Google Sheets unless you tested them. The lecture period is 50 minutes, including in-class tasks.

Note on network access: direct curl/wget to outside sites is blocked in this environment; use the WebFetch tool to read sources, and save the relevant source text in draft/src/. A number that stands for the world is real and sourced, or labeled as made up.
