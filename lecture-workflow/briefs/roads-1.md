Module: Building mountain roads (week 10)   Lecture: 1 of 3   Title: Weighted graphs

Situation: A county must connect a handful of mountain towns by road, and each possible road segment has a construction cost. The question students will care about: how do you even draw this problem so it can be reasoned about?

Mathematics: Graphs as vertices and edges; drawing a map as a graph and noticing what is lost (exact positions) and kept (connections); weighted graphs with costs on edges; paths, cycles and connected graphs; trees as connected graphs with no cycles; counting edges in small trees and noticing that a tree on n vertices has n − 1 edges (shown with examples, argued in words); the problem of connecting every town as cheaply as possible, posed for lecture 10.2.

Learning objectives addressed: reading diagrams and tables; logic; word problems.

Students already know: Students have done weeks 1 to 9; this module uses arithmetic and the careful step-by-step reasoning from week 9 (following an algorithm, an argument by contradiction). Assume nothing else.

Data and sources (the writer must find, verify and cite each; save the source text in draft/src/):
- Real towns in a named mountain area (for example in the Washington Cascades) and real road distances or straight-line distances between them from a cited source (for example WSDOT or a mapping source with access date); or a clearly labeled made-up map.
- A real cost per lane-mile or per mile of new rural road construction from a named transportation agency source (for example WSDOT or FHWA) if costs are derived from distances; otherwise label costs as made up.

In-class tools: calculators, projector, whiteboard, paper for drawing graphs.

Format and length: a single self-contained HTML page (inline CSS and JavaScript, no external dependencies, no MathJax; math in plain HTML) that the instructor projects and students read afterwards, and that works on a phone. Header line "Math 111 · Module: Building mountain roads (week 10) · Lecture 1 of 3", then the lecture title. Numbered sections with plain titles that name their content. In-class tasks are boxed and numbered with time and tool in the label, for example "Task 2 (about 4 minutes, calculator)", each saying exactly what to produce, with its solution in a collapsed box right after it; task times add up to about half the period. A Sources list at the end, cited in the text with bracketed numbers, each source with its date and the figure quoted. Include an interactive element (for example a slider or a draggable point that redraws a graph) only if students use it in a task; when the lecture has graphs, draw them with inline SVG or canvas. Where students use a spreadsheet, give the layout as a CSV download and a copy-to-clipboard layout, with formulas, and say they were not tested in Excel or Google Sheets unless you tested them. The lecture period is 50 minutes, including in-class tasks.

Note on network access: direct curl/wget to outside sites is blocked in this environment; use the WebFetch tool to read sources, and save the relevant source text in draft/src/. A number that stands for the world is real and sourced, or labeled as made up.
