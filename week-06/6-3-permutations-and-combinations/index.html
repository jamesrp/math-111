A group of friends orders a round of fall drinks. Two kinds of counting questions come up at the counter.

  • Choosing. The group wants to share 3 different drinks from the 12 and taste them all. How many different sets of 3 drinks could they order?
  • Ordering. The barista finishes the drinks one at a time. In how many different orders can the drinks come out?

In the first question, the order the drinks are picked in does not change what ends up on the table. In the second question, different orders are different answers. Both questions can be answered by listing every possibility, but the lists get long very fast. A short calculation gives the same counts.

Simplifications: the group sizes in this lecture are made up. The lecture assumes all 12 drinks are available unless a problem says otherwise; actual availability varies by store.

Task 1 (about 3 minutes, paper)

Three friends order a Pumpkin Spice Latte (L), an Iced Pumpkin Cream Matcha (M) and an Iced Chaider (H). The barista finishes them one at a time.

  1. List every order in which the three drinks can come out. One order is L, M, H.
  2. Use the multiplication principle from lecture 6.1 to count the possible orders for 4 different drinks, and then for 6 different drinks. Write the product you used each time.
Solution to Task 1

(a) There are 6 orders: LMH, LHM, MLH, MHL, HLM, HML. Two of them start with L, two start with M and two start with H.

(b) With 4 drinks there are 4 choices for the first drink out, then 3 for the second, 2 for the third and 1 for the last: 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 24 orders. With 6 drinks: 6 × 5 × 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 720 orders.

2. Arranging n items in a row: n factorial

The count in Task 1 follows the multiplication principle. Any of the 3 drinks can come out first. After that, 2 drinks are left for second place, and then 1 drink for last place. The number of orders is 3 × 2 × 1 = 6. The same reasoning works for any number of different items.

n factorial. For a whole number n ≥ 1, n! (read "n factorial") is the product

n! = n × (n − 1) × (n − 2) × … × 2 × 1.

It counts the ways to arrange n different items in a row.

Examples: 3! = 3 × 2 × 1 = 6, 4! = 24, 5! = 120 and 6! = 720. By agreement, 0! = 1: there is exactly one way to arrange no items. Excel follows the same agreement: =FACT(0) returns 1 [3]. The formulas in sections 3 and 4 need it.

Factorials grow quickly. If the barista had to make all 12 fall drinks one after another, the number of possible orders would be 12! = 479,001,600, close to half a billion.

Task 2 (about 4 minutes, calculator)

  1. Compute 10! and 12!.
  2. Divide 12! by 10!. Write one sentence explaining why the answer equals 12 × 11.
  3. Someone writes down one order of all 12 fall drinks every second, without stopping. How many years would it take to write all 12! orders? Use 365.25 days per year.
Solution to Task 2

(a) 10! = 3,628,800 and 12! = 479,001,600.

(b) 12! ÷ 10! = 132 = 12 × 11. The product 12! is 12 × 11 × (10 × 9 × … × 1) = 12 × 11 × 10!, so dividing by 10! leaves 12 × 11.

(c) Convert seconds to years by multiplying by fractions equal to 1, as in week 1:

479,001,600 s × (1 min / 60 s) × (1 h / 60 min) × (1 day / 24 h) = 5,544 days.

5,544 days × (1 year / 365.25 days) ≈ 15.2 years.

3. Choosing and ordering k of n items: permutations

Three friends each order a different fall drink from the 12, and we keep track of who gets which. Friend 1 has 12 choices. Friend 2 has 11 choices left, and Friend 3 has 10. By the multiplication principle there are 12 × 11 × 10 = 1,320 ways.

Here order matters, in the same sense as in section 2. Friend 1, Friend 2 and Friend 3 act like first, second and third place: a way of handing out drinks is a list of 3 drinks in a fixed order, the first for Friend 1, the second for Friend 2 and the third for Friend 3. "Friend 1 gets the Pumpkin Spice Latte and Friend 2 gets the Iced Chaider" is a different outcome from "Friend 1 gets the Iced Chaider and Friend 2 gets the Pumpkin Spice Latte."

Permutation. A permutation of k items from n is a choice of k different items from n items, in a particular order. The number of them is written P(n, k):

P(n, k) = n × (n − 1) × … × (n − k + 1) (k factors).

For three friends and 12 drinks, n = 12 and k = 3. The product has 3 factors, and the last one is 12 − 3 + 1 = 10, so P(12, 3) = 12 × 11 × 10 = 1,320.

The same number comes from factorials. Write 12! and 9! as products and cancel the common factors 9 × 8 × … × 1:

P(12, 3) = 12! divided by 9!

= 12 × 11 × 10 × 9 × … × 1 divided by 9 × … × 1

= 12 × 11 × 10 = 1,320.

In general, P(n, k) = n! divided by (n − k)! for whole numbers k from 0 to n.

When k = n, this gives n! ÷ 0! = n!, the count of arrangements from section 2. When k = 0, it gives n! ÷ n! = 1: there is one way to choose nothing.

Simplification: each friend orders a different drink. If two friends could order the same drink, each friend would have 12 choices and the count would be 12 × 12 × 12 = 1,728.

Task 3 (about 3 minutes, calculator)

  1. Suppose (made up for this task) that 2 of the 12 fall drinks are sold out, so 10 are available. Three friends each order a different one of the 10. Keeping track of who gets which, how many ways are there?
  2. A group of 5 friends (made up) each orders a different drink from all 12. Compute P(12, 5) two ways: as a product of 5 factors, and as 12! ÷ 7!.
Solution to Task 3

(a) P(10, 3) = 10 × 9 × 8 = 720.

(b) P(12, 5) = 12 × 11 × 10 × 9 × 8 = 95,040. Also 12! ÷ 7! = 479,001,600 ÷ 5,040 = 95,040.

4. Choosing k of n when order does not matter: combinations

Back to the choosing question from section 1. The friends will order 3 different drinks to share, chosen from all 12 fall drinks. Nobody cares who orders which. The group only cares which 3 drinks end up on the table.

The count P(12, 3) = 1,320 from section 3 is too big for this question. It counts "Pumpkin Spice Latte, then Iced Chaider, then Pecan Cortado" and "Pecan Cortado, then Pumpkin Spice Latte, then Iced Chaider" as different, though both put the same 3 drinks on the table. To find out how much too big, start with a list small enough to write out.

Task 4 (about 5 minutes, paper)

Use four iced fall drinks: B = Iced Banana Bread Latte, H = Iced Chaider, M = Iced Pumpkin Cream Matcha, S = Iced Pumpkin Cream Shaken Espresso.

  1. List every ordered choice of 3 of these drinks (tasted first, second, third), such as BHM and BMH. Check with the multiplication principle that you have 4 × 3 × 2 = 24.
  2. Sort your list into groups that use the same 3 drinks, ignoring order. How many groups are there? How many ordered choices are in each group?
Solution to Task 4

The 24 ordered choices fall into 4 groups of 6:

Drinks B, H, MBHM BMH HBM HMB MBH MHB
Drinks B, H, SBHS BSH HBS HSB SBH SHB
Drinks B, M, SBMS BSM MBS MSB SBM SMB
Drinks H, M, SHMS HSM MHS MSH SHM SMH

Each group has 6 = 3! ordered choices, because putting 3 chosen drinks in order is arranging 3 items in a row.

Task 4 shows the pattern. Each unordered choice of 3 drinks appears in the ordered list exactly 3! = 6 times, once for each way to arrange those 3 drinks. So the number of unordered choices is the ordered count divided by 6:

24 ÷ 3! = 24 ÷ 6 = 4.

The same reasoning works for choosing 2 of the 4 drinks. There are 4 × 3 = 12 ordered choices: BH, HB, BM, MB, BS, SB, HM, MH, HS, SH, MS, SM. Each pair appears 2! = 2 times, so there are 12 ÷ 2 = 6 pairs: BH, BM, BS, HM, HS, MS.

Combination. A combination of k items from n is a choice of k different items from n items where order does not matter. In the language of lecture 6.2, it is a subset with k elements. The number of them is written C(n, k) and read "n choose k".

Each combination is counted k! times in P(n, k), so, for whole numbers k from 0 to n,

C(n, k) = P(n, k) divided by k! = n! divided by the product k! times (n − k)!k! (n − k)!.

The choosing question from section 1, with all 12 fall drinks available:

C(12, 3) = 12 × 11 × 10 divided by 3 × 2 × 1 = 1,320 divided by 6 = 220.

The group can order 220 different sets of 3 drinks. Listing them at 10 seconds per set would take 2,200 seconds, about 37 minutes.

With the made-up sold-out case from Task 3, only 10 drinks are available, and the count is C(10, 3) = 720 ÷ 6 = 120.

Computing on a calculator. When dividing by a product, put the product in parentheses:

12 × 11 × 10 ÷ (3 × 2 × 1) = 220.

Typing 12 × 11 × 10 ÷ 3 × 2 × 1 without parentheses gives 880, because the calculator divides by 3 and then multiplies by 2 (order of operations, week 1). Dividing by each factor in turn also works: 12 × 11 × 10 ÷ 3 ÷ 2 ÷ 1 = 220. Some scientific calculators have functions marked nPr and nCr, on a key or in a menu, and some phone calculators show a factorial key (x! or n!) in scientific mode. Without them, the products above are enough.

5. Deciding from the wording whether order matters

To decide between P(n, k) and C(n, k), use the swap test. Take one outcome and swap two of the chosen items. If the result is a different outcome, order matters and the count is a permutation. If the result is the same outcome, order does not matter and the count is a combination.

Certain words usually point one way. The swap test decides when the words are unclear.

Order matters: permutationOrder does not matter: combination
first, second, thirdchoose a group of
rank, top 3 in orderpick some to share
who gets which (each person is a place in line, as in section 3)which ones, a set of
in what order, line upa sampler, a flight

Example: "Three friends each order a different drink from the 12." If the question cares which friend gets which drink, swapping two friends' drinks gives a different outcome, and the count is P(12, 3) = 1,320. If the question only asks which 3 drinks get ordered, the swap changes nothing, and the count is C(12, 3) = 220.

Task 5 (about 5 minutes, calculator)

For each problem, decide whether order matters. Write the count as P(n, k), C(n, k) or n!, and compute it.

  1. The group orders a flight of 4 different fall drinks, chosen from all 12, to share.
  2. Four friends (made up) each order a different fall drink from the 12. We keep track of who gets which.
  3. You rank your top 3 of the 6 new fall drinks, from favorite to third favorite.
  4. You choose 2 of the 6 new fall drinks to try this week.
  5. The group picks 2 different fall drinks from the 12: one to order on Friday and one to order on Saturday.
Solution to Task 5
  1. Swapping two drinks in the flight gives the same flight. Order does not matter: C(12, 4) = (12 × 11 × 10 × 9) ÷ (4 × 3 × 2 × 1) = 11,880 ÷ 24 = 495.
  2. The four friends act like first, second, third and fourth place, so swapping two friends' drinks gives a different outcome. Order matters: P(12, 4) = 12 × 11 × 10 × 9 = 11,880.
  3. Swapping your first and second favorite changes the ranking. Order matters: P(6, 3) = 6 × 5 × 4 = 120.
  4. Trying drink X and then drink Y gives the same 2 drinks as trying Y and then X. Order does not matter: C(6, 2) = (6 × 5) ÷ 2 = 15.
  5. The word "picks" suggests a combination, but the swap test says otherwise. Pumpkin Spice Latte on Friday and Iced Chaider on Saturday is a different plan from Iced Chaider on Friday and Pumpkin Spice Latte on Saturday. Friday and Saturday act like first and second place. Order matters: P(12, 2) = 12 × 11 = 132.

7. Combinations and the 2n subsets

Lecture 6.2 showed that a set with n elements has 2n subsets: each element is either in the subset or out, so there are 2 × 2 × … × 2 = 2n subsets. A combination of k items is a subset with k elements, so sorting all the subsets by size gives a second way to count them. Task 1 of lecture 6.2 sorted the subsets of three toppings this way.

Take the 3 drinks from Task 1: Pumpkin Spice Latte (L), Iced Pumpkin Cream Matcha (M) and Iced Chaider (H).

Size kSubsetsC(3, k)
0∅ (order none of the three)1
1{L}, {M}, {H}3
2{L, M}, {L, H}, {M, H}3
3{L, M, H}1
Total8

The total 1 + 3 + 3 + 1 = 8 equals 23. Both sides count the same 8 subsets. The left side counts them size by size. The right side counts them by deciding in or out for each drink. The same holds for any n:

C(n, 0) + C(n, 1) + C(n, 2) + … + C(n, n) = 2n.

In summation notation (week 4): Σk=0n C(n, k) = 2n.
