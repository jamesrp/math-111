Expected return and risk

1. The claim that the stock market averages about 10% a year

People who invest often hear that the stock market averages about 10% a year. One published version: the S&P 500, with dividends, has grown at “approximately 9.8%” a year since 1926 [3]. Lecture 7.2 followed the S&P 500 day by day for five years, from September 30, 2021 to October 2, 2026 [2]. Five years are too few to check a claim about the long run, so this lecture uses the same index over a much longer time: its total return for every year from 1928 to 2025 [1]. A total return adds the dividends the companies paid to the change in price. The daily closes of lecture 7.2 are prices only, without dividends.

The index got its present form of 500 companies on March 4, 1957. From 1926 until then it was a smaller index of 90 companies [3], and the returns in [1] for 1928 to 1956 come from that smaller index. This lecture calls the whole series the S&P 500.

A loss is written as a negative return. In 2022 the index lost 18.04%, so its return was −18.04%.

Two questions follow from the claim:

  • What does an average yearly return say about one person's money over a few years?
  • Why does a 50% gain followed by a 50% loss leave you 25% below where you started?

Simplification: the returns ignore taxes and fund fees, and they are not adjusted for inflation.

2. Expected value of a simple game

Start with a game made up for this example. You roll a fair die once.

  • A roll of 1 or 2: you pay $3.
  • A roll of 3, 4 or 5: you win $1.
  • A roll of 6: you win $6.

Write a payment as a negative amount: paying $3 is an amount of −$3.

Task 1 (about 3 minutes, calculator)

Suppose you play the game 600 times.

  1. About how many times do you pay $3, win $1 and win $6?
  2. What is your total over the 600 games?
  3. What is your total per game? Produce one dollar amount.
Solution to Task 1

(a) The sample space is U = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}, as in lecture 7.1. By the long-run meaning of probability, each number comes up in about 1/6 of the games, so about 600 ÷ 6 = 100 times. You pay $3 about 200 times (rolls 1 and 2), win $1 about 300 times (rolls 3, 4 and 5) and win $6 about 100 times.

(b) 200 × (−$3) + 300 × $1 + 100 × $6 = −$600 + $300 + $600 = $300

(c) $300 ÷ 600 = $0.50 per game.

Dividing by 600 can be done term by term. 200/600 = 2/6 is the probability of paying $3, 300/600 = 3/6 the probability of winning $1, and 100/600 = 1/6 the probability of winning $6. In the notation of lecture 7.1, the event A = {1, 2} has P(A) = |A|/|U| = 2/6. So the total per game is

(−$3) × 2/6 + $1 × 3/6 + $6 × 1/6 = −$1.00 + $0.50 + $1.00 = $0.50

The number 600 dropped out. Any large number of games gives about the same $0.50 per game.

Expected value. The expected value of an uncertain amount is the probability-weighted average of its possible values: multiply each possible value by its probability, then add.

expected value = (value 1) × (probability 1) + (value 2) × (probability 2) + … + (last value) × (last probability)

Example: the expected value of the game is $0.50.

No single game pays $0.50. You pay $3, win $1 or win $6. The expected value is the average per game over many games, in the long-run sense of lecture 7.1.

3. Expected change of one trading day in the lecture 7.2 model

Lecture 7.2 built a model of the market from the 1003 trading days of October 1, 2021 to September 30, 2025 [2]. Each day is an up day with probability P(up) = 533/1003 = 0.531 and a down day with probability P(down) = 1 − 0.531 = 0.469, by the complement rule. An up day raises the index by 0.80% and a down day lowers it by 0.80%, the average sizes of the real up and down days.

Task 2 (about 2 minutes, calculator)

Compute the expected change of one trading day in the lecture 7.2 model, as a percentage rounded to 4 decimal places.

Solution to Task 2
0.80% × 0.531 + (−0.80%) × 0.469 = 0.4248% − 0.3752% = 0.0496%

The expected change is 0.0496%, about 0.05% a day. Over the 1003 real days, the average of the daily changes was 0.0502% [2], close to the model's value.

An expected change of 0.05% a day is small next to the size of a single day's move, 0.80% up or down: about one sixteenth of it (0.80 ÷ 0.0496 = 16.1). No day in the model moves by 0.05%. As in the game, the expected value describes the average over many days.

4. Expected return of one year of the S&P 500

The same idea works for whole years. The table in [1] has 98 yearly returns, from 1928 to 2025. Pick one of the 98 years at random, each with probability 1/98. The sample space U is the set of 98 years, and |U| = 98. The expected return of the picked year is the sum of (return × 1/98) over all 98 years. That is the same as adding the 98 returns and dividing by 98, the ordinary average.

Ninety-eight terms are a lot to add by hand. Grouping the years gives a shorter calculation. The groups below are three events, and each of the 98 years is in exactly one of them. The group averages were computed from the data in [1].

Table 2. The 98 yearly returns, 1928 to 2025, in three groups [1]

EventNumber of yearsAverage return in the group
L: a loss (return below 0%)26−13.5%
M: a gain from 0% up to 20%3611.2%
G: a gain of 20% or more3630.8%
All years98

Task 3 (about 3 minutes, calculator)

A year is picked at random from the 98.

  1. Find P(L), P(M) and P(G), each as a decimal rounded to 3 places.
  2. Treat the group average as the return of every year in the group. Compute the expected return: (group average) × (probability) for each group, then add. Produce one percentage.
  3. Find P(L ∪ G), the probability that the year is a loss or a gain of 20% or more.
Solution to Task 3

(a) P(L) = 26/98 = 0.265, P(M) = 36/98 = 0.367, P(G) = 36/98 = 0.367. Rounded, they add to 0.999 instead of 1.

(b) (−13.5%) × 0.265 + 11.2% × 0.367 + 30.8% × 0.367 = −3.578% + 4.110% + 11.304% = 11.836%, about 11.84%

(c) L and G have no years in common, L ∩ G = ∅, so P(L ∪ G) = 26/98 + 36/98 = 62/98 = 0.633. The complement rule gives the same: L ∪ G is the complement M′, and 1 − 36/98 = 0.633.

The exact average of all 98 returns is 11.86% [1]. Task 3 gave 11.84% because of rounding. With unrounded probabilities and group averages the grouped calculation gives exactly 11.86%, since a group average times the number of years in the group is the sum of the returns in that group. This ordinary average is called the arithmetic mean of the returns.

For a year picked at random from 1928 to 2025, the expected return is 11.86%. Using 11.86% as the expected return of next year is a simplification, like the coin-flip model of lecture 7.2: it treats next year as one more random pick from these 98 years. Nothing guarantees that the future behaves like the past.

Few single years come close to 11.86%. Only 7 of the 98 years had a return within 2 percentage points of it (between 9.86% and 13.86%), and only 15 were within 5 percentage points. In 26 years the index lost money. The figure 11.86% is also larger than the “about 10%” of Section 1. Section 7 explains the difference.

5. Returns as multipliers

From week 4: increasing an amount by a percentage is the same as multiplying it by 1 plus the rate. Write the return r as a decimal. One year changes a value like this:

value at end of year = value at start of year × (1 + r)

The number 1 + r is the multiplier for the year, as the daily multiplier was for a day in lecture 7.2. A return of 12% has multiplier 1 + 0.12 = 1.12. A return of −20% has multiplier 1 + (−0.20) = 0.80. A loss always has a multiplier below 1.

Task 4 (about 3 minutes, calculator)

Start with $1,000 each time. For each part write the two multipliers and the value after two years.

  1. A 50% gain, then a 50% loss.
  2. A 50% loss, then a 50% gain.
  3. A 10% gain, then a 10% loss.
Solution to Task 4

(a) Multipliers 1.5 and 0.5: $1,000 × 1.5 × 0.5 = $750.

(b) Multipliers 0.5 and 1.5: $1,000 × 0.5 × 1.5 = $750.

(c) Multipliers 1.1 and 0.9: $1,000 × 1.1 × 0.9 = $990.

Adding the returns would predict no change: 50% + (−50%) = 0%. That is wrong because each return applies to a different starting amount. The 50% gain applies to $1,000 and adds $500. The 50% loss applies to $1,500 and removes $750. Consecutive returns combine by multiplying their multipliers: 1.5 × 0.5 = 0.75, a 25% loss over the two years. The order does not matter, since 0.5 × 1.5 is also 0.75. This answers the second question of Section 1.

Lecture 7.2 met the same effect with days. In its model, one up day and one down day give 1.008 × 0.992 = 0.999936, a small loss, and a drop of 10% followed by a rise of 10% gives 0.90 × 1.10 = 0.99.

A loss needs a larger gain to recover. After a 50% loss the multiplier needed to get back to the start is 1 ÷ 0.5 = 2, a gain of 100%. A real case: in 1931 the S&P 500 returned −43.84% [1]. $1,000 became $1,000 × 0.5616 = $561.60, and getting back to $1,000 needed a gain of 1 ÷ 0.5616 − 1 = 0.7806, or 78.06%.

7. The growth rate actually achieved

The arithmetic mean adds the returns, but money grows by multiplying the multipliers. The number that describes what happened to the money is the growth rate: the single return that, repeated every year, gives the same final value. It is also called the geometric mean return.

If the total multiplier over n years is M, the growth rate g satisfies

(1 + g) × (1 + g) × … × (1 + g) = (1 + g)n = M

with n factors on the left. So 1 + g is the number that, multiplied by itself n times, gives M. That number is the n-th root of M, written M1/n. Example: 81/3 = 2, because 2 × 2 × 2 = 8. The square root of week 5 is the case n = 2: 91/2 = √9 = 3.

1 + g = M1/n,   so   g = M1/n − 1

On a calculator, type M, then the power key (xy or ^, as in lecture 7.2), then (1 ÷ n) in parentheses. Without the parentheses the calculator computes M1 and then divides by n. On many phones the power key appears only when the phone is turned sideways. To check an answer, raise 1 + g to the power n: the result should be M.

For 2000 to 2009, M = 0.9086 and n = 10: 0.90861/10 = 0.9905, so g = −0.0095, a growth rate of −0.95% a year, while the arithmetic mean was 1.16%. In the spreadsheet, enter =G3^(1/10)-1 in G4; it shows about −0.0095.

Task 6 (about 4 minutes, calculator)

Give each growth rate as a percentage rounded to 2 decimal places.

  1. Two years with returns +50% and −50% have total multiplier 0.75 (Task 4). Compute the growth rate per year and check it.
  2. In lecture 7.2, the index closed at 4307.54 on September 30, 2021 and at 6688.46 on September 30, 2025 [2]: a total multiplier of 1.5527 over 4 years. The four yearly changes were −16.76%, +19.59%, +34.38% and +16.07%. Compute their arithmetic mean and the growth rate per year.
  3. From the start of 1928 to the end of 2025, $1 in the S&P 500 grew to $11,575.91 (computed from [1]). That is 98 years. Compute the growth rate per year, then check it by raising 1 + g to the power 98.
Solution to Task 6

(a) 0.751/2 = 0.8660, so g = 0.8660 − 1 = −0.1340, a growth rate of −13.40% a year. The arithmetic mean of +50% and −50% is 0%. Check: 0.8660 × 0.8660 = 0.75.

(b) Arithmetic mean: (−16.76 + 19.59 + 34.38 + 16.07) ÷ 4 = 53.28 ÷ 4 = 13.32%. Growth rate: 1.55271/4 = 1.1163, so g = 11.63% a year. Check: 1.11634 = 1.5528, which is 1.5527 up to rounding. These are price changes without dividends, for years that run from October to September, so they differ from the calendar-year total returns in [1].

(c) 11,575.911/98 = 1.1002, so g = 0.1002, a growth rate of 10.02% a year. Check: 1.100298 ≈ 11,594, close to 11,575.91; the difference comes from rounding 1.1002 to four decimal places.

Task 6(c) checks the “about 10%” claim for 1928 to 2025. Money in the index grew at 10.02% a year. The arithmetic mean of the yearly returns, 11.86%, is almost 2 percentage points higher. The most recent 30 years, 1996 to 2025, show the same pattern: an arithmetic mean of 11.80% and a growth rate of 10.26% (both computed from [1]). The “about 10%” is a growth rate.

The same gap shows up day by day in the data of lecture 7.2 [2]. Over the 1003 trading days of the first four years, the average daily change was 0.0502%, while the growth rate per day was 1.55271/1003 − 1 = 0.0439%.

The growth rate is never above the arithmetic mean, and the two are equal only when every year has the same return. For returns of similar size, the gap tends to be wider when the yearly returns are more spread out. The +50% / −50% example has very spread-out returns, and its gap is large: an arithmetic mean of 0% and a growth rate of −13.40%.

8. Spread of returns as risk

Risk. In investing, risk means how widely the possible outcomes are spread around the expected value.

Two investments can have the same expected value and very different spreads. A second game, also made up: roll a fair die, pay $6 on a roll of 1 to 5, and win $33 on a roll of 6. Its expected value is (−$6) × 5/6 + $33 × 1/6 = −$5.00 + $5.50 = $0.50, the same as the game of Section 2. The outcomes of the first game lie between −$3 and $6. The outcomes of the second lie between −$6 and $33, and you lose on 5 rolls out of 6. The second game is riskier.

For the S&P 500, the worst year was 1931 (−43.84%) and the best was 1954 (52.56%) [1]. The graphs below let you choose a holding period: how many years an investor holds the index, and the first of those years. The “−1” and “+1” buttons move the first year by one.

9. What an average return means for one person's money

If money grew at exactly 10% every year, $1,000 would become $1,000 × 1.105 = $1,610.51 after five years. Read literally, the claim “about 10% a year” says this.

The real five-year periods give a different picture. There are 94 five-year periods from 1928 to 2025 in [1]: 1928 to 1932, 1929 to 1933, and so on up to 2021 to 2025.

Table 3. What $1,000 became in five years, 1928 to 2025 (computed from [1])

$1,000 became

Lowest (1928 to 1932)$506.65
Highest (1995 to 1999)$3,475.83

In 11 of the 94 periods the money ended below $1,000. In 55 periods it ended at or above $1,610.51.

Task 8 (about 2 minutes, calculator)

A five-year period is picked at random from the 94.

  1. Find P(the money ended below $1,000), as a fraction and as a decimal rounded to 3 places.
  2. Find P(the money ended at or above $1,610.51). Then use the complement rule to find P(the money ended below $1,610.51).
Solution to Task 8

(a) 11/94 = 0.117, about 1 period in 9.

(b) 55/94 = 0.585. By the complement rule, 1 − 55/94 = 39/94 = 0.415, about 2 periods in 5.

For one person's money over five years in this history, the typical outcome was a little better than a steady 10% a year: the median of $1,808 is a growth rate of about 12.6% a year. The outcomes were also spread widely in both directions. About 1 period in 9 lost money, and about 2 in 5 ended below the steady-10% amount. The average describes the center of many outcomes pooled together. It does not say which outcome one person gets. An investor with five years gets one draw from this spread, and the expected return gives no guarantee about that draw. Lecture 7.2 found the same for a single simulated year: starting from 6688.46, 96% of simulated years ended anywhere from 5884 to 9663.

These numbers describe 1928 to 2025. Future returns can differ.
