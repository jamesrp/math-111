Independence and simulation

1. Up days and down days of the S&P 500

The S&P 500 is a stock index: one number computed from the share prices of 500 large U.S. companies traded on the New York Stock Exchange or NASDAQ. Its value at market close is recorded each trading day [1]. The data are five years of these closing values, from September 30, 2021 (4307.54) to October 2, 2026 (7722.72), read from Yahoo Finance [2]. That is 1257 closing values, so 1256 trading days with a change from the day before.

The five years are split in two parts. The first four years, October 1, 2021 to September 30, 2025 (1003 trading days), are used to estimate the numbers in a model of the market. The last year, October 1, 2025 to October 2, 2026 (253 trading days), is kept aside. Section 6 compares the model with this last year, which the model has not seen.

Up day: a trading day on which the index closes higher than it closed on the previous trading day. Down day: a trading day on which it closes lower. Weekends and market holidays are skipped, so the trading day after a Friday is usually the next Monday. In the five years, no day closed exactly unchanged.

To compare the size of moves, use percents, as with prices in week 4. Divide a day's close by the previous close. The result is the daily multiplier. On October 1, 2025:

6711.20 ÷ 6688.46 = 1.0034

A multiplier of 1.0034 means the index was multiplied by 1 + 0.0034, so it rose 0.34%. A multiplier below 1 is a down day: 0.99 means a drop of 1%.

2. Estimating the probability of an up day

Ten days are too few to say much. In lecture 7.1, the probability of an event was its long-run frequency: the fraction of tries in which the event happens, over very many tries. Here one try is one trading day, and the event is “up day”. A script counted the 1003 trading days of the first four years [2]: 533 were up days and 470 were down days. The fraction of up days estimates the probability that a day is an up day. Write P(up) for this probability:

P(up) ≈ 533/1003 = 0.531

Every day is either up or down, so “down day” is the complement up′ of “up day”. By the complement rule of lecture 7.1, P(down) = 1 − P(up) ≈ 1 − 0.531 = 0.469.

3. Independent events and the multiplication rule

Lecture 7.1 rolled a red die and a blue die. By the multiplication principle of lecture 6.1 there are 6 × 6 = 36 equally likely pairs (red, blue). Let A be the event “the red die shows 6” and B the event “the blue die shows 6”. Only one pair is in both: A ∩ B = {(6, 6)}, so P(A ∩ B) = 1/36. The same answer comes from multiplying: P(A) × P(B) = 1/6 × 1/6 = 1/36. The red die shows 6 in 1/6 of all rolls, and in 1/6 of those rolls the blue die also shows 6. This works because what one die shows does not affect the other.

Independent events. Two events A and B are independent if knowing that A happened does not change the probability of B. Example: A = “the red die shows 6” and B = “the blue die shows 6”.

Multiplication rule. If A and B are independent, the probability that both happen is

P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B)

As in lecture 7.1, A ∩ B is read “A and B”.

Market days as coin flips

The simplest model of the market treats each trading day as the flip of a coin that lands “up” with probability 0.531, independent of every other day. Real traders react to news and to what happened the day before, so this is a simplification, and it has to be checked against the data. Under the model, for a day and the next trading day,

P(up on day 1 ∩ up on day 2) = 0.531 × 0.531 = 0.282

In the four years there are 1002 pairs of a day and the next trading day (October 1 and October 4, 2021, then October 4 and October 5, and so on). Both days were up in 278 of these pairs, and 278/1002 = 0.277 [2]. That is close to 0.282.

A second check compares the day after an up day with the day after a down day [2]:

DaysNext day upFraction
After an up day5322780.523
After a down day4702540.540

The last day, September 30, 2025, was an up day with no next day inside the four years, so only 532 of the 533 up days count. If the direction of one day changed the chance for the next day by much, these two fractions would be far apart. They differ by 0.540 − 0.523 = 0.017, or 1.7 percentage points. (A percentage point is the unit for a difference between two percents: 54.0% and 52.3% differ by 1.7 percentage points.) A computer ran the coin-flip model for 1003 independent days 20,000 times, and a gap of 1.7 points or more appeared in 58% of the runs. A gap this small is what independent days produce, so the data give no reason to drop the coin-flip model for the direction of a day's move.

An example where the events are not independent

Let A = “the index closes above 6000 on a given day” and B = “the index closes above 6000 on the next trading day”. In the four years the index closed above 6000 on 115 of the 1003 days [2], so P(A) ≈ 115/1003 = 0.115, and P(B) is the same. Of the 1002 pairs of a day and the next trading day, 106 had both days above 6000 [2]. The dashed line in Figure 1 is at 6000.

(c) Prediction: 0.115 × 0.115 = 0.0132, which is about 13 of the 1002 pairs (1002 × 0.0132 = 13.2). Observed: 106/1002 = 0.106, about 8 times the prediction. A and B are not independent: knowing that the index closed above 6000 today makes a close above 6000 tomorrow much more likely. The reason for the mismatch in part (c) is that each day starts from the previous close. The average size of a move, ignoring its direction, is 0.80% [2], about 48 points at a level of 6000. So a close above 6000 is usually followed by another one. The level of the index depends strongly on the day before. The direction of the move, up or down, behaves close to independently from day to day.

4. The chance of five up days in a row

Five up days in a row means: up on day 1 ∩ up on day 2 ∩ up on day 3 ∩ up on day 4 ∩ up on day 5. If days are independent, the multiplication rule extends to five events: multiply all five probabilities. For a fair coin, five heads in a row has probability

0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.55 = 1/32 = 0.03125

On a calculator, use the power key (xy or ^) and enter 0.5 ^ 5.

In the four years the index had 16 streaks of five or more up days in a row [2]. The longest was 9 up days, from April 22 to May 2, 2025.

Task 3 (about 4 minutes, calculator)

  1. Use P(up) = 0.531. Compute the probability of five up days in a row, rounded to four decimal places, and write it as “about 1 in ___”. Then use the complement rule to find the probability of at least one down day among the five days.
  2. Compute the probability of ten up days in a row, 0.53110, rounded to five decimal places, and write it as “about 1 in ___”.
  3. The 1003 trading days contain 999 stretches of five consecutive days: days 1 to 5, days 2 to 6, and so on up to days 999 to 1003. Multiply 999 by the probability of five up days in a row from (a) to estimate how many of these stretches are five up days. Compare with the real count, which is 42 [2].
Solution to Task 3

(a) 0.5315 = 0.0422, and 1 ÷ 0.0422 = 23.7, so about 1 in 24. This is a little more likely than the fair coin's 1 in 32, because 0.531 is above 0.5. At least one down day is the complement of five up days: 1 − 0.0422 = 0.9578.

(b) 0.53110 = 0.00178, and 1 ÷ 0.00178 = 562, so about 1 in 560.

(c) 999 × 0.0422 = 42.2, so the model expects about 42 stretches of five up days. The real count is 42. A fair coin would expect 999 × 0.03125 = 31.2.

The match in part (c) is a check on the average count. How much the count varies from one period to another, or how likely a 9-day streak is somewhere in one year, the multiplication rule cannot settle. The 999 stretches overlap: days 1 to 5 and days 2 to 6 share four days, so the events “stretch 1 is all up” and “stretch 2 is all up” are not independent. Listing outcomes is also out of reach, since one year of 253 days has 2253 patterns of up and down days. A simulation can answer these questions.

5. Simulating a year of random days in a spreadsheet

A simulation lets the computer flip the coin for each day. Two spreadsheet functions do the work.

=RAND() returns a random number between 0 and 1. Every number in that range is equally likely, so RAND()<0.531 is true 53.1% of the time. The spreadsheet picks a new number every time it recalculates.

=IF(condition, value if true, value if false) checks a condition and returns one of two values. For example, =IF(RAND()<0.531,"Up","Down") returns “Up” with probability 0.531 and “Down” otherwise.

The size of each simulated move uses the averages from Section 2. An up day multiplies the index by 1.0080 (a rise of 0.80%), and a down day multiplies it by 0.9920 (a drop of 0.80%). This is a second simplification, and it has two effects.

  • Real days range from −5.97% to +9.52%, and 69 of the 1003 days moved more than 2%. The model never moves more than 0.80% in a day, so its simulated years spread out less than real years can.
  • Big real moves come in clusters. After one of the 69 days that moved more than 2%, the next day also moved more than 2% in 13 cases (19%), while over all days the share is 69/1003 = 7% [2]. So the size of a move is not independent of the day before, even though its direction nearly is. The model leaves this out.

As a check, a run of the model over the four years with exactly the real 533 up days and 470 down days ends at

4307.54 × 1.0080533 × 0.9920470 = 6905

The real close on September 30, 2025 was 6688.46, about 3% lower. The order of the days does not change the model's number, because multiplication can be done in any order. The real index ends lower because its moves vary in size. A drop of 10% followed by a rise of 10% gives 0.90 × 1.10 = 0.99, a loss, and bigger swings lose more. The model replaces every swing by 0.80%, so it misses this loss.

After copying down, row 4 contains =A3+1, =IF(B4="Up",1.008,0.992), =D3*C4 and so on: each formula refers to the row above, as in lecture 1.3.

  • Column D is a random walk: a list of values in which each value is the one before it changed by a random step. Here each day's index is the previous day's index times that day's multiplier, the same rule as a balance growing by a percent in week 4, except that the percent is chosen at random each day.
  • Column E counts the current run of up days. On an up day it adds 1 to the count above it; on a down day it goes back to 0. Its largest value is the longest streak.
  • COUNTIF(B3:B255,"Up") counts the cells from B3 to B255 that contain “Up”. MAX(E3:E255) gives the largest number from E3 to E255.
  • To get a new run: in Excel press F9 (Fn+F9 on a Mac); in Google Sheets type any number into an empty cell and press Enter.

Solution to Task 5

Counts vary from one set of 100 paths to the next. A calculation beyond this course gives the model's long-run shares.

(a) 81% of simulated years end above the start, so expect a count near 81. A path ends higher when it has at least 128 up days out of 253. With P(up) = 0.531 that happens in most years, but about one year in five still ends lower. The first of the four real years, October 2021 to September 2022, fell 16.76%.

(b) 44% of simulated years end above 7722.72, so expect a count near 44. The real year ended close to the middle of the simulated years.

(c) The readout shows a fraction close to 0.531, since 100 paths are 25,300 simulated days.

(d) The real path usually lies inside the spread of blue paths, since it ends near the middle of the simulated years. (A batch of 100 paths does not cover every possible year, so in some batches the real path can poke outside at some point.) It looks different in its steps: its daily moves have many sizes, from −2.71% to +2.91% in this year, while every blue step is exactly 0.80%. The real year was calmer than the four years the model came from. Its average move, ignoring direction, was 0.62% [2], against 0.80% in the model. A calmer year is one of the things the two-size model cannot produce.

The real year is one run. It fits the model, but a single run cannot confirm a model. It shows only that this year was the kind of year the model produces. The model cannot say which way the next year will go.

7. One year versus the long run

One year of about 253 trading days is a small sample. In the model with P(up) = 0.531, the fraction of up days in one simulated year falls between 0.470 and 0.593 in 96% of years (a calculation beyond this course). All five real years fall inside this range: 0.480, 0.492, 0.590 and 0.564 for the four years of Section 2, and 136/253 = 0.538 for the last year. So the differences between real years in Section 2 are the size that chance alone produces.

For a fair coin, P(up) = 0.5, one year gives a fraction between 0.439 and 0.561 in 96% of years. The last year's fraction 0.538 lies inside both ranges, so one year of data cannot tell a market with an up-chance of 0.531 from a fair coin. Four years do better. A fair coin gives 533 or more heads in 1003 flips only 2.5% of the time. The four years of data therefore give good evidence that up days are more common than down days, though even 1003 days only just show it.

Pooling runs is how the long run shows up. For one sequence of simulated years (computer, P(up) = 0.531):

The first year alone gave 0.601, far from 0.531. As more years are pooled, the fraction settles near 0.531, as the fraction of sixes settled near 1/6 in lecture 7.1. The readout under Figure 2 shows the same thing: its fraction of up days moves a lot after 1 path and very little after 100.

The streak probability works the same way. The value 0.0422 from Task 3 describes what happens over many five-day stretches. It does not predict any particular week. For whole years of 253 days, a calculation beyond this course gives:

  • 99.6% of simulated years contain at least one streak of five or more up days.
  • 33% of simulated years contain a streak of 9 or more up days. The last real year had one, from May 20 to June 2, 2026 [2], so a 9-day streak is not unusual for a year.
  • The model expects 249 × 0.0422 = 10.5 all-up five-day stretches in a year of 253 days. The last real year had 12 [2].
