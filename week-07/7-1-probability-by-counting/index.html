Suppose you let chance choose your drink. Number the 31 drinks from 1 to 31 and let a spreadsheet pick a number with =RANDBETWEEN(1,31). Every drink has the same chance of being picked. Real customers do not order this way. The random pick is a simplification that makes the counting work.

The pick lands on one of 31 equally likely drinks, and 12 of the 31 are lattes. We write the chance of a latte as the fraction 12/31. On a calculator, 12 ÷ 31 = 0.387…, which is about 38.7%.

The question: what does a chance like 38.7% or 1/6 mean when you pick only once, and how do you find a chance when the outcomes are too many to list? Section 3 answers the first part and Section 7 the second.

Task 1 (about 3 minutes, calculator)

A drink is picked at random from the 31 hot drinks. For each event below, write the chance as a fraction, as a decimal rounded to 3 places, and as a percentage rounded to 1 decimal place.

  1. The drink is a cortado.
  2. The drink is from the Brewed Coffee group.
  3. The drink has “Pistachio” in its name.
Solution to Task 1
  1. 4 of the 31 drinks are cortados: 4/31 ≈ 0.129 = 12.9%.
  2. 6 drinks are brewed coffee: 6/31 ≈ 0.194 = 19.4%.
  3. Two drinks have “Pistachio” in the name, Pistachio Latte and Pistachio Cortado: 2/31 ≈ 0.065 = 6.5%.

To turn a decimal into a percentage, multiply by 100 and add the % sign: 0.129 × 100 = 12.9, so 0.129 = 12.9%. The fraction, the decimal and the percentage are the same number written three ways.

2. Sample space, events and the counting formula

Task 1 used one calculation three times: count the drinks in the event, then divide by 31. In the set language of lecture 6.2:

  • An experiment is something with an uncertain result: picking a drink at random, rolling a die, drawing a card. Each possible result is an outcome.
  • The sample space is the set of all outcomes. It is the universal set for the experiment, so we write it U, as in lecture 6.2. For the random drink, U is the set of 31 drinks in Section 1, and |U| = 31. For one roll of a die, U = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6} and |U| = 6.
  • An event is a subset of U. The event “the drink is a cortado” is the set C = {Cortado, Pecan Cortado, Pistachio Cortado, Brown Sugar Oatmilk Cortado}, with |C| = 4. The event “the die shows an even number” is E = {2, 4, 6}, with |E| = 3.

As in lecture 6.2, |A| is the number of elements of the set A. When every outcome in U is equally likely, the probability of an event A, written P(A), is

P(A) = |A||U|

In words: the number of outcomes in A divided by the number of outcomes in U. For the die, P(E) = 3/6 = 1/2 = 50%. For the drink, P(C) = 4/31 ≈ 12.9%.

A probability is always between 0 and 1, or between 0% and 100%. The empty event ∅, such as “the die shows 7”, has probability 0. The event U itself has probability 1, because some outcome always happens.

The formula needs the condition “equally likely”. Section 5 shows what goes wrong without it.

3. What a probability means: the long run

A fair die has P(6) = 1/6. This does not mean that every 6 rolls contain exactly one six. Roll a die 6 times and you may get no sixes, or three. The probability describes what happens over many rolls: as the number of rolls grows, the fraction of rolls that show a six settles near 1/6 ≈ 0.167. The same reading applies to any probability. An event with probability 30% happens in about 30 of every 100 repetitions, when the experiment is repeated many times. This is the long-run frequency meaning of probability. For the random drink, about 38.7% of many random picks would be lattes.

A spreadsheet can roll a die a thousand times in a second, so the long-run meaning can be tested in class.

Solution to Task 2

Your numbers will differ from everyone else’s, because the rolls are random. The pattern below is what usually happens, not a guarantee for every run.

  • After 10 rolls the fraction jumps around a lot. With only 10 rolls it can only be 0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3 and so on, so it can never equal 0.167. Two tries can differ a lot, for example one at 0 and another at 0.3. No six at all in 10 rolls happens in about 16% of tries (a calculation beyond this course gives (5/6)10 ≈ 0.162).
  • After 100 rolls the fraction is usually between 0.10 and 0.23 (a calculation beyond this course gives about 94% of tries).
  • After 1000 rolls the fraction is usually between 0.14 and 0.19 (about 97% of tries). It changes the least from try to try, and it is usually the closest to 0.167. Usually, not always: a run with 2 sixes in the first 10 rolls, 17 in the first 100 and 180 in the first 1000 gives 0.20, 0.17 and 0.18, so its 100-roll fraction is the closest. If your run did something like that, it is a fair result, not a mistake. Comparing many tries across the room shows the pattern better than any one run.

On the simulator, the lines are far apart on the left of the graph and jump up and down. As the number of rolls grows, they settle and squeeze together around the dashed line at 1/6.

The more rolls, the closer the fraction of sixes tends to be to 1/6. That is what P(6) = 1/6 means.

4. The complement rule

Lecture 6.2 defined the complement A′ (read “A prime” or “not A”): the set of elements of U that are not in A. It counted the complement by subtracting:

|A′| = |U| − |A|

Dividing both sides by |U| turns this count into the complement rule for probabilities:

P(A′) = 1 − P(A)

Example. Let L be the event “the drink is a latte”. The chance that the random drink is not a latte is P(L′) = 1 − 12/31 = 19/31 ≈ 61.3%. Counting directly gives the same: |L′| = 31 − 12 = 19 drinks are not lattes.

For one drink, counting L′ directly is just as easy. The rule saves work when an event is hard to count and its complement is easy, as in Task 5.

5. Equally likely outcomes: rolling two dice

Roll two dice and add the numbers. The sum can be any of the 11 numbers 2, 3, 4, …, 12. Using the counting formula on these 11 sums would give P(sum is 7) = 1/11 ≈ 9.1%. The formula needs equally likely outcomes, so first check whether the 11 sums are equally likely. Before reading on, decide whether a sum of 7 and a sum of 2 should come up equally often.

A sample space with equally likely outcomes comes from looking at both dice. Think of them as a red die and a blue die. An outcome is a pair (red, blue), such as (3, 4). By the multiplication principle from lecture 6.1, the red die has 6 options and the blue die has 6 options whatever the red die shows, so there are 6 × 6 = 36 pairs. Each pair is equally likely, because each die is fair and what one die shows does not affect the other.

Task 3 (about 4 minutes, calculator and the table above)

Two fair dice are rolled. Use the table of 36 pairs. Give each answer as a fraction and as a percentage rounded to 1 decimal place.

  1. P(sum is 7).
  2. P(sum is 2).
  3. P(sum is not 7), using the complement rule.
  4. Is 1/11 the right answer to (a)? Explain in one sentence, using your counts from (a) and (b).
Solution to Task 3
  1. The pairs with sum 7 run along a diagonal of the table: (1,6), (2,5), (3,4), (4,3), (5,2), (6,1). That is 6 of 36 pairs: P(sum is 7) = 6/36 = 1/6 ≈ 16.7%.
  2. Only (1,1) has sum 2: P(sum is 2) = 1/36 ≈ 2.8%.
  3. P(sum is not 7) = 1 − 1/6 = 5/6 ≈ 83.3%. Check by counting: 36 − 6 = 30 pairs, and 30/36 = 5/6.
  4. No. The 11 sums are not equally likely: a sum of 7 comes from 6 pairs and a sum of 2 from only 1 pair, so the counting formula cannot be used on the list of sums.

Rolling two dice many times and recording the sums would show 7 coming up about 6 times as often as 2. That is the long-run meaning from Section 3.

The same mistake can happen with the menu. Suppose you pick one of the 9 groups at random, then a drink at random within that group. The Americano, alone in its group, is then picked 1 time in 9, a chance of 1/9 ≈ 11.1%. A particular latte needs the Latte group (1 time in 9) and then that latte out of 12, a chance of 1/9 × 1/12 = 1/108 ≈ 0.9%. Picking directly from the 31 drinks gives every drink the same chance, 1/31 ≈ 3.2%.

6. The probability of A or B

Lecture 6.2 counted a union with inclusion-exclusion. The union A ∪ B, read “A or B”, holds the outcomes in A or in B or in both. The intersection A ∩ B, read “A and B”, holds the outcomes in both. Adding |A| and |B| counts every outcome of A ∩ B twice, so it is subtracted once:

|A ∪ B| = |A| + |B| − |A ∩ B|

Dividing every term by |U| gives the same rule for probabilities:

P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B)

Example. A drink is picked at random from the 31. Let L be “the drink is a latte” and T be “the name contains Pistachio”. Then |L| = 12 and |T| = 2, and one drink, the Pistachio Latte, is in both: |L ∩ T| = 1.

|L ∪ T| = 12 + 2 − 1 = 13, so P(L ∪ T) = 13/31 ≈ 41.9%. Adding without subtracting would give 14 and count the Pistachio Latte twice. Check with the diagram: 11 + 1 + 1 = 13 drinks inside the circles and 18 outside, for 31 in all.

When two events have no outcomes in common, A ∩ B = ∅ and the rule is the addition principle of lecture 6.1. With C the cortados, L ∩ C = ∅, so P(L ∪ C) = 12/31 + 4/31 = 16/31 ≈ 51.6%.

Task 4 (about 4 minutes, calculator)

A standard deck has 52 cards: 4 suits (hearts, diamonds, clubs, spades) of 13 cards each. Each suit has 3 face cards: jack, queen and king. One card is drawn at random. Let H be the event “the card is a heart” and F the event “the card is a face card”.

  1. Find |H|, |F| and |H ∩ F|.
  2. Find P(H ∪ F) as a fraction and as a percentage rounded to 1 decimal place.
  3. Find the probability that the card is neither a heart nor a face card.
Solution to Task 4
  1. |H| = 13. |F| = 4 × 3 = 12. The hearts that are face cards are the jack, queen and king of hearts, so |H ∩ F| = 3.
  2. |H ∪ F| = 13 + 12 − 3 = 22. P(H ∪ F) = 22/52 = 11/26 ≈ 42.3%.
  3. “Neither a heart nor a face card” is the complement (H ∪ F)′. P((H ∪ F)′) = 1 − 22/52 = 30/52 = 15/26 ≈ 57.7%.

7. Counting with combinations: random sets of three drinks

A group buys 3 different lattes, chosen at random from the 12 lattes in Section 1. What is the chance that the set of 3 drinks includes the Pumpkin Spice Latte?

An outcome is a set of 3 lattes. Swapping two of the drinks gives the same set, so by the swap test of lecture 6.3 order does not matter, and the sets are counted with a combination. From lecture 6.3, C(n, k), read “n choose k”, is the number of ways to choose k different items from n when order does not matter:

C(n, k) = P(n, k)k!

Here P(n, k) = n × (n − 1) × … is the product of k factors counting downward from n, and k! = k × (k − 1) × … × 1.

Some scientific calculators have functions marked nPr and nCr. A phone calculator usually does not; type the product with the bottom in parentheses, as in lecture 6.3: 12 × 11 × 10 ÷ (3 × 2 × 1). In a spreadsheet, =COMBIN(12,3) gives the same number.

Step 1: count the sample space. The number of possible sets is C(12, 3) = (12 × 11 × 10)/(3 × 2 × 1) = 1320/6 = 220, the same count as the 220 sets of 3 fall drinks in lecture 6.3. That is too many to list. Each of the 220 sets is equally likely, because the drinks are chosen at random.

Step 2: count the event. A set that includes the Pumpkin Spice Latte has that drink plus 2 of the other 11 lattes. There are C(11, 2) = (11 × 10)/(2 × 1) = 55 such sets.

Step 3: divide. P(set includes Pumpkin Spice Latte) = 55/220 = 1/4 = 25%.

The answer makes sense: the set holds 3 of the 12 lattes, and 3/12 = 1/4.

Counting ordered lists gives the same answer, as long as the top and the bottom of the fraction both count ordered lists. Of the P(12, 3) = 12 × 11 × 10 = 1320 ordered lists of 3 lattes, 3 × 11 × 10 = 330 include the Pumpkin Spice Latte (it can be in any of the 3 places), and 330/1320 = 1/4.

The complement rule gives a check. The sets that do not include the Pumpkin Spice Latte choose all 3 drinks from the other 11: C(11, 3) = 165. So P(no Pumpkin Spice Latte) = 165/220 = 3/4, and 1 − 3/4 = 1/4.

Task 5 (about 5 minutes, calculator)

Now a set of 3 different drinks is chosen at random from all 31 hot drinks in Section 1.

  1. How many different sets of 3 drinks are possible?
  2. What is the probability that the set includes the Caffè Latte? Give a fraction and a percentage rounded to 1 decimal place.
  3. What is the probability that the set contains at least one cortado? (Hint: the complement of “at least one cortado” is “no cortado”.)
Solution to Task 5
  1. C(31, 3) = (31 × 30 × 29)/(3 × 2 × 1) = 26970/6 = 4495 sets.
  2. Sets with the Caffè Latte: the Caffè Latte plus 2 of the other 30 drinks, C(30, 2) = (30 × 29)/2 = 435. P = 435/4495 = 3/31 ≈ 9.7%. Check: the set holds 3 of the 31 drinks, and 3/31 ≈ 9.7%.
  3. There are 31 − 4 = 27 drinks that are not cortados. Sets with no cortado choose all 3 drinks from these 27: C(27, 3) = (27 × 26 × 25)/6 = 2925. P(no cortado) = 2925/4495 ≈ 0.651. By the complement rule, P(at least one cortado) = 1 − 2925/4495 = 1570/4495 ≈ 0.349 = 34.9%.

Counting “at least one cortado” directly needs three separate counts: sets with exactly 1 cortado (4 × C(27, 2) = 4 × 351 = 1404), exactly 2 (C(4, 2) × 27 = 6 × 27 = 162) and exactly 3 (C(4, 3) = 4). These add to 1570, the same as the complement gives in one step.

In the long-run meaning of Section 3: if many groups each bought 3 random hot drinks, about 35 of every 100 groups would get at least one cortado.
