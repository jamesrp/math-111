In this module the county plans a road network for these towns as if no roads joined them yet. That starting point is made up so that the planning question is open.

The county's engineers list every pair of towns that are less than 25 miles apart in a straight line as a possible road segment. The 25-mile cutoff is also made up. Eight pairs qualify. Their straight-line distances were computed from the latitudes and longitudes above, treating the Earth as a sphere. A real mountain road follows valleys and climbs passes, so it is longer than the straight line. Using the straight-line distance is a simplification.

For the cost, the Federal Highway Administration (FHWA) uses a typical cost of $5,474 thousand per lane-mile, in 2014 dollars, for adding road on a new alignment to a rural road of the class it calls a "major collector", in mountainous terrain [7]. Major collector is the least expensive of the four rural road classes in that FHWA table. This module uses the figure as the cost of building a brand-new road, a second simplification. A lane-mile is one traffic lane, one mile long. A road with one lane in each direction has 2 lanes, so one mile of it is 2 lane-miles. FHWA states that real projects vary a lot around this typical value [7]. Using one cost per mile for every segment is a third simplification.

The figure comes from the 23rd edition of FHWA's report to Congress on the nation's highways, sent in 2019, and it is in 2014 dollars. FHWA has published two newer editions since [8]. The costs here come from an older report in 2014 dollars, so the same roads would cost more today. Every cost in this module is in millions of 2014 dollars.

Solution to Task 1

(a) $5,474 thousand is $5.474 million. Two lanes cost 2 × 5.474 = $10.948 million per mile.

(b) Each cost is distance × 10.948, rounded to the nearest million.

(c) 145 + 223 + 86 + 224 + 239 + 261 + 270 + 82 = $1,530 million, about $1.5 billion.

The county does not need all eight segments to join the six towns. The planning question for this module is which segments to build. A list of eight rows makes that hard to see. For example, the table alone does not make it easy to tell whether a driver could get from Mazama to Brewster using only some of the segments. A drawing helps.

2. Drawing the map as a graph

In Figure 1 each town is a dot placed by its latitude and longitude, and each possible segment is a straight line between two dots. Use it to find which towns have a segment to Twisp.

A graph is a list of objects called vertices (one of them is a vertex) together with a list of pairs of vertices called edges. We draw each vertex as a dot and each edge as a line joining its two vertices.

In Figure 1 the vertices are the six towns and the edges are the eight possible road segments. This graph has 6 vertices and 8 edges.

A graph records only which pairs of vertices are joined by an edge. Where the dots sit on the page is not part of the graph. Figure 2 is the same graph with the towns placed around a circle in a mixed-up order. Check one edge from the table, for example Twisp–Brewster, in both figures.

Going from the map to the graph loses where the towns are: their distances, their directions from each other, and the mountains between them. In Figure 2 Mazama and Pateros are drawn next to each other, though they are about 44 miles apart. What the graph keeps is the list of which towns a segment would join. Since the length of a drawn edge no longer means anything, the distance or the cost has to be written on the edge as a number.

3. Weighted graphs

A weighted graph is a graph with a number, called a weight, written on each edge.

In the town graph the weight of an edge is the cost of building that segment, in millions of 2014 dollars. The edge Winthrop–Twisp has weight 86.

Each row of the segment table in Section 1 is one edge of the town graph, and its cost is the weight of that edge. In other problems a weight could be a distance, a travel time or a fare.

Tap or click an edge in Figure 3 to remove it from the road plan; tap it again to put it back. Removed edges turn into light dashed lines. The line under the figure reports the number of roads in the plan, their total cost, and whether the plan is connected and has a cycle. Those two words are defined in Section 4. You use Figure 3 in Tasks 3, 4 and 6. Each task can also be done on paper.

4. Paths, cycles and connected graphs

A path is a route from one vertex to another along edges that never visits a vertex twice. A path is written as its list of vertices, for example Mazama → Twisp → Pateros. The cost of a path is the sum of the weights of its edges: 223 + 261 = 484.

A cycle is a route along edges that starts and ends at the same vertex, uses at least 3 edges, and visits no other vertex twice. For example, Twisp → Pateros → Brewster → Twisp is a cycle. Its cost is 261 + 82 + 270 = 613.

A graph is connected if there is a path between every pair of its vertices.

The graph with all 8 edges is connected: any town can reach any other. For a road plan, being connected means a driver can get from every town to every other town using only the roads in the plan. If the county built only Winthrop–Twisp and Pateros–Brewster, the plan would not be connected. There would be no path from Mazama to anywhere.

5. Removing roads from a connected plan

The county wants a plan that is connected and costs less than $1,530 million. Some of the 8 edges can be removed while the plan stays connected.

An edge that lies on a cycle can be removed, and the graph stays connected. A trip that used that edge can go the other way around the cycle instead. If the new route visits a town twice, skip the loop between the two visits; what is left is a path. For example, Twisp–Brewster is on the cycle Twisp → Pateros → Brewster → Twisp. After removing it, a driver from Twisp to Brewster goes Twisp → Pateros → Brewster.

Task 4 (about 6 minutes, Figure 3 or paper)

Start with all 8 roads. Remove roads one at a time. Each time, remove only a road that lies on a cycle of the roads still in the plan. Stop when no road in the plan lies on a cycle.

Write down: the roads you removed, in order; the number of roads left; the total cost of the roads left. Then compare with a neighbor.

Different orders give different plans, but every plan ends with 5 roads. Two examples:

Order A keeps Mazama–Winthrop, Mazama–Twisp, Twisp–Conconully, Twisp–Pateros and Twisp–Brewster. Order B keeps Mazama–Winthrop, Winthrop–Twisp, Twisp–Conconully, Twisp–Brewster and Pateros–Brewster. Both plans are connected, and Order A costs $316 million more than Order B.

When no road in the plan lies on a cycle, removing any one of the remaining roads would disconnect the plan. The reason is an argument by contradiction, as in week 9. Suppose a road between towns A and B is on no cycle, and suppose the plan stays connected after it is removed. Then some other path joins A to B. That path, followed by the road from B back to A, is a cycle containing the road. This contradicts the road being on no cycle. So a road on no cycle cannot be removed without cutting some town off.

6. Trees and the number of their edges

A tree is a connected graph with no cycles.

Each plan you ended with in Task 4 is a tree. It is connected, because you removed only roads on a cycle. It has no cycle, because you stopped when no road was on a cycle.

(c) The number of edges is one less than the number of vertices each time. The prediction is 5 edges for 6 vertices and 19 edges for 20 vertices. The six-town trees from Task 4 had 5 roads, which agrees.

The pattern holds for every tree: a tree with n vertices has n − 1 edges.

Step 1: every tree with at least 2 vertices has a vertex with exactly one edge. Call such a vertex a leaf. To find one, start at any vertex and walk along edges, never going straight back along the edge you just used. In a tree you can never return to a vertex you already visited, because that would close a cycle. There are only finitely many vertices, so the walk must stop. It stops at a vertex where the only edge is the one you arrived on. That vertex is a leaf.

Step 2: remove a leaf and its one edge. What remains is still a tree. It has no cycles, because removing things cannot create a cycle. It is still connected, because a path between two other vertices never passes through a leaf: passing through a vertex uses two of its edges, and a leaf has only one. The new tree has one vertex fewer and one edge fewer.

Step 3: repeat. Keep removing leaves until one vertex is left. A tree with one vertex has 0 edges, since an edge needs two vertices. Each removal took away exactly one vertex and one edge. Starting from n vertices, you made n − 1 removals to get down to 1 vertex, so you removed n − 1 edges. Since 0 edges remain, the tree had n − 1 edges at the start.

For the six towns, every connected road plan with no cycle has exactly 6 − 1 = 5 roads. A computer check of all the ways to choose 5 of the 8 segments finds 24 such plans.

7. Connecting every town as cheaply as possible

The county's question, stated for a graph:

In a connected weighted graph, choose some of the edges so that every vertex is connected to every other, and the total weight of the chosen edges is as small as possible.

When every edge has a positive weight, as here, the cheapest plan is always a tree. The argument is by contradiction. Suppose the cheapest connected plan had a cycle. Remove one edge of that cycle. By Section 5 the plan is still connected, and it costs less, because every road has a positive cost. That contradicts the plan being the cheapest. So the cheapest plan has no cycle, which makes it a tree, and for six towns it has 5 roads.

Task 6 (about 3 minutes, Figure 3 or paper)

In 3 minutes, find the cheapest connected plan you can for the six towns. Write down its 5 roads and its total cost. Then compare totals with your neighbors and write down the lowest total any of you found.

Solution to Task 6

Any plan with 5 roads that is connected and has no cycle is a valid answer. In Figure 3 the status line reads "Roads in plan: 5", "Connected: yes" and "Has a cycle: no". The 24 possible plans have a range of totals; Order B from Task 4 gives one of them, at $822 million.

The lowest total you found is the record to beat. Lecture 10.2 gives a method that finds the cheapest plan, and you can then check your record against it.

Trying plans one at a time works for a map this small. It stops working quickly as the map grows. If every pair of the six towns were a possible segment (15 segments), there would be 1,296 trees to check. With 20 towns and every pair possible there would be about 2.6 × 1023. Both counts come from Cayley's formula, which says that n towns with every pair possible have nn−2 trees: 64 = 1,296 and 2018 ≈ 2.6 × 1023. A computer that checks a million trees each second would need about 8 billion years to check every tree for 20 towns.
