When greedy algorithms work

1. The six towns and their minimum spanning tree

The map is the one from Lectures 1 and 2: six towns in Okanogan County, Washington [1]–[6], and eight candidate road segments, one for each pair of towns less than 25 miles apart in a straight line. Each cost is the straight-line distance times $10.948 million per mile, the FHWA typical cost for a two-lane rural road on a new alignment in mountainous terrain [7], rounded to the nearest million. All costs are in millions of 2014 dollars. The simplifications named in Lecture 10.1 still apply: straight-line distances, one cost per mile for every segment, no existing roads, and a made-up 25-mile cutoff.

In Lecture 10.2, Kruskal's algorithm went down the list of edges from cheapest to most expensive. It built each edge whose two towns were not yet joined by a path of built edges, skipped each edge that would have closed a cycle, and stopped at 6 − 1 = 5 edges.

A computer check in Lecture 10.2 added up all 24 spanning trees of this graph. Only Kruskal's tree costs $798 million, and the next cheapest costs $807 million. That check covers this one map. Kruskal's algorithm takes the cheapest edge it can at every step and ends with the cheapest tree. Two everyday problems have a rule of the same kind, which takes the option that looks best at each step:

  • Driving from one town to another: at each town, drive to the nearest town you have not visited yet.
  • Making change: hand over the largest coin that does not go over the amount still owed, and repeat.

Whether a rule like this gives the best answer depends on the problem.

2. Greedy algorithms

A greedy algorithm builds an answer one step at a time. At each step it takes the option that looks best right now, and it never goes back to change an earlier step. Kruskal's algorithm is greedy: each step builds one edge, and the option that looks best is the cheapest edge that does not close a cycle. Prim's algorithm from Lecture 10.2 is greedy too.

ProblemOne stepGreedy choice at each step
Minimum spanning tree (Kruskal)Build one edgeThe cheapest edge that does not close a cycle
Minimum spanning tree (Prim)Build one edgeThe cheapest edge with one town in the group and one outside
Shortest drive from one town to anotherDrive to one more townThe nearest town, along a road, that you have not visited yet
Change for an amount, with as few coins as possibleHand over one coinThe largest coin that is not more than the amount still owed

Greedy algorithms are quick to follow by hand. For each problem, either an argument shows that the greedy rule always gives the best answer, or one example shows that it can fail.

3. Why each road Kruskal's algorithm builds is safe

In this section a group is a set of towns with at least one town but not all six. An edge leaves the group if one of its towns is inside the group and the other is outside. Prim's algorithm in Lecture 10.2 used the same idea: at each step it built the cheapest edge leaving its group. For example, with the group {Mazama, Winthrop, Twisp}, the edge Twisp–Pateros leaves the group, and the edge Mazama–Winthrop is inside it.

An edge is safe if some minimum spanning tree contains it. On this map no two costs are equal and there is only one minimum spanning tree, so the safe edges are the five roads of Figure 1.

Network N is a spanning tree with five roads: Pateros–Brewster, Winthrop–Twisp, Mazama–Winthrop, Twisp–Conconully and Twisp–Pateros. It does not contain Winthrop–Conconully, the cheapest edge leaving the group {Mazama, Winthrop, Twisp}. Task 2 turns N into a tree that does contain it.

Task 2 (about 5 minutes, paper and calculator)

  1. Add up the costs of the five roads of network N.
  2. Add the edge Winthrop–Conconully to N. This closes a cycle. Write the towns of the cycle in order.
  3. On that cycle, find the edge other than Winthrop–Conconully that leaves the group {Mazama, Winthrop, Twisp}. Remove it. Write the new total, and say which spanning tree you now have.
Solution to Task 2

(a) 82 + 86 + 145 + 239 + 261 = $813 million.

(b) In N, the path from Winthrop to Conconully is Winthrop → Twisp → Conconully. With the new edge the cycle is Winthrop → Conconully → Twisp → Winthrop.

(c) The cycle has three edges. Winthrop–Conconully leaves the group. Twisp–Conconully also leaves it, since Twisp is inside and Conconully is outside. Winthrop–Twisp is inside the group. Remove Twisp–Conconully ($239 million). The new total is 813 + 224 − 239 = $798 million. Every town is still connected: a trip that used Twisp–Conconully now goes Twisp → Winthrop → Conconully. The five roads are the minimum spanning tree of Figure 1. This is the swap of Lecture 10.2, Task 4(a), run backwards.

The safe-edge rule on any map (continuation)

Continuation, for reading after class or if time allows. Tasks 1 and 2 are the core of this section: they show the idea on this map.

Task 2 is one case of an argument that works on every map. Take any group of towns. Let e be a cheapest edge leaving the group, and let W be any minimum spanning tree.

  1. If W contains e, then e is safe.
  2. If W does not contain e, add e to W. W is connected, so a path in W already joins the two towns of e. That path and e form a cycle.
  3. Go around the cycle, starting from the town of e inside the group. Crossing e takes you outside the group. To get back to the start you must cross into the group again, along another edge f of the cycle. So f also leaves the group, and f is in W.
  4. Since e is a cheapest edge leaving the group, f costs at least as much as e.
  5. Remove f. This is a swap from Lecture 10.2, so the result is again a spanning tree. Its total is (total of W) + (cost of e) − (cost of f), which is at most the total of W.
  6. W is a minimum spanning tree, so the new tree cannot cost less. It costs the same, so it is also a minimum spanning tree, and it contains e. So e is safe.

Why Kruskal's tree is a minimum spanning tree on every map (continuation)

Continuation, for reading after class or if time allows.

In Kruskal's algorithm a group is a set of towns joined to each other by the edges built so far, as in Lecture 10.2. At each step the algorithm builds an edge whose two towns are in different groups. Take the group of one of those two towns. Every cheaper edge was looked at earlier and was either built or skipped. Either way its two towns are now in the same group, so it does not leave this group. The edge being built is therefore a cheapest edge leaving the group, and by the safe-edge rule it is safe.

On the map, row 5 builds Winthrop–Conconully. Just before it, Mazama, Winthrop and Twisp form one group, and Task 1 found that Winthrop–Conconully is the cheapest edge leaving that group.

Each built edge is safe. The argument must also show that all the built edges fit in one minimum spanning tree, and it goes step by step.

  • Before the first step no edges are built, so every minimum spanning tree contains all of them.
  • Suppose that after some step a minimum spanning tree W contains every edge built so far, and the next step builds e. Use this W in the argument above. If W does not contain e, the removed edge f leaves the group. Every edge built so far has both towns in one group, so it does not leave the group, and f is not a built edge. The new minimum spanning tree contains e and every edge built before it.

So after every step some minimum spanning tree contains all the edges built so far. After the last step the algorithm has built n − 1 edges. A minimum spanning tree has exactly n − 1 edges, so the one that contains them has no others. Kruskal's tree is that minimum spanning tree.

Why the swap test works (continuation)

Continuation, for reading after class or if time allows.

Lecture 10.2 used this fact from the literature [9]: if no single swap lowers the total of a spanning tree, that tree is a minimum spanning tree. Here is the argument when no two edges cost the same, as on this map. It is a proof by contradiction, as in week 9.

Suppose T is a spanning tree that no swap improves, and suppose some spanning tree costs less than T. Let M be a minimum spanning tree. M costs less than T, so M is a different tree, and M contains some edge e that T does not.

  1. Remove e from M. M has no cycle, so no other path in M joins the two towns of e. The towns split into two groups: the towns still joined to one town of e, and the towns joined to the other. No edge of M other than e leaves these groups.
  2. Add e to T. It closes a cycle with the path in T between the two towns of e. That path starts in one group and ends in the other, so some edge f on it leaves the group. The edge f is in T, and by step 1 it is not in M.
  3. No swap improves T, so adding e to T and removing f does not lower the total: f costs no more than e. No two costs are equal, so f costs less than e.
  4. Put f into M in place of e. The edge f joins the two groups, so every town is connected again. There is no cycle, because a cycle would have to cross between the two groups and come back, which needs two edges between them, and f is the only one. So this is a spanning tree, and its total is (total of M) − (cost of e) + (cost of f), which is less than the total of M.
  5. That contradicts M being a minimum spanning tree. So no spanning tree costs less than T.

The fact also holds when some edges cost the same, with a longer argument [9].

4. The shortest drive between two towns

For this section, suppose all eight candidate roads have been built, so a driver can use any of them. This is made up; the county's plan has five roads. The length of each road is its straight-line distance from Section 1.

Nearest-next-town rule. Start at the starting town. From the town you are in, look at the roads that lead to towns you have not visited yet, and drive along the shortest of them. Stop when you reach the destination. If every road from the town you are in leads to a town you have already visited, the rule is stuck and gives no route.

Task 3 (about 4 minutes, paper)

  1. Use Figure 5. Follow the nearest-next-town rule from Pateros to Twisp. Write the route and its length in miles. Then find a shorter route from Pateros to Twisp and write its length.
  2. Follow the rule from Twisp to Pateros. Write the towns you visit, in order, and what happens.
Solution to Task 3

(a) At Pateros the roads go to Brewster (7.5 miles) and Twisp (23.8 miles). Drive to Brewster. At Brewster the only road to a town not yet visited goes to Twisp (24.7 miles). Drive to Twisp and stop.

Greedy route: Pateros → Brewster → Twisp, 7.5 + 24.7 = 32.2 miles. Shorter route: the road Pateros–Twisp, 23.8 miles, which is 32.2 − 23.8 = 8.4 miles shorter. These are the only two paths from Pateros to Twisp, so 23.8 miles is the shortest.

(b) At Twisp the roads go to Winthrop (7.9), Mazama (20.4), Conconully (21.8), Pateros (23.8) and Brewster (24.7). Drive to Winthrop. At Winthrop the roads to towns not yet visited go to Mazama (13.2) and Conconully (20.5). Drive to Mazama. At Mazama both roads lead to towns already visited, Winthrop and Twisp. The rule is stuck at Mazama and never reaches Pateros, though a road from Twisp to Pateros exists.

At Pateros the rule compares only the next road: 7.5 miles to Brewster is less than 23.8 miles to Twisp. The road from Brewster to Twisp is 24.7 miles, so that first choice leads to a 32.2-mile trip. A choice that is shortest for one step can make the whole trip longer, or lead to a town with no way forward. A computer check of all 30 start-and-destination pairs on this map finds that the rule gives a shortest route for 6 pairs, a longer route for 12 and gets stuck for 12.

The Pateros-to-Twisp drive settles the question: the nearest-next-town rule does not always find the shortest route. A method that does, when no road length is negative, was published by Edsger Dijkstra in 1959 [8]. It is also greedy. At each step it picks, among the towns it has not finished, the one with the shortest route from the start found so far. The distance it compares is the total distance from the start.

5. Making change with coins

U.S. coins in everyday use are worth 1, 5, 10 and 25 cents. A cashier making change can follow the greedy rule.

Greedy change rule. Hand over the largest coin that is not more than the amount still owed. Subtract it. Repeat until nothing is owed.

The goal is to use as few coins as possible.

Task 4 (about 4 minutes, paper)

  1. Use the greedy change rule with U.S. coins to make 68 cents. List the coins and count them.
  2. Now use a made-up coin system with coins worth 1, 3 and 4 cents. Use the greedy change rule to make 6 cents. List the coins and count them. Then find a way to make 6 cents with fewer coins.
Solution to Task 4

(a) 68 → hand 25, 43 left → hand 25, 18 left → hand 10, 8 left → hand 5, 3 left → hand 1, 2 left → hand 1, 1 left → hand 1. Coins: 25 + 25 + 10 + 5 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 68, which is 7 coins.

(b) 6 → hand 4, 2 left → hand 1, 1 left → hand 1. Coins: 4 + 1 + 1, which is 3 coins. Two 3-cent coins make 3 + 3 = 6 with only 2 coins. The greedy rule does not give the fewest coins here.

Why greedy change works for U.S. coins (continuation)

Continuation, for reading after class or if time allows. Task 4 and the change checker below are the core of this section.

Take any way of making an amount with the fewest U.S. coins. It cannot contain any of these, because each could be traded for fewer coins of the same total:

  • 5 pennies (trade for 1 nickel),
  • 2 nickels (trade for 1 dime),
  • 3 dimes (trade for 1 quarter and 1 nickel: 2 coins instead of 3),
  • 2 dimes and a nickel together (trade for 1 quarter).

So in a fewest-coins answer there are at most 4 pennies, at most 1 nickel and at most 2 dimes, and not 2 dimes together with a nickel. The pennies, nickels and dimes together are then worth at most 24 cents (2 dimes and 4 pennies: 2 × 10 + 4 = 24). If a fewest-coins answer used fewer quarters than fit, the other coins would have to make up at least 25 cents. So a fewest-coins answer uses as many quarters as fit, which is what the greedy rule does. The same reasoning with the bounds 9 cents (1 nickel and 4 pennies) and 4 cents (4 pennies) shows it also uses as many dimes, and then nickels, as fit. So the greedy rule always gives the fewest U.S. coins.

For the made-up system with coins 1, 3 and 4, no such argument exists, because Task 4 found an amount, 6 cents, where greedy fails. The change checker finds every such amount in a range. For each amount it lists the greedy coins and the fewest possible coins, found by computer, and shades the rows where greedy uses more coins.

  1. Enter coins 1, 3, 4. List every amount from 1 to 20 cents where greedy uses more coins than the fewest possible.
  2. Change the coins to 1, 10, 25. This is the U.S. system with the nickel taken out (made up). Check amounts from 1 to 40 cents. Write down the smallest amount where greedy fails, the greedy coins for it, and the fewest coins for it.
Solution to Task 5

(a) 6, 10, 14 and 18 cents. For example, at 10 cents greedy gives 4 + 4 + 1 + 1 (4 coins) and the fewest is 4 + 3 + 3 (3 coins). Each failing amount is 4 more than the one before: adding a 4-cent coin to a failing amount gives another failing amount.

(b) 30 cents. Greedy gives 25 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1, which is 6 coins. Three dimes, 10 + 10 + 10, use 3 coins. Without a nickel, the trade of 3 dimes for a quarter and a nickel is not available, so the argument for U.S. coins breaks down.

6. When a counterexample settles a question

Each question here has the form "Does this greedy rule always give the best answer?" A counterexample is one specific case where the rule does not give the best answer. One correct counterexample settles the question: the answer is no. The 6-cent example settles it for coins 1, 3, 4. The Pateros-to-Twisp drive settles it for the nearest-next-town rule.

A proposed counterexample settles the question only when all of these checks pass:

  1. It is a case of the question asked. It uses only the coins of the coin system in question, or only roads on the stated map.
  2. The rule was followed exactly. Every step of the greedy answer is the step the rule says to take.
  3. A better answer is written down and checked. The other answer is valid (the coins add up to the amount, the route joins the two towns along roads, the roads form a spanning tree), and it is strictly better (fewer coins, fewer miles, lower cost).

Examples where a rule works never settle an "always" question in the other direction. The computer check of all 24 spanning trees covers only this map. A computer check of every amount from 1 cent to $10.00 finds no amount where greedy change fails for U.S. coins, but it covers only those amounts. The arguments in Sections 3 and 5 cover every map and every amount.

Task 6 (about 4 minutes, paper)

For each claim, write "settles it" or "does not settle it". If it does not, name a check from the list above that fails.

  1. "Greedy change fails for U.S. coins. For 30 cents greedy gives 25 + 5, but 10 + 10 + 10 also makes 30 cents."
  2. "The nearest-next-town rule fails. From Brewster to Twisp it goes Brewster → Pateros → Twisp, 7.5 + 23.8 = 31.3 miles, but the road Brewster–Twisp is 24.7 miles."
  3. "Kruskal's algorithm fails on the six towns. It builds Pateros–Brewster, Winthrop–Twisp, Mazama–Winthrop, Mazama–Twisp and Winthrop–Conconully, for $760 million, which is less than $798 million."
  4. "Greedy change fails for U.S. coins. For 40 cents greedy gives 25 + 10 + 5, which is 3 coins, but two 20-cent coins make 40 cents."
Solution to Task 6

(a) Does not settle it. Check 3 fails: the other answer uses 3 coins and greedy uses 2, so the other answer is worse.

(b) Settles it. At Brewster the roads go to Pateros (7.5 miles) and Twisp (24.7 miles), so the rule drives to Pateros, and from Pateros the only road to a town not yet visited goes to Twisp. The road Brewster–Twisp exists and is shorter.

(c) Does not settle it. Check 2 fails: Kruskal's algorithm skips Mazama–Twisp, because Mazama and Twisp are already joined through Winthrop. Check 3 fails too: the five roads contain the cycle Mazama → Winthrop → Twisp → Mazama and do not reach Pateros or Brewster from the other towns, so they are not a spanning tree.

(d) Does not settle it. Check 1 fails: there is no 20-cent coin among U.S. coins in everyday use. With the coins 1, 5, 10 and 25, 40 cents takes at least 3 coins.
