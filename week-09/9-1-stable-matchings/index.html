Stable matchings

1. The residency Match

A new U.S. doctor finishes medical school and then trains for several more years as a resident in a hospital program. Applicants and programs are paired up by the National Resident Matching Program (NRMP). Each applicant sends a ranked list of programs, from most wanted to least wanted. Each program sends a ranked list of applicants. A computer program then turns all these lists into one assignment.

The NRMP reported these figures for its 2026 Main Residency Match [1, 2].

2026 Main Residency Match [1, 2]

Applicants who registered53,373

Positions filled (PGY-1 and PGY-2)41,482

PGY-1 means post-graduate year 1, the first year of residency. Some specialties start in the second year (PGY-2) and require a one-year preliminary position first. One person can match to both: a PGY-1 preliminary position and a PGY-2 position that starts a year later. So the 41,482 filled positions count some people twice, and the number of people who matched is smaller than 41,482 [2]. Each person can hold at most one PGY-1 position, so the 38,354 PGY-1 matches are 38,354 different people [1].

Task 1 (about 3 minutes, calculator)

Use the 2026 table. Write down four numbers.

  1. The percent of offered positions that were filled, to one decimal place.
  2. The percent of active applicants who matched to a PGY-1 position, to one decimal place.
  3. The number of positions left unfilled.
  4. The number of active applicants who did not match to a PGY-1 position.
Solution to Task 1
  1. 41,482 ÷ 44,344 = 0.9355…, so about 93.5% of positions were filled. The NRMP reports the same figure, 93.5 percent [1].
  2. 38,354 ÷ 48,050 = 0.7982…, so about 79.8% of active applicants matched to a PGY-1 position.
  3. 44,344 − 41,482 = 2,862 positions unfilled. The NRMP reports the same number [1].
  4. 48,050 − 38,354 = 9,696 active applicants did not match to a PGY-1 position. A few of them may have matched to a PGY-2 position only, so this is not quite the number of people with no match at all.

Both sides have some left over. There are more active applicants (48,050) than positions (44,344), so some applicants cannot get a position. Some positions stay empty as well, because an applicant can only match to a program that is on their list and that has them on its list.

With 41,482 positions filled by a computer, people need a reason to accept the result. One reason is this: after the assignment, no applicant and program should both want to drop their assigned partners and go with each other instead. An assignment with this property is called stable. Alvin Roth, who studied the Match in 1984, hypothesized that "the fundamental reason for the success of the NRMP was that it produced stable matches" [4]. The algorithm the NRMP uses today was designed around this idea [4, 5].

Simplifications in the small examples. The examples are made up. Applicants are named A, B, C, D and programs W, X, Y, Z. Each example has the same number of applicants and programs, each program has exactly one position, and everyone ranks everyone on the other side. In the real Match, programs have several positions, the two sides have different sizes, lists usually include only some of the other side, one person can match to a PGY-1 and a PGY-2 position, and couples can apply together [4]. The facts about stable matchings in this module are for the simple case without couples.

To read the tables, find a person's row and read left to right. Row B of the applicants' table says B wants Z most, then X, then Y. Row Y of the programs' table says program Y wants C most, then B, then A.

A person prefers one choice to another when the first choice is further left in their row. B prefers X to Y because X is B's 2nd choice and Y is B's 3rd choice.

Task 2 (about 3 minutes, pencil and paper)

Use the Example 1 tables. Suppose the assignment is: A goes to X, B goes to Y, C goes to Z. Find an applicant and a program who are not assigned to each other but who would both rather be together than with the partner they were given. Write down the applicant, the program, and the two table rows that show it.

Solution to Task 2

C and Y. C was given Z. C's row is Y, Z, X, so C would rather have Y than Z. Y was given B. Y's row is C, B, A, so Y would rather have C than B.

No other applicant and program work. A has X, A's 1st choice, so A wants no change. B would rather have Z or X than Y, but Z's row is C, A, B, so Z would rather keep C, and X's row is A, C, B, so X would rather keep A. The only program C would rather have than Z is Y.

3. Matchings and blocking pairs

A matching is a set of applicant–program pairs in which every applicant is in exactly one pair and every program is in exactly one pair. A person's partner is the one they are paired with.

We write a pair as (applicant, program) and give each matching a name. The assignment in Task 2 is the matching

M1 = {(A, X), (B, Y), (C, Z)}.

In M1, A's partner is X, B's partner is Y and C's partner is Z. A got their 1st choice, B their 3rd choice and C their 2nd choice.

In Task 2, C and Y both want to leave M1 to go together. If they were free to make their own deal, both would gain by ignoring M1. A pair like this has a name.

An applicant and a program that are not paired with each other form a blocking pair when both of these hold:

  1. the applicant prefers the program to the applicant's own partner, and
  2. the program prefers the applicant to the program's own partner.

So (C, Y) is a blocking pair of M1. Both conditions are needed. Take B and Z in M1. B's row is Z, X, Y and B's partner is Y, so B prefers Z to Y. Condition 1 holds. Z's partner is C, and Z's row is C, A, B, so Z prefers C to B. Condition 2 fails. Z will not leave C for B, so (B, Z) is not a blocking pair.

Task 3 (about 4 minutes, pencil and paper)

Use the Example 1 tables and this matching: A goes to Y, B goes to X, C goes to Z.

  1. Write this matching as a set of pairs. Call it M2.
  2. For each of the pairs (A, X), (C, Y) and (B, Z), write the two comparisons from the definition and then write "blocking" or "not blocking".
Solution to Task 3

(a) M2 = {(A, Y), (B, X), (C, Z)}.

(b)

  • (A, X). A's partner is Y; A's row is X, Y, Z, so A prefers X to Y. X's partner is B; X's row is A, C, B, so X prefers A to B. Both hold: blocking.
  • (C, Y). C's partner is Z; C's row is Y, Z, X, so C prefers Y to Z. Y's partner is A; Y's row is C, B, A, so Y prefers C to A. Both hold: blocking.
  • (B, Z). B's partner is X; B's row is Z, X, Y, so B prefers Z to X. Z's partner is C; Z's row is C, A, B, so Z prefers C to B. Condition 2 fails: not blocking.

4. Stable matchings and how to check one

A matching is stable when it has no blocking pair. It is unstable when it has at least one blocking pair.

To show that a matching is unstable, one blocking pair is enough. To show that a matching is stable, we must rule out every applicant–program pair. A fixed order of work makes sure no pair is missed.

The checking method

Go through the applicants one at a time. For each applicant:

  1. Find the applicant's partner in the applicant's row. Only the programs to the left of the partner can be in a blocking pair with this applicant, because condition 1 needs the applicant to prefer the program. Programs to the right are skipped.
  2. For each program to the left of the partner, find that program's row. If the applicant is to the left of the program's current partner, the two form a blocking pair. If not, they do not.

Each step 2 comparison is one check. An applicant who got their 1st choice needs no checks at all.

Worked example: checking M3

Take the matching A goes to Y, B goes to Z, C goes to X, with the Example 1 tables:

M3 = {(A, Y), (B, Z), (C, X)}.

Applicant (partner, rank)Program to the leftThat program's row and partnerResult
A (Y, 2nd)XX's row A, C, B; partner C. A is left of C.(A, X) blocks
B (Z, 1st)noneB has their 1st choice.0 checks
C (X, 3rd)YY's row C, B, A; partner A. C is left of A.(C, Y) blocks
C (X, 3rd)ZZ's row C, A, B; partner B. C is left of B.(C, Z) blocks

That is 3 checks, and they find 3 blocking pairs. Every other pair was skipped in step 1 for a reason, so there are no others. M3 is unstable.

With 3 applicants, each applicant has at most 2 programs to the left of their partner, so a matching never needs more than 3 × 2 = 6 checks. With n applicants and n programs the limit is n × (n − 1) checks.

A stable matching for Example 1

Fix the blocking pair (C, Y) in M1 by putting C with Y. That leaves B with Z. The result is

M4 = {(A, X), (B, Z), (C, Y)}.

Every applicant has their 1st choice in M4, so step 1 finds no program to the left of any partner. There are 0 checks and no blocking pair: M4 is stable. Program Z ends up with B, its 3rd choice. Z would like A or C instead, but each of them prefers their own partner, so Z cannot form a blocking pair.

Here, fixing one blocking pair gave a stable matching in one step. That happened by luck in this example. With other tables, fixing one blocking pair can create a new one, and fixing pairs one at a time can go round in a circle without ever reaching a stable matching. The method of Gale and Shapley in Section 6 avoids this.

Consider the matching

M5 = {(A, X), (B, W), (C, Y), (D, Z)}.

Three of the four applicants get their 1st choice. One way to score a matching is its rank total: for each of the 8 people, write the rank they gave their partner (1 for 1st choice, 2 for 2nd, and so on), and add the 8 numbers. A lower total means people are, overall, closer to the top of their lists. Of all the matchings in Example 2, M5 has the lowest rank total. (This was found by computer, by listing every matching.)

W and Z each rank C to the right of their partner, so they do not block. X ranks C to the left of its partner A. That is 3 checks, and exactly one blocking pair: (C, X).

(c) No. C prefers X (3rd choice) to Y (4th choice), and X prefers C (its 1st choice) to A (its 2nd choice). Both would leave M5 to go together, so M5 is unstable even though it has the lowest rank total.

M5 gives most people what they want, and still one applicant and one program both gain by leaving it. A low rank total does not make a matching stable.

6. Finding a stable matching for Example 2

Use this checker with the Example 2 tables. Pick a program for each applicant. Once every applicant has a program, the checker shows each person's rank for their partner, the rank total, and every blocking pair.

Task 5 (about 6 minutes, matching checker in this section, then pencil and paper)

Start from M5 and its blocking pair in Task 4. Use the checker to find a matching for Example 2 that has no blocking pair. Then write down

  1. the stable matching as a set of pairs (call it M6),
  2. a check of M6 by hand with the method from Section 4: each check you make, its result, and the number of checks,
  3. the rank total of M6 and how many applicants get their 1st choice.
Solution to Task 5

(a) The blocking pair of M5 is (C, X). Put C with X. Then A has lost X, and the program C left, Y, is free, so put A with Y:

M6 = {(A, Y), (B, W), (C, X), (D, Z)}.

The checker reports no blocking pair.

(b) B and D have their 1st choices: 0 checks. A's partner Y is A's 2nd choice; X is to its left. X's partner is C, whom X ranks 1st, so X does not prefer A: no block. C's partner X is C's 3rd choice; W and Z are to its left. W's partner is B and W's row is D, A, B, C, so W prefers B to C: no block. Z's partner is D, Z's 1st choice: no block. That is 3 checks and no blocking pair, so M6 is stable.

(c) Applicants' ranks: A 2, B 1, C 3, D 1, sum 7. Programs' ranks: W ranks B 3rd, X ranks C 1st, Y ranks A 3rd, Z ranks D 1st, sum 8. Rank total = 7 + 8 = 15. 2 applicants (B and D) get their 1st choice.

A computer check of all the matchings in Example 2 finds that M6 is the only stable one. It has a higher rank total than M5 (15 against 14), and fewer applicants get their 1st choice (2 against 3). In this example, stability costs one point of rank total and one 1st choice. Fixing the one blocking pair happened to work in one step here, as in Example 1.

David Gale and Lloyd Shapley showed that tables like these always have at least one stable matching, and they gave a step-by-step method that always finds one [3]. Alvin Roth showed that the residency match had been using a closely related method, and starting in 1995 he and Elliott Peranson designed a new version, which the NRMP adopted in 1997 [4]. Shapley and Roth shared the 2012 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences "for the theory of stable allocations and the practice of market design" [3]. The NRMP describes its algorithm as "applicant-proposing" [5].

7. Counting matchings

In Task 5 you could have tried every matching until you found a stable one. Whether that is practical depends on how many matchings there are.

Count the matchings with n applicants and n programs by choosing partners in order. A has n programs to choose from. B then has n − 1 programs left, C has n − 2, and so on, until the last applicant has 1 program left. The number of matchings is

n × (n − 1) × (n − 2) × … × 2 × 1 = n!,

the factorial from Lecture 6.3. Example 1 has 3! = 6 matchings and Example 2 has 4! = 24.

Checking 6 or 24 matchings is quick. For larger n, n! grows very fast. To measure how fast, suppose a computer can check one billion (1,000,000,000) matchings per second. That speed is a round number made up for this example.

The NRMP does not search through matchings; its algorithm builds the matching directly from the ranked lists [4, 5].
