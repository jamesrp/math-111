Volume and surface area

1. Cabinets in plan and elevation views

Kitchen cabinets are boxes sold in standard sizes. KraftMaid, a cabinet manufacturer, gives these standard sizes [1]:

  • A base cabinet (on the floor, under the counter) is 34½ inches tall and 24 inches deep.
  • A wall cabinet (hung on the wall above the counter) is 12 inches deep. Wall cabinets have no single standard height; KraftMaid makes them from 12 to 54 inches tall.
  • Both kinds come in widths from 6 to 48 inches, in steps of 3 inches.

Our example is a 10-foot wall (120 inches) with four base cabinets, 36, 30, 24 and 30 inches wide, and four wall cabinets of the same widths above them. The wall cabinets are 30 inches tall, a height inside KraftMaid's range. The left end of the run stands against a side wall; the right end is open. The layout is made up for this lecture; the cabinet sizes are real.

Three questions about this run of cabinets: how much storage it gives, how much paint covers it, and why a cabinet twice as wide holds twice as much while a cabinet twice as big in every direction holds eight times as much.

Two standard drawings describe a cabinet. A plan view is the drawing you see looking straight down from above. An elevation view is the drawing you see looking straight at the front. A box has three measurements: width (left to right), depth (front to back) and height (floor to top). Each view shows two of them. Both views show the width; only the plan shows the depth; only the elevation shows the height.

2. Volume of one cabinet

The volume of a box is the amount of space inside it. It is measured by counting cubes. A cubic inch (in3) is a cube 1 inch on each side.

Take the 30-inch base cabinet: 30 in wide, 24 in deep, 34½ in tall. The bottom of the box is a rectangle 30 in by 24 in, so one layer of 1-inch cubes on the bottom holds 30 × 24 = 720 cubes. The box is 34 full layers and a half layer tall, so it holds

720 × 34.5 = 24,840 cubic inches.

The general rule is

volume of a box = width × depth × height.

The order of the three numbers does not matter. On a calculator, type 30 × 24 × 34.5.

We treat each cabinet as a closed box and use its outside measurements. This is a simplification. The space you can fill is smaller, because the side panels, shelves and the recessed strip at the floor (the toe kick) take up some of it. The volumes in this lecture are the size of the box, so they are an upper limit on the storage.

3. Cubic inches and cubic feet

A number like 24,840 cubic inches is hard to picture. Volumes this size are easier to picture in cubic feet (ft3): a cube 1 foot on each side.

One foot is 12 inches. A cube 1 foot on each side is 12 inches wide, 12 inches deep and 12 inches tall, so it holds

12 × 12 × 12 = 1,728 cubic inches.

So 1 ft3 = 1,728 in3. In lecture 5.1 the same reasoning gave 1 ft2 = 12 × 12 = 144 in2. For volume there is a third factor of 12.

To convert, multiply by a fraction equal to 1, as in week 1:

24,840 in3 × (1 ft3 / 1,728 in3) = 24,840 ÷ 1,728 = 14.375 ft3.

Check by converting each measurement to feet first: 30 in = 2.5 ft, 24 in = 2 ft, 34.5 in = 2.875 ft, and 2.5 × 2 × 2.875 = 14.375 ft3. The two methods agree.

A common mistake is to divide by 144, the factor for square feet, which gives 172.5. Estimate the size of the answer to catch it: the cabinet is about 2½ ft by 2 ft by 3 ft, so its volume is about 15 ft3, and 172.5 is far too big.

Task 2 (about 4 minutes, calculator)

The 30-inch wall cabinet is 30 in wide, 12 in deep and 30 in tall. Find its volume (a) in cubic inches and (b) in cubic feet. (c) Check your answer to (b) by converting the three measurements to feet first.

Solution to Task 2

(a) 30 × 12 × 30 = 10,800 in3.

(b) 10,800 ÷ 1,728 = 6.25 ft3.

(c) 30 in = 2.5 ft and 12 in = 1 ft, so 2.5 × 1 × 2.5 = 6.25 ft3.

The wall cabinet holds less than half of the 30-inch base cabinet's 14.375 ft3. It is half as deep and a little shorter.

5. Surface area from a net

Paint and veneer cover the outside of a box, so they depend on its surface area: the total area of all its faces, in square inches or square feet. A box has six rectangular faces in three matching pairs: top and bottom, front and back, left side and right side.

A net is the box cut along some edges and unfolded flat. Every face appears once, so the surface area of the box is the area of the net.

Add the faces in pairs:

As one rule: surface area of a box = 2 × (width × depth + width × height + depth × height). Here 2 × (720 + 1,035 + 828) = 2 × 2,583 = 5,166 in2.

To convert to square feet, divide by 144 (lecture 5.1): 5,166 ÷ 144 = 35.875 ft2.

Task 4 (about 4 minutes, calculator and paper)

The 30-inch wall cabinet is 30 in wide, 12 in deep and 30 in tall. (a) Sketch its net and label each face with its two measurements. (b) Find its surface area in square inches and in square feet.

Solution to Task 4

(a) The net has the same shape as the one above: a row of four faces (side, front, side, back) with the top and bottom attached to the front. Top and bottom are 30 × 12; front and back are 30 × 30; the sides are 12 × 30.

(b) Top and bottom: 2 × 360 = 720. Front and back: 2 × 900 = 1,800. Sides: 2 × 360 = 720. Total 720 + 1,800 + 720 = 3,240 in2, and 3,240 ÷ 144 = 22.5 ft2.

Benjamin Moore's data sheet for its ADVANCE Waterborne Interior Alkyd Semi Gloss paint, which it lists for "interior doors, trim, cabinets, walls, and ceilings," gives "Coverage per Gallon at Recommended Film Thickness 400 – 500 sq. ft." and recommends 1 or 2 coats [2]. To be safe, plan with the low end, 400 ft2 per gallon. The data sheet also calls for a coat of primer on cabinets before the paint [2]; we estimate only the paint.

Task 5 (about 3 minutes, calculator)

You will paint the 72 ft2 of exposed faces with 2 coats of this paint. (a) How many square feet of painting is that? (b) How many gallons does it take at 400 ft2 per gallon, and at 500 ft2 per gallon? (c) A quart is ¼ gallon. Write your answer at 400 ft2 per gallon in quarts, and give the smallest whole number of quarts that is sure to be enough.

Solution to Task 5

(a) Two coats cover the area twice: 2 × 72 = 144 ft2.

(b) Divide the area by the rate, as with any rate in week 1: 144 ft2 × (1 gal / 400 ft2) = 0.36 gal, and 144 ÷ 500 = 0.288 gal.

(c) In quarts, 0.36 gal = 0.36 × 4 = 1.44 quarts, so 2 quarts are sure to be enough. One quart is never enough: it would have to cover 144 ft2, which takes a coverage of 144 ÷ 0.25 = 576 ft2 per gallon, above the top of the range. A full gallon is 1 ÷ 0.36 = 2.8 times what the job needs at 400 ft2 per gallon.

7. Scaling: twice as wide or twice as big

Start again from the 30-inch base cabinet: 30 × 24 × 34.5, with volume 24,840 in3 and surface area 5,166 in2.

Twice as wide, same depth and height: 60 × 24 × 34.5 = 49,680 in3. That is 49,680 ÷ 24,840 = 2 times the volume. Only one of the three factors doubled, so the product doubled. For volume this is the same as placing two cabinets side by side. The surface area is less than double, because the two side panels that touch end up inside the wider box.

Twice as big in every direction: every length is doubled, to 60 in wide, 48 in deep and 69 in tall.

The number every length is multiplied by is the scale factor, written k. Doubling every length is k = 2. The slider multiplies every length of the 30-inch base cabinet by k. The grey dashed outline is the original cabinet; the tan box is the scaled one. The Ratio column is the scaled value divided by the original value.

(b) The area ratio equals k × k and the volume ratio equals k × k × k. At k = 0.5 the cabinet is half as big in every direction and holds only 1/8 as much. At k = 3 it holds 27 times as much.

The reason is in the formulas. The volume is width × depth × height. When all three lengths are doubled, each of the three factors is doubled, so the volume is multiplied by 2 × 2 × 2 = 8. The surface area of the doubled box is 2 × (60 × 48 + 60 × 69 + 48 × 69) = 20,664 in2, which is 20,664 ÷ 5,166 = 4 times the original. Each face is a rectangle with both sides doubled, and 2 × 2 = 4.

The same reasoning works for any scale factor k. Each area is a product of two lengths, so it is multiplied by k × k = k2 ("k squared"). Each volume is a product of three lengths, so it is multiplied by k × k × k = k3 ("k cubed").

Multiply every length by k: area is multiplied by k2, volume by k3.

8. Cubes and cube roots on a calculator

The cube of a number is the number times itself three times: the cube of 1.5 is 1.53 = 1.5 × 1.5 × 1.5 = 3.375. On a calculator, type 1.5 ^ 3, or use a key marked x3 or xy. The cubes of 1 through 12 are

1, 8, 27, 64, 125, 216, 343, 512, 729, 1,000, 1,331, 1,728.

The cube root undoes a cube. The cube root of 8, written ∛8, is 2, because 23 = 8. Lecture 5.2 did the same with squares and square roots. On a calculator, use a key marked ∛ if there is one. Otherwise type 8 ^ (1/3). The parentheses matter: without them, order of operations gives 8 ^ 1 / 3 = 8 ÷ 3 = 2.667, which is wrong. Check any cube root by cubing the answer.

Cube roots answer the scaling question backward. Suppose you want a base cabinet with the same shape as the 30-inch one that holds twice as much. The volume ratio is k3 = 2, so

k = ∛2 = 1.26 (to two decimal places).

Every length is multiplied by k. With four decimal places, k = 1.2599: 30 × 1.2599 = 37.8 in wide, 24 × 1.2599 = 30.2 in deep, 34.5 × 1.2599 = 43.5 in tall. Check: 37.8 × 30.2 × 43.5 = 49,658, close to 2 × 24,840 = 49,680; the small gap comes from rounding. Doubling the volume this way needs each length only 26% larger. The surface area, and so the paint, grows by k2 = 1.262 = 1.59.

Real cabinets come in widths in steps of 3 inches [1], so the closest standard widths are 36 or 39 inches, and a standard base cabinet is never 43.5 inches tall. In practice you would pick the nearest standard sizes and compute the volume again.

Task 7 (about 2 minutes, calculator)

You want a box with the same shape as the 30-inch base cabinet that holds 3 times as much. (a) Find the scale factor k to two decimal places, and check it by cubing. (b) By what factor does the paint needed for the outside grow?

Solution to Task 7

(a) k3 = 3, so k = ∛3. Type 3 ^ (1/3) to get 1.4422…, which is 1.44. Check: 1.443 = 2.99, close to 3, and the unrounded 1.44223 = 3.000 (to three decimal places).

(b) The surface area is multiplied by k2 = 1.44222 = 2.08. Three times the storage takes about 2.08 times the paint, because area grows more slowly than volume.
