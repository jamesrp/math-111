2. Tipping a tall cabinet upright

A tall pantry cabinet arrives lying on its back. It is 90 inches tall and 24 inches deep. These are made-up sizes for the example. The room has a ceiling height of 8 feet, which is 8 × 12 = 96 inches; this is also an example value. The International Residential Code (2021 edition), a model building code that states and cities adopt, sometimes with changes, sets a minimum ceiling height of 7 feet for habitable space, which includes kitchens [3]. Ceilings vary, so a real ceiling should be measured.

Standing up, the cabinet is 90 inches tall, which is less than 96. To get it standing, though, the installers tip it up. The bottom back edge stays on the floor and the top end swings up. During the swing, the top front corner (the red dot in the figure) rises higher than 90 inches.

Task 2 (about 3 minutes, slider above)

Move the slider slowly from 0° to 90°. Write down (a) the largest value of "highest point" you can find, to one decimal place, (b) the angle where it happens, and (c) whether the cabinet clears the 96-inch ceiling and by about how much.

Solution to Task 2

(a) The highest point reaches about 93.1 inches. (b) This happens near 75°. After that it drops back to 90 inches when the cabinet stands straight. (c) The cabinet clears the ceiling, by a little under 3 inches. The slider's 93.1 is rounded; the exact gap is 96 − 93.145… = 2.85… inches. Section 5 rounds the space needed up to 93.2 inches and so gives the spare room as 2.8 inches.

Why the highest point is the diagonal

The dashed line in the figure runs from the pivot edge to the top front corner. It is the diagonal of the cabinet's side view. The cabinet does not bend, so the top front corner always stays the same distance from the pivot edge: the length of the diagonal. As the cabinet tips, that corner moves along a circle centered at the pivot edge (the dotted red arc). The highest point of that circle is directly above the pivot, and its height there equals the diagonal. Every other point of the cabinet is closer to the pivot, so nothing goes higher. So the height a cabinet needs for tipping up is the length of its diagonal.

The slider answers this for one cabinet. For a cabinet of any size, we want to compute the diagonal from the height and the depth. That needs squares and square roots.

3. Squares and square roots

The square of a number is the number times itself. It is written with a small 2: 32 = 3 × 3 = 9, read "3 squared." The name comes from area: a square floor tile 3 inches on a side has area 32 = 9 square inches.

The square root goes the other way. The square root of 9, written √9, is the positive number whose square is 9, so √9 = 3. In area terms, √9 is the side length of a square whose area is 9.

The numbers 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, … are the perfect squares: the squares of whole numbers. Their square roots are whole numbers.

Most square roots are not whole numbers. √2 lies between 1 and 2, because 12 = 1 and 22 = 4. A calculator gives √2 = 1.41421…, and you can check it: 1.412 = 1.9881 and 1.422 = 2.0164, which are on either side of 2.

Estimating between perfect squares

To estimate √8676, find the perfect squares on either side. 932 = 8649 and 942 = 8836. Since 8676 is between them, √8676 is between 93 and 94. It is much closer to 8649 than to 8836, so the answer is just above 93.

For one decimal place, see how far 8676 is along the way from 8649 to 8836. The gap between the two perfect squares is 8836 − 8649 = 187, and 8676 is 8676 − 8649 = 27 past the lower one. 27 ÷ 187 = 0.14, so the estimate is about 93.1. The calculator gives 93.145… The estimate can be off in the second decimal place.

Order of operations on a calculator

Squares come before addition. A square root applies to everything inside the parentheses after it. The steps for √(902 + 242) are 902 = 8100, 242 = 576, 8100 + 576 = 8676, √8676 = 93.145…

Calculators enter this in two different ways.

  • On a calculator that shows the whole expression as you type it (a graphing calculator, or a phone app that shows a line of input), type √( 90 x² + 24 x² ), closing the parenthesis after the 242.
  • On a calculator where the √ key acts at once on the number already showing (the basic phone calculator in its scientific view, for example), compute 902 + 242 = 8676 first, then press √.

Two mistakes give very different answers. Taking the square root of the 902 part only gives √(902) + 242 = 90 + 576 = 666. Taking square roots of each part separately gives 90 + 24 = 114. The square root of a sum is not the sum of the square roots. Estimating first catches both mistakes: the answer must be between 93 and 94.

In a spreadsheet, the square root is the function SQRT, and squaring is ^2. For example, =SQRT(90^2+24^2).

4. The Pythagorean theorem

A right triangle has one square corner, called a right angle (90°). The two sides that meet at the square corner are the legs. The side opposite the square corner is the longest side, called the hypotenuse. Call the legs a and b and the hypotenuse c.

Pythagorean theorem. In every right triangle,

a2 + b2 = c2.

In words: the square on the hypotenuse has the same area as the squares on the two legs together. Taking square roots gives the length of the hypotenuse:

c = √(a2 + b2).

Example: legs 3 and 4 give c = √(9 + 16) = √25 = 5.

Why it is true: rearranging squares

Take four copies of the right triangle and a large square whose side is a + b. The four triangles fit inside the large square in two ways.

Both large squares have the same area, and both contain the same four triangles. So the uncovered areas are equal: c2 = a2 + b2. The pictures are drawn with a = 3 and b = 4. Then the large square has area 72 = 49, the four triangles have area 4 × (3 × 4 ÷ 2) = 24, and the uncovered part is 49 − 24 = 25 = 52 in both arrangements.

The converse: checking for a square corner

The theorem also works backwards. If a triangle has sides a, b, c with a2 + b2 = c2, then the angle between sides a and b is exactly 90°. This is called the converse of the theorem. It also tells which way a corner is off:

  • If c2 is larger than a2 + b2, the angle is wider than 90°.
  • If c2 is smaller than a2 + b2, the angle is narrower than 90°.

Opening a pair of scissors with fixed blade lengths shows why: the wider the angle, the farther apart the tips. Section 7 uses the converse to check kitchen corners.

5. The diagonal of a rectangle

A diagonal cuts a rectangle into two right triangles. The width and height of the rectangle are the legs, and the diagonal is the hypotenuse. So for a rectangle of width w and height h,

diagonal = √(w2 + h2).

Back to the cabinet. Its side view is a rectangle 24 inches by 90 inches, so its diagonal is

√(242 + 902) = √(576 + 8100) = √8676 = 93.145… inches.

This matches the 93.1 inches from the slider in Task 2. As Section 2 explained, the top front corner swings on a circle whose radius is the diagonal, so the cabinet needs 93.145… inches of height to be tipped up.

Rounding up safely

When an answer is a clearance, round in the safe direction. Round the space needed up and the space available down. For the cabinet, the space needed is 93.145… inches. Rounded up to the next tenth, that is 93.2 inches; rounded up to the next whole inch, it is 94 inches. The ceiling is 96 inches, so the spare room is 96 − 93.2 = 2.8 inches.

Rounding to the nearest value can give the wrong answer when the numbers are close. A cabinet 93 inches tall and 24 inches deep has diagonal √(8649 + 576) = √9225 = 96.047… inches. Rounded to the nearest tenth, that is 96.0, which looks like an exact fit under a 96-inch ceiling. Rounded up, it is 96.1, which does not fit. The rounded-up answer gives the correct decision: the diagonal is about 0.05 inches more than the ceiling height, so this cabinet catches on the ceiling.

This model is a simplification. It treats the cabinet as a perfect box and the floor and ceiling as perfectly level, and it assumes the pivot edge stays in place.

Back to the refrigerator

Inside the house, the delivery crew tips the refrigerator back onto a hand truck, pivoting on its bottom back edge, just like the cabinet. With the doors off, its side view is a rectangle 68 5/8 inches tall (height to top of case) and 29 inches deep [1]. Its diagonal is

√(68.6252 + 292) = √(4709.39… + 841) = √5550.39… = 74.50… inches,

which rounds up to 74.6 inches. That leaves 96 − 74.6 = 21.4 inches under the 96-inch ceiling, and it is even less than the 80-inch doorway height from Section 1, so the crew could tip it back under a door header. The hinges on top of the case reach 69 3/4 inches [1]; using that height instead gives a diagonal of 75.5 inches, still under 80.

7. Checking that a corner is square: the 3-4-5 rule



Base cabinets and countertops are straight boxes, so they fit well only if the corner of the room is square (90°). Carpenters check this with a tape measure, using the 3-4-5 rule. A carpentry guide describes it this way: "If the short side of the triangle is 3 feet, and the leg that extends from it 90 degrees is 4 feet, the hypotenuse, or longest leg, will be 5 feet" [4].





The rule is the converse of the Pythagorean theorem: 32 + 42 = 9 + 16 = 25 = 52, so if the marks are exactly 5 feet apart, the corner is exactly 90°. Any multiple of 3, 4, 5 works too, because multiplying all three sides by the same number keeps the equation true. The same guide says larger multiples, such as 6-8-10 or 12-16-20, can be used for more accuracy on larger walls [4]: a small error in the tape reading matters less when the distances are longer. For 6-8-10: 36 + 64 = 100 = 102.



The marks do not have to be a nice triple. With marks at 5 feet and 7 feet, the diagonal should be √(25 + 49) = √74 = 8.602… feet. The 0.602… feet is 0.602… × 12 = 7.23 inches, close to 7 1/4 inches, so the tape should read about 8 feet 7 1/4 inches. Rounding to the nearest 1/8 inch is right here, because 8 feet 7 1/4 inches is a target reading for the tape. Nothing has to fit inside it.
