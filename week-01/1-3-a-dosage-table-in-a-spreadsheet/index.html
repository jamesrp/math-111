Math 111 · Module: Titrating medicine · Lecture 3 of 3

A dosage table in a spreadsheet

A bottle of Children's Tylenol liquid contains 160 mg of acetaminophen in each 5 mL, and its label prints a dosing chart by weight [1]. In lecture 1.1 you computed one dose by hand. The chart covers children from 24 to 95 pounds, which is 72 whole-pound weights. Computing each of them by hand is slow, and one slip on a calculator gives a wrong dose that looks like a right one.

This is a mathematics lecture, not clinical training. Real dosing follows the prescriber's order, the product label and the institution's protocol; the numbers here illustrate the arithmetic.

1. The dosing chart on the package

The label of Children's Tylenol oral suspension (160 mg per 5 mL) says: “find right dose on chart below. If possible, use weight to dose; otherwise, use age.” It also says “repeat dose every 4 hours while symptoms last” and “do not give more than 5 times in 24 hours” [1]. Its chart, quoted exactly:

Dosing chart from the Children's Tylenol label [1]
Weight (lb)Age (yr)Dose (mL)*

24-35 lbs2-3 years5 mL
36-47 lbs4-5 years7.5 mL
48-59 lbs6-8 years10 mL
60-71 lbs9-10 years12.5 mL
72-95 lbs11 years15 mL

*or as directed by a doctor

Each row of the chart is a weight band: a range of weights that all get the same dose. A child who weighs 24 lb and a child who weighs 35 lb both get 5 mL.

To compute a dose from weight we need a dose per kilogram. StatPearls, a medical reference hosted on the National Library of Medicine's NCBI Bookshelf, says: “The recommended dosage for children aged 2 to 12 is 12.5 mg/kg every 4 hours or 15 mg/kg every 6 hours,” and “The maximum single dose should not exceed 15 mg/kg” [2]. The label repeats doses every 4 hours, so our computed column uses 12.5 mg per kg.

One pound is 0.4535924 kg [3], so 1 kg is 1 ÷ 0.4535924 = 2.2046 lb. We round this to 2.2 lb per kg. This simplification makes every weight in kg about 0.2% too large, which is far smaller than the differences we will find between the chart and our table.

2. One dose by hand

For a child who weighs 50 lb, the dose takes three multiplications. The first is a conversion factor, a fraction equal to 1, as in lecture 1.1. The other two are rates: the dose rate and the concentration of the liquid. A rate is not equal to 1 (12.5 mg of medicine is not the same amount as 1 kg of child), but it is used the same way, written so that the unit you want to get rid of cancels.

50 lb × 1 kg2.2 lb = 22.727 kg
22.727 kg × 12.5 mg1 kg = 284.09 mg
284.09 mg × 5 mL160 mg = 8.88 mL

The first fraction changes pounds to kilograms. The second is the dose rate: 12.5 mg for each kg of body weight. The third uses the concentration of the liquid, 160 mg in 5 mL, turned so that mg cancels and mL is left. We keep all the digits until the last step and round only the answer.

The chart puts 50 lb in the 48-59 lb band and gives 10 mL. Our computed 8.88 mL is different. Section 4 explains why.

Task 1 (about 3 minutes, calculator)

A child weighs 30 lb. Using 2.2 lb per kg, 12.5 mg per kg and 160 mg per 5 mL, write down the three answers below. Keep all the digits on your calculator from one step to the next; round only what you write down.

  1. the weight in kg, to two decimal places;
  2. the dose in mg, to two decimal places;
  3. the dose in mL, to two decimal places;
  4. the dose the chart gives for 30 lb.
Solution to Task 1
30 lb × 1 kg2.2 lb = 13.636… kg
13.636… kg × 12.5 mg1 kg = 170.45 mg
170.45 mg × 5 mL160 mg = 5.33 mL

Written to two decimal places, the answers are 13.64 kg, 170.45 mg and 5.33 mL.

If you carried the rounded 13.64 kg into the second step, you got 13.64 × 12.5 = 170.50 mg. The small difference comes from rounding too early; the dose in mL is 5.33 either way.

The chart puts 30 lb in the 24-35 lb band, so it gives 5 mL.

Each weight needs three calculator steps. For 72 weights that is 216 steps, with 216 chances to mistype. A spreadsheet does the three steps for one weight, and then repeats them for every other weight with no further typing.

3. Using a spreadsheet

Cells, columns and the header row

A spreadsheet is a grid. Columns are named by letters (A, B, C, …) and rows by numbers (1, 2, 3, …). Each box in the grid is a cell, and its address is its column letter followed by its row number. The cell in column B, row 2 has address B2.

We put one kind of quantity in each column, with its unit. Row 1 is the header row: it holds the name of each column and nothing else. Each later row is one child's weight.

Formulas

A formula is a cell entry that starts with =. The spreadsheet computes it and shows the result. A formula can use the number in another cell by naming its address. For example, =A2/2.2 means “take the number in A2 and divide it by 2.2”. If you later change the number in A2, the result changes too.

The spreadsheet uses * for multiplication and / for division, and it follows the order of operations from lecture 1.1.

The three hand steps from Section 2 become three formulas, one per column:

  • =A2/2.2 in B2 changes the pounds in A2 to kilograms.
  • =B2*12.5 in C2 multiplies the kilograms in B2 by 12.5 mg per kg.
  • =C2*5/160 in D2 multiplies the milligrams in C2 by the fraction 5 mL / 160 mg.
  • =A2+1 in A3 adds 1 to the weight above it, which gives 25.

The numbers 2.2, 12.5, 5 and 160 are typed inside the formulas. A number fixed inside a formula is called a constant. Section 6 comes back to them.

Copying a formula down

To fill the table, copy the formulas down to row 73. Row 73 is the last row because the weights 24 through 95 take 72 rows, and they start in row 2.

  • Fill handle: click a cell, then drag the small square at its bottom-right corner down to row 73.
  • Keyboard: select from the formula cell down to row 73 (for example B2 to D73), then press Ctrl+D (Cmd+D on a Mac). This works in Excel and in Google Sheets.

When a formula is copied down one row, every cell address in it moves down one row as well. The copy of =A2/2.2 in B3 becomes =A3/2.2, and the copy in B73 becomes =A73/2.2. Addresses that shift like this are called relative references. The spreadsheet stores =A2/2.2 in B2 as “the cell in the same row, one column to the left, divided by 2.2”, so each row uses its own weight.

4. Comparing the computed table with the chart

We add three columns to the dosage table.

  • E, Chart dose (mL). The dose the label gives for that weight. Copying a plain number down copies the same number. Type 5 in E2 and copy it down to E13 (24 to 35 lb). Then type 7.5 in E14 and copy to E25 (36 to 47 lb), 10 in E26 to E37 (48 to 59 lb), 12.5 in E38 to E49 (60 to 71 lb), and 15 in E50 to E73 (72 to 95 lb).
  • F, Difference (mL). =E2-D2, copied down. A positive difference means the chart gives more than our computed dose; a negative one means it gives less.
  • G, Chart rate (mg/kg). =E2*160/5/B2, copied down. The part E2*160/5 changes the chart's mL to mg. Dividing by the weight in kg in B2 gives the mg per kg that the chart's dose amounts to for that child.

To find the largest number in a column, a spreadsheet has the function MAX. A range such as F2:F73 means every cell from F2 down to F73. Typing =MAX(F2:F73) in an empty cell shows the largest difference, and =MIN(F2:F73) shows the smallest. You can also find them by reading down the column.

Task 3 (about 10 minutes, laptop with Excel or Google Sheets)

  1. Add columns E, F and G as described above.
  2. Write down the largest and the smallest value in column F, to two decimal places, and the weight in each of those rows.
  3. Write down the largest and the smallest value in column G, to two decimal places, and the weight or weights where each occurs.
  4. Look at the rows for 24 to 35 lb. Write one sentence that says why column F starts positive and ends negative inside that band.
Solution to Task 3
  1. The largest difference is +2.22 mL, at 72 lb (chart 15 mL, computed 12.78 mL). The smallest is −1.87 mL, at 95 lb (chart 15 mL, computed 16.87 mL).
  2. The largest chart rate is 14.67 mg/kg. It occurs five times, at 24, 36, 48, 60 and 72 lb, the lightest weight in each band. The smallest is 10.06 mg/kg, at 35 lb.
  3. Inside the band the chart dose stays at 5 mL while the computed dose grows with weight, from 4.26 mL at 24 lb to 6.21 mL at 35 lb, so it passes 5 mL (between 28 and 29 lb) and the difference turns negative.

The chart and the computed table differ for three reasons.

Weight bands. The chart gives one dose for a band of 12 whole-pound weights (24 for the last band, 72 to 95 lb). The computed dose rises a little with every pound. So the chart gives more than the computed dose at the light end of each band and less at the heavy end. The biggest gaps sit at the edges of the bands: +2.22 mL at 72 lb, the first weight of the last band, and −1.87 mL at 95 lb, its last weight.

Rounding. The chart's doses are multiples of 2.5 mL. Our computed doses such as 8.88 mL are not.

The dose rate. We used 12.5 mg/kg for every child. Column G shows the rate the chart's doses amount to: it starts at 14.67 mg/kg at the lightest weight of each band and falls as weight rises within the band, to between 10.06 and 12.39 mg/kg at the heaviest weight of a band. It never goes above 15 mg/kg, the maximum single dose in [2]. The light end of each band shows a pattern:

160 mg24 lb = 240 mg36 lb = 320 mg48 lb = 400 mg60 lb = 480 mg72 lb = 6.67 mg per lb

and 6.67 mg per lb × 2.2 lb per kg = 14.67 mg per kg. The label does not say how its chart was made; this pattern is what the numbers show. In every band the chart's rate falls from above 12.5 mg/kg to below it, which is why our computed dose is under the chart dose at the light end of each band and over it at the heavy end.

5. IV infusion rates

An IV bag can run fluid into a vein by gravity through IV tubing [4]. The fluid falls in drops, so the rate can be given in mL per hour or in drops per minute.

An infusion order gives a volume and a time. The order used here is made up for this example: 1,000 mL of fluid over 8 hours. The rate in mL per hour is volume divided by time:

1000 mL8 h = 125 mL per hour

To count drops we need the drip factor of the tubing (also called the drop factor). Open RN's nursing textbook defines it: “The drop factor is the number of drops in one mL of solution using gravity IV tubing.” It continues: “Macrodrip includes tubing with drop factors of 10, 15, or 20 drops per mililiter [sic] and is typically used to deliver general IV solutions to adults. Microdrip includes tubing with a drop factor of 60 drops per milliliter” [4]. The textbook adds: “The drop factor is printed on the IV tubing package” [4].

With 15 drops per mL tubing, change mL per hour to drops per minute by multiplying by a conversion factor and the drip factor:

125 mLh × 1 h60 min × 15 drops1 mL = 31.25 drops per minute

Hours cancel, mL cancels, and drops per minute is left. Nobody can count a quarter of a drop, so the textbook rounds to the nearest whole number [4]: 31 drops per minute.

6. Constants inside formulas

Every formula in both tables carries its own constants. Each of the 72 cells in column B holds its own 2.2, each cell in column C its own 12.5, and each cell in column D of the IV table its own 15. When a constant changes, every formula that contains it has to be edited. Task 5 showed this with the drip factor. In the dosage table, switching to 15 mg/kg, the other rate in [2], means rewriting C2 and copying it down all 72 rows again. Using 2.2046 lb per kg instead of 2.2 means the same for column B, and column G uses B. In a larger spreadsheet, a constant that is missed in one cell leaves a wrong number that looks exactly like the right ones around it.

A tempting fix is to type the constant once, say 12.5 in cell H1, and write =B2*H1 in C2. Copying that down does not work. Relative references move, so C3 receives =B3*H2, and H2 is empty, so C3 shows 0.

That leaves a question for lecture 4.1 (Buying a house): how to write a formula that refers to one fixed cell holding a constant, and keeps referring to that same cell when it is copied down.
