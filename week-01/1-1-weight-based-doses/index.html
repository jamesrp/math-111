Math 111 · Module: Titrating medicine · Lecture 1 of 3

Weight-based doses

1. Children's liquid acetaminophen

Acetaminophen is the drug in Children's Tylenol. In the United States, liquid acetaminophen for children is sold as 160 mg of acetaminophen in every 5 mL of liquid [5]. A store-brand label lists the active ingredient as "Acetaminophen 160 mg" in each 5 mL [2]. Until 2011, liquid acetaminophen for infants came only in stronger forms, such as 80 mg in 0.8 mL. After dosing errors with them, manufacturers began changing infant products to the same 160 mg per 5 mL [1].

Doses for children are often figured from body weight. One drug reference gives the oral dose for a child as "10 to 15 mg/kg orally every 4 to 6 hours as needed not to exceed 5 doses in 24 hours" [3]. Here mg/kg means milligrams of drug for each kilogram the child weighs.

In the United States a parent or nurse usually knows the child's weight in pounds. So three facts have to be combined:

  • the child's weight, in pounds;
  • the dose, in milligrams per kilogram of body weight;
  • the concentration, in milligrams per milliliter of liquid.

The question is how many milliliters to give, and how to check that the answer is not off by a factor of ten. A 40 lb child is the running example.

This is a mathematics lecture. Real dosing follows the prescriber's order, the product label and institutional protocol. The numbers here illustrate the arithmetic.

Task 1 (about 3 minutes, pencil and paper)

The liquid has 160 mg of acetaminophen in every 5 mL. Without a calculator, write each answer with its unit.

  1. How many milligrams are in 1 mL?
  2. How many milligrams are in 10 mL?
  3. How many milliliters hold 240 mg?
Solution to Task 1
  1. Share 160 mg equally among 5 mL: 160 ÷ 5 = 32 mg in each mL.
  2. 10 mL is twice 5 mL, so 2 × 160 = 320 mg. Or 10 × 32 = 320 mg.
  3. 240 mg is one and a half times 160 mg, so it takes 1.5 × 5 = 7.5 mL. Or 240 ÷ 32 = 7.5 mL.

2. Rates written as fractions

A rate compares two quantities measured in different units. "160 mg per 5 mL" is a rate. Write it as a fraction with its units:

160 mg5 mL    read "160 milligrams per 5 milliliters".

The word "per" becomes the fraction bar. The dose "15 mg/kg" is the rate 15 mg1 kg.

Simplifying a fraction

If the top and the bottom share a factor, divide both by it. The value does not change.

1605 = 32 × 51 × 5 = 321 = 32

So 160 mg per 5 mL is the same rate as 32 mg per 1 mL. This is Task 1(a) written as fractions. The rate can also be written the other way up, as milliliters per milligram:

5 mL160 mg = 1 × 532 × 5 mL per mg = 132 mL per mg

Each milligram takes 132 of a milliliter. Both fractions describe the same liquid.

Multiplying fractions, and units that cancel

To multiply fractions, multiply the tops and multiply the bottoms. A whole number is a fraction with 1 on the bottom, so 12 = 121.

23 × 57 = 2 × 53 × 7 = 1021

Units in a fraction behave like numbers. A unit on top cancels the same unit on the bottom, just as 5 on top cancels 5 on the bottom. This tells you which way up to write each rate.

Worked example with made-up numbers, chosen so the arithmetic can be done by hand. A medicine has 100 mg in every 5 mL. The dose is 10 mg/kg. The child weighs 12 kg.

Step 1: milligrams. Multiply the weight by the dose rate. The kilograms cancel and milligrams are left.

12 kg × 10 mg1 kg = 120 mg

Step 2: milliliters. Multiply by the concentration written with mg on the bottom, so that mg cancels and mL is left.

120 mg × 5 mL100 mg = 600100 mL = 6 mL

If you write the concentration the other way up, as 100 mg5 mL, the units come out as mg × mg / mL, which is not a volume. The units tell you that the setup is wrong before you compute anything.

Task 2 (about 5 minutes, pencil and paper)

Made-up numbers. A medicine has 120 mg in every 5 mL. The dose is 15 mg/kg. The child weighs 16 kg. Write each step with its units, and cross out units that cancel.

  1. How many milligrams are in 1 mL of this medicine?
  2. How many milligrams is the dose?
  3. How many milliliters is the dose?
Solution to Task 2
  1. 120 mg5 mL = 24 mg1 mL, since 120 ÷ 5 = 24. Each milliliter holds 24 mg.
  2. 16 kg × 15 mg1 kg = 240 mg. (16 × 15 = 160 + 80 = 240.)
  3. 240 mg × 5 mL120 mg = 1200120 mL = 10 mL. Using part (a) instead: 240 ÷ 24 = 10 mL.

3. Converting pounds to kilograms

The pound is defined in terms of the kilogram. By definition, 1 lb = 0.45359237 kg exactly [4].

Because 1 lb and 0.45359237 kg are the same amount, the fraction

0.45359237 kg1 lb = 1.

Multiplying by this fraction changes the units and leaves the amount the same. It is called a conversion factor: a fraction equal to 1 whose top and bottom are in different units. Choose which way up to write it so that the unit you start with cancels and the unit you want is left.

Example: a 44 lb child.

44 lb × 0.45359237 kg1 lb = 19.95806428 kg ≈ 20.0 kg

Going the other way, from kilograms to pounds, flip the factor so kg cancels:

1 lb0.45359237 kg

Then 1 kg = 1 ÷ 0.45359237 lb ≈ 2.2046 lb. This is where the familiar "1 kg is about 2.2 lb" comes from. For estimating, a kilogram count is a little less than half the pound count.

4. The whole calculation in one line

Now the real question for a 40 lb child, at the top of the range, 15 mg/kg, with liquid at 160 mg per 5 mL [2]. Chain the three rates so each unit cancels until only mL is left:

40 lb × 0.45359237 kg1 lb × 15 mg1 kg × 5 mL160 mg

Pounds cancel, then kilograms, then milligrams. Milliliters are left, which is what the question asks for.

Step by step:

  • 40 × 0.45359237 = 18.1436948 kg
  • 18.1436948 × 15 = 272.155422 mg
  • 272.155422 × 5 ÷ 160 = 8.5048569… mL

Entering it on a calculator

The whole chain can go in as one line. Everything on top is multiplied, and everything on the bottom is divided:

40 × 0.45359237 × 15 × 5 ÷ 160 = 8.5048569…

This works on any calculator, including a phone. Calculators follow the order of operations: multiplication and division are done from left to right, after anything in parentheses. A line with only × and ÷ gives the right answer as long as each ÷ divides by a single number from the bottom of a fraction.

Dividing by a fraction

Task 1 used the concentration as 32 mg per mL, which is 1605. Then mL = mg ÷ 1605, a division by a fraction. To divide by a fraction, flip it over and multiply. The flipped fraction is called the reciprocal.

34 ÷ 12 = 34 × 21 = 64 = 32

So dividing by 1605 is the same as multiplying by its reciprocal 5160: multiply by 5, then divide by 160. That is what the one-line key sequence above does, and why the made-up example in Section 2 multiplied by 5 mL100 mg.

Typing the division by 1605 directly goes wrong without parentheses:

KeysCalculator doesResult
272.155422 × 5 ÷ 160 =272.155422 × 5, then ÷ 1608.505 mL
272.155422 ÷ 160 ÷ 5 =(272.155422 ÷ 160) ÷ 50.340 mL

The second line divides by 160 and then divides by 5 again, which is dividing by 800. The answer is 25 times too small. Typing 272.155422 ÷ (160 ÷ 5) = also works, but some phone calculators have no parentheses keys in portrait mode. Multiplying by 5 and dividing by 160 works everywhere.

Another slip is dividing by 0.45359237 instead of multiplying. Then 40 lb becomes 88.18 "kg", and the dose comes out as 41.3 mL, almost five times too much. Writing the units catches this: lb ÷ (kg/lb) leaves lb² per kg, which is not a weight.

From a number to an amount you can measure

No one can measure 8.5048569 mL. The label [2] and the FDA [1] both say to use the dosing device that comes with the product, so the possible amounts are the markings on that cup. For the arithmetic in this lecture, assume a cup marked every 0.5 mL. This is a simplification, and a real cup may be marked differently.

The dose is a range, 10 to 15 mg/kg [3], so compute both ends:

  • At 10 mg/kg: 40 × 0.45359237 × 10 × 5 ÷ 160 = 5.67 mL
  • At 15 mg/kg: 8.50 mL

Round toward the inside of the range. At the top, round down: 8.5049 mL becomes 8.5 mL (272 mg, 14.99 mg/kg). Rounding up to 9 mL would give 288 mg, or 15.87 mg/kg, above the top. At the bottom, round up: 5.67 mL becomes 6 mL (10.58 mg/kg), since 5.5 mL would be 9.70 mg/kg. So the marked amounts from 6 mL to 8.5 mL fall inside the range.

The range is a check on a computed number. It does not choose the dose: the label or the prescriber's order does. For children from 36 to 47 lb the label says 7.5 mL [2]. That is 7.5 × 32 = 240 mg. For a 40 lb child, 240 mg ÷ 18.14 kg = 13.2 mg/kg, inside the range, so the check agrees with the label.

5. Estimating the size of the answer

A calculator gives an answer to many decimal places whether or not the keys were right. An estimate made beforehand catches answers that are 10 times too big or too small. Use round numbers:

  • kilograms: about half the pounds;
  • concentration: 32 mg per mL is about 30 mg per mL.

For the 40 lb child at 15 mg/kg: about 20 kg, so about 20 × 15 = 300 mg, so about 300 ÷ 30 = 10 mL.

The computed answer, 8.5 mL, is the same size as 10 mL. An answer of 0.85 mL or 85 mL would be off by a factor of ten, and the estimate shows it immediately. It also catches the two slips from Section 4: 0.34 mL is far below 10 mL, and 41.3 mL is far above it. The estimate runs a little high because both round numbers push it up: half of 40 is more than 40 × 0.45, and dividing by 30 gives more than dividing by 32.

Task 5 (about 3 minutes, no calculator)

Three people computed the 15 mg/kg dose of the 160 mg per 5 mL liquid for a 60 lb child. They got 1.28 mL, 12.8 mL and 128 mL. Without a calculator, estimate the dose and say which answer is plausible. Write your estimate with its steps.

Solution to Task 5

60 lb is about 30 kg. 30 × 15 = 450 mg. 450 ÷ 30 = 15 mL. The plausible answer is 12.8 mL. The other two are 10 times too small and 10 times too big.

Exact value: 60 × 0.45359237 × 15 × 5 ÷ 160 = 12.757… mL.

6. Comparing with the maximum daily amount

The label says not to give more than 5 doses in 24 hours, and calls this "the maximum daily amount" [2]. The American Academy of Pediatrics gives the same limit of 5 doses [5]. On the label, this means 5 doses of the amount in its weight table.

The drug reference gives at most 15 mg/kg per dose and at most 5 doses in 24 hours [3]. Multiplying these two numbers gives a limit per kilogram for a whole day:

5 doses × 15 mg/kg1 dose = 75 mg/kg in 24 hours

The 75 mg/kg figure is our own calculation from the two numbers in the reference. For the 40 lb child (18.1436948 kg) the limit is 75 × 18.1436948 = 1360.8 mg in 24 hours.

Five doses of the label's 7.5 mL:

  • 5 × 7.5 mL = 37.5 mL
  • 37.5 mL × 32 mg per mL = 1200 mg
  • 1200 mg ÷ 18.1436948 kg = 66.1 mg/kg, under 75 mg/kg

Five doses at the top of the range, 8.5 mL each, give 42.5 mL, or 1360 mg, which is 74.96 mg/kg. That is within 1 mg of the limit. A dose at the top of the per-dose range, given the most times allowed, lands on the daily limit, so there is no room to round up.

Task 6 (about 3 minutes, calculator)

The 26 lb child from Task 4 (11.7934 kg) gets the label's 5 mL, five times in 24 hours.

  1. How many mL and how many mg is that in 24 hours?
  2. How many mg/kg is that in 24 hours, to one decimal place?
  3. What is the 75 mg/kg limit in mg for this child? Is the day's total under it?
Solution to Task 6
  1. 5 × 5 mL = 25 mL. 25 mL × 32 mg per mL = 800 mg.
  2. 800 mg ÷ 11.7934 kg = 67.8 mg/kg.
  3. 75 × 11.7934 = 884.5 mg. The day's total, 800 mg, is under it.
