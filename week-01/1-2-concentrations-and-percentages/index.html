Concentrations and percentages

1. Normal saline

Normal saline is salt dissolved in water, given through an IV. One manufacturer's 1,000 mL bag is printed like this [1]:

0.9% Sodium Chloride Injection USP
1000 mL
Each 100 mL contains 900 mg Sodium Chloride USP

Sodium chloride is table salt. The concentration of a solution is the amount of dissolved substance in a given volume, for example 900 mg of salt in 100 mL. The label gives the concentration twice: as a percentage, 0.9%, and as an amount in a volume, 900 mg in each 100 mL. Three questions about that bag:

  1. What does 0.9% mean?
  2. How much salt is in the whole 1,000 mL bag?
  3. How do you make a weaker solution from a stronger one?

The label already answers the second question with arithmetic you know. A liter is 1,000 mL, which is 10 groups of 100 mL. Each group holds 900 mg, so the bag holds

10 × 900 mg = 9,000 mg = 9 g of salt.

The same manufacturer's prescribing information states it directly: 0.9% sodium chloride "contains 9 g/L" [1].

This is a mathematics lecture. Real dosing follows the prescriber's order, the product label and the institution's protocol. The numbers here illustrate the arithmetic.

2. Fractions, decimals and percentages

Percent means "per hundred." 0.9% means 0.9 parts per 100 parts. Every percentage is a fraction with denominator 100:

0.9% = 0.9/1005% = 5/10050% = 50/100

Percent to decimal: divide by 100

Dividing by 100 moves the decimal point two places to the left.

0.9% → 0.9 ÷ 100 = 0.0095% → 5 ÷ 100 = 0.05

Decimal to percent: multiply by 100 and add the % sign

The number in front of the % sign is 100 times the decimal. Multiplying by 100 moves the decimal point two places to the right. The value does not change, because the % sign divides by 100 again.

0.001 = 0.1%, since 0.001 × 100 = 0.10.5 = 50%, since 0.5 × 100 = 50

Fraction to decimal: divide the top by the bottom

Use a calculator when the division is not obvious.

1/20 → 1 ÷ 20 = 0.051/1000 → 1 ÷ 1,000 = 0.001

Decimal to fraction: read the place value

The last digit of 0.009 is in the thousandths place, so 0.009 is 9 thousandths.

0.009 = 9/10000.05 = 5/100 = 1/20

Percent to fraction: write it over 100, then simplify

If the percent has a decimal point, multiply top and bottom by 10 until it is gone. For 0.9%, one multiplication by 10 turns 0.9/100 into 9/1000.

5% = 5/100 = 1/200.9% = 0.9/100 = 9/1000

Fraction to percent: go through the decimal

Convert the fraction to a decimal, then the decimal to a percent.

1/1000 → 0.001 → 0.1%1/10000 → 0.0001 → 0.01%

3. Percent weight per volume

A solution is a mixture, so a percentage on its label needs a convention for "parts." For IV fluids the convention is percent weight per volume, written w/v [1, 2]:

x% w/v means x grams of the dissolved substance (the drug, or the salt) in every 100 mL of solution.

So 0.9% sodium chloride means 0.9 g of salt in every 100 mL. Converting grams to milligrams gives 0.9 g = 900 mg, which is the "900 mg per 100 mL" printed on the bag [1].

Grams of a substance and milliliters of solution are different kinds of units, so on these labels "x%" is shorthand for "x g per 100 mL." For a watery solution this is close to a percent by weight, because 1 mL of water weighs about 1 g.

The salt in the whole bag

A concentration is a rate, and you use it like the dose rates of Lecture 1.1: multiply so that the units cancel. It is not a fraction equal to 1, because 0.9 g of salt is not the same amount as 100 mL of solution. Write 0.9% as a rate and multiply by the volume:

1,000 mL × (0.9 g / 100 mL) = 9 g

The mL cancel, leaving grams. This matches the 9 g per liter on the label [1].

Dividing by 100 also gives the concentration per milliliter:

0.9 g / 100 mL = 0.009 g/mL = 9 mg/mL

The decimal 0.009 from Section 2 reappears here: 0.9% is 0.009 grams in each milliliter.

A second solution: 5% dextrose

Dextrose is a sugar. A manufacturer's label for 5% dextrose injection (often called D5W) states "Each mL ... contains ... Dextrose ... 50 mg" [2]. Check that this agrees with 5% w/v:

5% w/v = 5 g per 100 mL = 5,000 mg per 100 mL = 50 mg per mL

That label is for small containers. The 500 mL and 1,000 mL volumes of 5% dextrose used below are chosen for the arithmetic. A 1,000 mL volume of 5% dextrose contains 1,000 mL × (5 g / 100 mL) = 50 g of dextrose.

Task 2 (about 5 minutes, calculator)

Normal saline is sold in 500 mL and 250 mL bags as well as 1,000 mL [1].

  1. How many grams of salt are in a 500 mL bag of 0.9% sodium chloride?
  2. How many grams, and how many milligrams, are in a 250 mL bag of 0.9% sodium chloride?
  3. How many grams of dextrose are in 500 mL of 5% dextrose?
  4. A made-up solution has 2 g of drug dissolved in 250 mL. What is its concentration as a percent w/v?

Produce: four answers with units, each with the one-line calculation that gives it. Before calculating (a), write down an estimate from the 9 g in a 1,000 mL bag.

Solution to Task 2
  1. 500 mL × (0.9 g / 100 mL) = 4.5 g. Estimate: half of a 1,000 mL bag, so half of 9 g.
  2. 250 mL × (0.9 g / 100 mL) = 2.25 g = 2,250 mg.
  3. 500 mL × (5 g / 100 mL) = 25 g.
  4. Percent w/v asks for grams per 100 mL. 250 mL is 2.5 groups of 100 mL, so each 100 mL holds 2 g ÷ 2.5 = 0.8 g. The concentration is 0.8%. Another route: 2 g / 250 mL = 0.008 g/mL, and 0.008 × 100 = 0.8, so 0.8 g per 100 mL.

4. Ratio strength: epinephrine

Epinephrine (adrenaline) is an injectable drug. Its concentration was printed as a ratio, such as 1:1,000 or 1:10,000. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration explains the meaning: "a 1:1,000 ratio for epinephrine represents 1 gram of epinephrine in 1,000 mL of solution" [3].

1:r means 1 g of drug in r mL of solution.

To convert 1:1,000 to mg per mL, write it as a rate and convert grams to milligrams:

1 g / 1,000 mL = 1,000 mg / 1,000 mL = 1 mg/mL

For 1:10,000, the same gram is spread through ten times the volume:

1 g / 10,000 mL = 1,000 mg / 10,000 mL = 0.1 mg/mL

Both results match the FDA's conversion table [3] and the United States Pharmacopeia (USP) [4].

A ratio strength is also a fraction. 1:1,000 is 1/1000 g per mL, which is 1 g per 1,000 mL, or 0.1 g per 100 mL. In the percent w/v language of Section 3, 1:1,000 epinephrine is a 0.1% solution, and 1:10,000 is 0.01%. These are the last two rows of the table in Section 2.

Why ratio labels were removed

1 mg of epinephrine is in 1 mL of the 1:1,000 product and in 10 mL of the 1:10,000 product. The two labels differ by one zero, and the concentrations differ by a factor of 10. The FDA notes that the Institute for Safe Medication Practices (ISMP) reported errors in which "undiluted epinephrine 1:1,000 (1 mg/mL) was given intravenously to patients instead of the 1:10,000 (0.1 mg/mL) concentration" [3]. The FDA adds that "1,000 may easily look like 10,000 in small print, especially when typed without the comma (1:1000 and 1:10000)" [3].

USP made it official on May 1, 2016, that labels of single-ingredient drugs give the concentration "only as the strength per milliliter (e.g., mg/mL)," stating that "labeling drugs through ratio expressions is inadequate and error-prone" [4]. The standard does not cover local anesthetics mixed with epinephrine, such as bupivacaine with epinephrine 1:200,000, which may still be labeled with a ratio [4]. In June 2017 the FDA announced it was requiring the change "to help prevent medication errors related to strength being expressed as a ratio on labeling," and that "Epinephrine Injection 1:1,000 will now be labeled as Epinephrine Injection 1 mg/mL" [3].

Task 3 (about 5 minutes, calculator)

The FDA change also covered two other drugs once labeled with ratios [3]: isoproterenol 1:5,000 and neostigmine 1:2,000.

  1. Convert 1:5,000 and 1:2,000 to mg/mL.
  2. Write each as a percent w/v.
  3. How many mL of 1:10,000 epinephrine contain 0.5 mg? How many mL of 1:1,000 epinephrine contain 0.5 mg?

Produce: two mg/mL values, two percentages, and two volumes in mL, each with its calculation.

Solution to Task 3
  1. 1:5,000: 1,000 mg / 5,000 mL = 0.2 mg/mL. 1:2,000: 1,000 mg / 2,000 mL = 0.5 mg/mL. Both match the FDA table [3].
  2. 1:5,000 is 1 g per 5,000 mL. There are 50 groups of 100 mL in 5,000 mL, so each 100 mL holds 1/50 g = 0.02 g. That is 0.02%. Likewise 1:2,000 is 1 g in 20 groups of 100 mL, or 0.05 g per 100 mL: 0.05%. A shortcut: 1/5000 = 0.0002 and × 100 gives 0.02%.
  3. At 0.1 mg/mL, each mL holds 0.1 mg, so 0.5 mg needs 0.5 ÷ 0.1 = 5 mL. At 1 mg/mL, 0.5 mg needs 0.5 ÷ 1 = 0.5 mL. The volumes differ by a factor of 10, the same factor as the concentrations.

5. Diluting a solution

The third question was how to make a weaker solution from a stronger one. Adding water does it. The idea that makes the arithmetic work:

Adding water changes the volume. It does not change the amount of drug.

So a dilution problem has two steps: find the amount of drug, then spread it over the new volume. When the finished solution is given and the starting volume is unknown, the steps run the other way: find the amount of drug the finished solution needs, then find the volume of the stronger solution that holds it.

A first dilution by hand

Made-up numbers

Take 20 mL of a 5% solution. Add water until the total volume is 100 mL. Find the new concentration.

  1. Amount of drug. 5% means 5 g per 100 mL. 20 mL is one fifth of 100 mL, so it holds one fifth of 5 g, which is 1 g. As a calculation: 20 mL × (5 g / 100 mL) = 1 g.
  2. New concentration. The 1 g is now in 100 mL. That is 1 g per 100 mL, so 1%.

The volume went up by a factor of 5 (20 mL to 100 mL), and the concentration went down by the same factor of 5 (5% to 1%). The solution must come out weaker than it started, which gives a quick check on any dilution answer.

Working backward

Made-up numbers

You need 50 mL of a 1% solution, and you have a 5% solution. How much of the 5% solution do you use, and how much water do you add?

  1. Amount of drug needed. The finished 50 mL of 1% holds 50 mL × (1 g / 100 mL) = 0.5 g.
  2. Volume of the 5% solution that holds it. The 5% solution has 5 g in 100 mL. 0.5 g is one tenth of 5 g, so it is in one tenth of 100 mL, which is 10 mL.

Use 10 mL of the 5% solution and add water to a total of 50 mL: 50 − 10 = 40 mL of water. Taking the water as the difference is a simplification: for dilute solutions like these, the volumes add almost exactly.

Task 4 (about 6 minutes, pencil and paper)

Made-up numbers

  1. You have 10 mL of a 6% solution. You add water until the total is 30 mL. How many grams of drug are there, and what is the new percent?
  2. You need 60 mL of a 2% solution, and you have the 6% solution. How many mL of the 6% solution do you use, and how much water do you add?

Produce: for (a), the grams of drug and the new percent; for (b), the grams of drug needed, the mL of 6% solution, and the mL of water. Use the steps above. Do not use a formula.

Solution to Task 4
  1. Amount of drug: 10 mL × (6 g / 100 mL) = 0.6 g. New concentration: 0.6 g in 30 mL is 0.6 ÷ 30 = 0.02 g per mL, which is 2 g per 100 mL, so 2%. Another route, as in the worked example: the volume tripled (10 mL to 30 mL), so the concentration is a third of 6%, which is 2%.
  2. Work backward. The finished 60 mL of 2% must hold 60 mL × (2 g / 100 mL) = 1.2 g of drug. The 6% solution has 6 g in 100 mL. 1.2 g is one fifth of 6 g, so it is in one fifth of 100 mL, which is 20 mL of the 6% solution. Add water to bring it to 60 mL: 60 − 20 = 40 mL of water.

The dilution rule

Every dilution above follows the same pattern. The amount of drug is concentration × volume, and that amount is the same before and after adding water. Writing C for concentration and V for volume, with 1 for before and 2 for after:

C1 × V1 = C2 × V2
(concentration × volume before = concentration × volume after)

Check it on the first dilution, with concentrations in percent and volumes in mL: 5 × 20 = 100 and 1 × 100 = 100. Both sides agree. The two concentrations must use the same unit (both percent, or both mg/mL), and so must the two volumes.

When three of the four numbers are known, divide to find the fourth. For Task 4(b), C1 = 6%, C2 = 2% and V2 = 60 mL:

6 × V1 = 2 × 60V1 = 120 ÷ 6 = 20 mL

This is the same 20 mL found by reasoning.

Task 5 (about 5 minutes, calculator)

Use C1 × V1 = C2 × V2.

  1. How many mL of 0.9% sodium chloride, diluted with water to a total of 1,000 mL, give a 0.45% solution? How much water is added?
  2. How many mL of a 1 mg/mL epinephrine solution, diluted to a total of 10 mL, give a 0.1 mg/mL solution? How much water is added?
  3. 200 mL of 5% dextrose is diluted with water to a total of 1,000 mL. What is the new percent? How many grams of dextrose does the 1,000 mL contain?

Produce: for each part, the equation with numbers filled in, the answer with units, and a one-sentence check that the answer is weaker or smaller where it should be.

Solution to Task 5
  1. 0.9 × V1 = 0.45 × 1,000, so V1 = 450 ÷ 0.9 = 500 mL. Water added: 1,000 − 500 = 500 mL. Check: halving the concentration needs doubling the volume.
  2. 1 × V1 = 0.1 × 10, so V1 = 1 mL. Water added: 10 − 1 = 9 mL. Check: 1 mL of 1 mg/mL holds 1 mg, and 1 mg in 10 mL is 0.1 mg/mL. This is the 1:1,000 to 1:10,000 relationship from Section 4.
  3. 5 × 200 = C2 × 1,000, so C2 = 1,000 ÷ 1,000 = 1%. Dextrose: 200 mL × (5 g / 100 mL) = 10 g, and 1,000 mL of 1% also holds 1,000 mL × (1 g / 100 mL) = 10 g. Check: the volume grew by a factor of 5, and the percent fell from 5% to 1%.
