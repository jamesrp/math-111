Misleading graphs and analyses

Graphs and statistics reach you through news stories, ads and research summaries. A graph can show only correct numbers and still leave a wrong impression. The tools of lectures 8.1 and 8.2 (the mean x, the sample size n, scatterplots, association and the regression line) help to check such a graph or claim.

1. Bar charts with an axis that does not start at zero

In 2012, Fox News showed a bar chart titled “If Bush tax cuts expire” [1]. One bar showed the top federal income tax rate at the time, 35%. The other showed the top rate that would apply on January 1, 2013, if the tax cuts expired: 39.6%. Both numbers were correct. The vertical axis started at 34% [1].

Readers of a bar chart compare the lengths of the bars.

Task 1 (about 3 minutes, calculator)

Use the rates 35% and 39.6%. Write down three numbers:

  1. In the first chart of Figure 1, read the length of each bar from the axis, measured up from the bottom of the chart at 34%. How many times as tall is the 39.6% bar as the 35% bar?
  2. How many times as large 39.6 is as 35, to two decimal places. This is the ratio the second chart shows.
  3. The percent increase from a 35% tax rate to a 39.6% tax rate, to one decimal place.
Solution to Task 1

(a) The 35% bar has length 35 − 34 = 1 and the 39.6% bar has length 39.6 − 34 = 5.6, in units of the axis. Then 5.6 ÷ 1 = 5.6: the second bar is 5.6 times as tall.

(b) 39.6 ÷ 35 = 1.1314…, about 1.13.

(c) The increase is 39.6 − 35 = 4.6. As a share of the old rate, 4.6 ÷ 35 = 0.1314, an increase of 13.1%. The same answer comes from (b): a multiplier of 1.131 means an increase of 13.1% (week 4).

The first chart makes the new rate look more than five times the old one. The actual rate is 1.13 times the old one.

Part (c) uses two different words for change. The difference of two percentages, 39.6 − 35 = 4.6, is a change of 4.6 percentage points. The percent change divides that difference by the starting value: 4.6 ÷ 35 = 13.1%. A headline can report either one, and they sound very different: “up 4.6 points” and “up 13%” describe the same tax change.

Bar charts should start at zero, because the length of a bar stands for its value. Line graphs are different. A line graph of something that never comes near zero, such as body temperature, can start its axis elsewhere, because the reader looks at the rise and fall of the line and reads values from the axis labels. A line graph can still mislead through the shape of its frame. Stretching the vertical axis, or squeezing the horizontal axis, makes the same line look steeper. The slope in numbers (change in y ÷ change in x, from week 2) does not change. Reading the slope from the axis labels is the way to check (Section 3 has an example).

2. Pictures that grow in two or three directions

The New York Times once printed a graphic of the fuel economy standards the U.S. Department of Transportation required for new cars, from 18 miles per gallon (mpg) for 1978 models to 27.5 mpg for 1985 models. Edward Tufte measured the graphic: the line for 18 mpg was 0.6 inches long and the line for 27.5 mpg was 5.3 inches long [2][3].

Tufte defined the lie factor of a graphic as the size of a change shown in the graphic divided by the size of the change in the data [2]. Both sizes are percent changes.

  • Change in the data: (27.5 − 18) ÷ 18 = 0.528, an increase of 52.8%.
  • Change in the graphic: (5.3 − 0.6) ÷ 0.6 = 7.83, an increase of 783%.
  • Lie factor: 783 ÷ 52.8 = 14.8.

A graphic whose lengths are in proportion to the data has lie factor 1. This one shows the change about 15 times as large as it was. In Figure 2, the lower pair of lines keeps the 1985 line at 5.3 inches and makes the 1978 line the matching length, 5.3 × 18 ÷ 27.5 = 3.47 inches.

Pictures of objects cause a second problem. A chart may draw a small gas pump for 18 mpg and a larger one for 27.5 mpg, with the height of each pump matching its number. To keep the pump's shape, the artist also makes it wider by the same factor. The reader sees the area of the picture. From week 5, when every length is multiplied by a scale factor k, area is multiplied by k² and volume by k³. Figure 3 shows this for a scale factor of 1.5.

Task 2 (about 3 minutes, calculator)

A made-up pictogram shows the 1978 and 1985 standards as gas pump icons. The 1985 icon has its height and its width both multiplied by 27.5 ÷ 18. Write down:

  1. The scale factor 27.5 ÷ 18, to three decimal places.
  2. The factor by which the area of the icon grows, and the percent increase in area.
  3. The lie factor if readers judge the change by area. Use 52.8% for the change in the data.
  4. The lie factor if the icon is drawn as a 3D object and readers judge by volume.
Solution to Task 2

(a) 27.5 ÷ 18 = 1.528.

(b) Area factor: 1.528² = 2.33. The area increases by 133%.

(c) 133 ÷ 52.8 = 2.5.

(d) Volume factor: 1.528³ = 3.57, an increase of 257%. Lie factor 257 ÷ 52.8 = 4.9.

The icon's height is honest, yet a reader who compares the pictures sees a change two and a half times too large, and almost five times too large for a 3D drawing.

3. Choosing the time window

A claim that circulated for years was “It hasn't warmed since 1998” [4]. Figure 4 plots NOAA's yearly global average temperatures for 1998 through 2008 only [5]. The claim named 1998 as its start [4]; the end year 2008 is chosen here as an example of a window that ends in a cooler year. Each value is how far that year's average temperature over land and ocean was above the 1901–2000 average, in degrees Celsius (°C).

  1. Write each year as a point (x, y), with x the year and y the temperature. Find the slope from 1998 to 2008 in °C per year, using the two points (1998, 0.61) and (2008, 0.52). Multiply by 10 to get °C per decade.
  2. Find the slope from 1970 to 2020 the same way, in °C per year and in °C per decade.
Solution to Task 3

(a) (0.52 − 0.61) ÷ (2008 − 1998) = −0.09 ÷ 10 = −0.009 °C per year, or −0.09 °C per decade. Temperature went down slightly between these two years.

(b) (1.01 − 0.04) ÷ (2020 − 1970) = 0.97 ÷ 50 = 0.0194 °C per year, or about 0.19 °C per decade.

A claim of “no warming since 1998” rests on a slope like the one in (a).

Figures 4 and 5 also show how the frame of a line graph changes its look. From 1998 to 1999 the temperature fell 0.61 − 0.40 = 0.21 °C. In Figure 4, whose vertical axis covers only 0.5 °C, that drop takes up 0.21 ÷ 0.5 = 42% of the height of the chart. In Figure 5, whose axis covers 1.6 °C, the same drop takes up 0.21 ÷ 1.6 = 13%, about an eighth.

In Figure 5 the year 1998 stands well above the years around it. A strong El Niño, a periodic warming of the tropical Pacific Ocean, made 1998 unusually warm [4]. In the NOAA data, 8 of the 14 years from 1999 through 2012 were cooler than 1998, and 2005 was the first year warmer than 1998 [5]. A window that starts at an unusually high year and ends at a low one can make almost any rising series look flat.

Lecture 8.2 gives a better tool than two points: the least-squares regression line y = mx + b, which uses every year in the window. For 1998–2008, its slope is m = 0.012 °C per year, or 0.12 °C per decade, even though the two endpoints fall. For 1970–2025, it is 0.019 °C per year, or 0.19 °C per decade. The five warmest years in the record are 2024, 2023, 2025, 2020 and 2016 [5]. Two endpoint values can be chosen to tell either story. A regression line over a long window is much harder to steer.

4. Correlation, causation and a confounding variable

In May 1999, researchers at the Children's Hospital of Philadelphia reported a study of 479 children aged 2 to 16 [6]. Among children who had slept in darkness before age 2, 10% were nearsighted (myopic). Among those who had slept with a night light, 34% were. Among those who had slept with the room light on, 55% were, 5.5 times the rate for darkness [6]. The hospital's news release said that children who sleep with a light on before age 2 “may be at significantly higher risk” of nearsightedness [6].

In 2000, two other research groups reported similar rates of nearsightedness for children who had and had not slept with a light before age 2. They also noted that nearsighted parents were more likely to leave a light on in their child's room [7]. Children of nearsighted parents are at higher risk of nearsightedness themselves [8]. So the parents' nearsightedness can raise both the chance of a night light and the chance that the child is nearsighted.

A confounding variable is a third variable that affects both the variable thought of as the cause and the outcome. Here the suspected cause is the night light, the outcome is the child's nearsightedness, and the confounding variable is the parents' nearsightedness. A confounding variable can produce a strong association between two things when neither one causes the other. In lecture 8.2 an association was a tilt in a scatterplot, measured by r; with yes/no groups it shows up as a difference in percentages. Lecture 8.2 (Section 6) gave a warning of the same kind: richer countries differ from poorer ones in other ways that affect health, so the regression line does not show that more income causes longer lives.

Solution to Task 4

(a) Group 1: 60 ÷ 150 = 40% with a light, 20 ÷ 50 = 40% without. Group 2: 5 ÷ 50 = 10% with a light, 15 ÷ 150 = 10% without.

(b) With a light: (60 + 5) ÷ (150 + 50) = 65 ÷ 200 = 32.5%. Without a light: (20 + 15) ÷ (50 + 150) = 35 ÷ 200 = 17.5%.

(c) Within each parent group the light makes no difference, yet the combined numbers show nearly twice the rate with a light. The combined difference comes from the fact that most children who slept with a light (150 of 200) have a nearsighted parent.

Comparing within groups, as in part (a), is one way researchers check for a confounding variable they have measured. It works only for confounders someone thought to record. The stronger check is an experiment that assigns the suspected cause at random, for example by a coin flip. Random assignment makes the two groups alike on average in every other way, measured or not. The 1999 study observed children and did not assign night lights, so it could show an association and could not settle the cause [6].

5. Small samples

In 1974, Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman published this question [9]:

A certain town is served by two hospitals. In the larger hospital about 45 babies are born each day, and in the smaller hospital about 15 babies are born each day. As you know, about 50 percent of all babies are boys. However, the exact percentage varies from day to day. Sometimes it may be higher than 50 percent, sometimes lower. For a period of 1 year, each hospital recorded the days on which more than 60 percent of the babies born were boys. Which hospital do you think recorded more such days?

Of 95 undergraduate students, 21 answered “the larger hospital”, 21 answered “the smaller hospital” and 53 answered “about the same” [9].

The share of boys on one day is a mean in the sense of lecture 8.1. Give each baby the value 1 for a boy and 0 for a girl. With n babies born that day, the share of boys is the mean x of these n values. The two hospitals differ only in the sample size: n = 45 a day and n = 15 a day.

Each press of a button below plays out one year (365 days) at a hospital, using the simplification that each baby is a boy with probability exactly 0.5, independently of the others. Each bar counts the days on which a given percent of the babies were boys. Days with more than 60% boys are drawn in orange, to the right of the dashed line. A day with exactly 60% boys (9 of 15, or 27 of 45) does not count.

Press one of the buttons above.

Task 5 (about 3 minutes, the simulation above on a phone or laptop)

Press “15 births a day” three times and write down the number of days with more than 60% boys each time. Then do the same with “45 births a day”. Write one sentence saying which hospital records more such days, and about how many times as many.

Solution to Task 5

Simulated counts change from run to run. With n = 15 births a day, most years give between about 40 and 70 days. With n = 45, most years give between about 15 and 35 days. The smaller hospital records roughly twice as many days with more than 60% boys.

Week 7 counting gives the exact chance. With 15 births, “more than 60% boys” means 10 or more boys, since 9 of 15 is exactly 60%. Each of the 215 = 32,768 boy–girl sequences is equally likely, and the number of sequences with exactly k boys is the number of combinations C(15, k), the nCr key on many calculators:

C(15,10) + C(15,11) + C(15,12) + C(15,13) + C(15,14) + C(15,15) = 3003 + 1365 + 455 + 105 + 15 + 1 = 4944.

Probability: 4944 ÷ 32,768 = 0.151. Expected days: 0.151 × 365 = 55 days.

With 45 births, the day needs 28 or more boys. The same sum, with more terms, gives a probability of 0.068, so about 0.068 × 365 = 25 days.

A mean x computed from a small sample size n moves around much more by chance than one computed from a large n. The smaller hospital has more extreme days in both directions, above 60% boys and below 40%. Real rankings show the same pattern: the U.S. counties with the lowest and with the highest kidney cancer rates are both mostly small rural counties, and small schools crowd both the top and the bottom of a ranking by average test score [10].

6. Base rates and screening tests

A screening mammogram is an X-ray of the breast given to women with no symptoms, to look for breast cancer. A large study from the Breast Cancer Surveillance Consortium looked at 1,682,504 screening mammograms done in the United States from 2007 to 2013 [11]. It reports two numbers that describe how good the test is.

  • The sensitivity is the percent of women who have cancer whose test comes back positive. For screening mammograms it was 86.9% [11]. Out of 1,000 women with cancer, about 869 test positive.
  • The specificity is the percent of women who do not have cancer whose test comes back negative. It was 88.9% [11]. Out of 1,000 women without cancer, about 889 test negative and the other 111 test positive.

A true positive is a positive test for someone with the condition. A false positive is a positive test for someone without the condition, and a false negative is a negative test for someone with it.

Both numbers are close to 90%, so the test could be called “about 90% accurate”. The question a patient with a positive result has is a third number: given a positive test, what is the probability of cancer? That number depends on the base rate, the percent of the people tested who have the condition (also called the prevalence).

The study found 5.1 cancers per 1,000 screens, and the test finds 86.9% of the cancers that are there [11]. So about 5.1 ÷ 0.869 = 5.87 women per 1,000 screened had cancer. We round this to 5.9 per 1,000, which is 59 women out of 10,000. This is a simplification: it treats the cancers the study counted as all the cancers present.

Of the 1,154 positive tests, 51 are women with cancer: 51 ÷ 1,154 = 0.044, or 4.4%. The other 1,103 ÷ 1,154 = 95.6% of positive tests are false positives.

The study itself reports that 4.4% of positive screening mammograms led to a cancer diagnosis [11], the same as the table. It also reports that 11.6% of screens were positive [11]; the table gives 1,154 ÷ 10,000 = 11.5%, close to that.

The test is right for (51 + 8,838) ÷ 10,000 = 88.9% of women, yet most of its positives are false because of the base rate. Its 11.1% error rate for women without cancer applies to a large group, 9,941 women, and produces 1,103 false positives. Its 86.9% sensitivity applies to a small group, 59 women, and produces 51 true positives. A negative result, on the other hand, is very reliable here: 8,838 of the 8,846 negatives, or 99.9%, are women without cancer. A positive screening result leads to further tests, which sort the 51 from the 1,103.

In the language of week 7, the table finds a probability by counting: P(cancer, given a positive test) = (positives with cancer) ÷ (all positives). The same method works for any test. With made-up round numbers, a test with 90% sensitivity and 90% specificity, used where 1% of people have the condition, gives 90 true positives and 990 false positives out of 10,000 people, so 90 ÷ 1,080 = 8.3% of positives are correct.

Solution to Task 7

With B3 = 0.0059, B13 = 51.271 ÷ 1,154.722 = 0.0444, or 4.4%. The spreadsheet does not round the counts, so its answer can differ slightly from the rounded table.

(a) B3 = 0.1: 869 true positives and 999 false positives, so B13 = 869 ÷ 1,868 = 0.465, or 46.5%.

The test is the same in all three cases. Only the base rate changed, and the chance that a positive result is correct went from under 1% to almost one half.

7. Questions to ask before believing a graph or statistic

  1. Where does the vertical axis start, and are the bar lengths in proportion to the numbers? (Section 1)
  2. Is the change reported in percentage points or in percent? (Section 1)
  3. Does the size of a picture grow in more than one direction, so its area or volume exaggerates the change? (Section 2)
  4. Why does the time window start and end where it does, and what does a regression line over a longer window show? (Section 3)
  5. Was this an experiment with random assignment, or an observation in which a confounding variable could explain the pattern? (Section 4)
  6. What is the sample size n behind each mean or percent: how many people, days or places? (Section 5)
  7. For a test result, what is the base rate in the group being tested? (Section 6)
