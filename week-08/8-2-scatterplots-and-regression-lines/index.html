Scatterplots and regression lines

1. Income and life expectancy in ten South American countries

Table 1 gives two measurements for each of the ten South American countries for which the source gives both values for 2022 [1]. (Guyana and Suriname have no GDP figure for 2022 in the source.) The number of countries is n = 10, using n for the number of data values as in lecture 8.1; here each value is a pair of measurements.

  • GDP per person is the value of everything a country produced in a year, divided by its population. It is given in thousands of dollars and is adjusted for prices, so that a dollar buys the same amount in every country (the source uses 2011 prices) [1, 2]. Because the figures are in 2011 dollars, they are lower than dollar amounts from today: the United States, for example, shows as $58.5 thousand. GDP per person is a mean over the whole population, in the sense of lecture 8.1. It measures a country's income as a whole and does not describe the income of a typical person.
  • Life expectancy is the number of years a baby born in 2022 would live, on average, if the death rates at each age stayed as they were in 2022 [1].

Both columns are rounded to one decimal place. Every calculation in this lecture uses the rounded numbers.

Task 1 (about 3 minutes, pencil and paper)

Use Table 1. Write down the three countries with the highest GDP per person and the three with the lowest, each with its life expectancy. Then answer two questions in one sentence each.

  1. Do the three richest countries have longer life expectancy than the three poorest?
  2. Find one pair of countries in which the richer country has the shorter life expectancy.
Solution to Task 1

Highest GDP per person: Chile ($22.7 thousand, 79.2 years), Uruguay (20.2, 76.5), Argentina (18.3, 75.8).
Lowest GDP per person: Venezuela ($5.3 thousand, 72.6 years), Bolivia (6.5, 67.4), Paraguay (8.8, 72.3).

(a) Yes. Each of the three richest countries has a longer life expectancy than each of the three poorest. The shortest of the richest three, 75.8 years, is longer than the longest of the poorest three, 72.6 years.

(b) Several pairs work. Bolivia is richer than Venezuela (6.5 against 5.3) but has the shorter life expectancy (67.4 against 72.6). Uruguay is richer than Peru (20.2 against 12.8) but has the shorter life expectancy (76.5 against 76.8). Brazil and Ecuador are another such pair.

So the two measurements tend to rise together, with exceptions. Comparing pairs one at a time does not say how strong the tendency is or how much life expectancy goes up per $1,000.

2. Scatterplots and association

Each country gives a coordinate pair (x, y), as in week 2: x is GDP per person and y is life expectancy. Chile is the point (22.7, 79.2). A scatterplot plots one point for each country. The variable used to make predictions goes on the horizontal axis. Here we predict life expectancy from GDP per person, so GDP is x.

In Figure 1, countries further right tend to be higher. This is a positive association: larger values of x go with larger values of y. A negative association is the opposite: larger values of x go with smaller values of y, and the points drift downward from left to right.

The points do not lie on a line. Bolivia sits well below the others and Ecuador and Peru sit above. An association is a tendency of the whole cloud of points, and single countries can go against it, as Task 1 found.

3. The correlation coefficient r

The correlation coefficient, written r, is a single number that measures how close the points of a scatterplot are to a straight line. A spreadsheet computes it with the function CORREL. We read r and do not calculate it by hand. To read it:

  • r is always between −1 and 1.
  • The sign gives the direction. A positive r means positive association. A negative r means negative association.
  • The size gives the closeness to a line. When r = 1 or r = −1 every point lies exactly on a straight line. When r is near 0 the points show no straight-line pattern: no tilted line fits them better than a flat one.

For the ten countries in Table 1, r = 0.76. Section 5 computes this in a spreadsheet.

The six panels of Figure 2 are made-up points, generated by a computer to have particular values of r. They are practice for reading r, and they do not describe any real measurements.

A: −0.5. B: 0.95. D: 0. E: −0.9. F: 0.5.

The negative values go with the panels that slope down, A and E. Panel E is the closer of the two to a line, so it gets −0.9. Among the panels that slope up, B is almost a line, so it gets 0.95, and F is the looser one, 0.5. Panel D shows no tilt at all, so it gets 0.

The correlation coefficient only measures closeness to a straight line, so always look at the scatterplot as well as r.

4. Residuals and the least-squares line

A straight line y = mx + b drawn through a scatterplot works as a prediction rule: put in a GDP per person x and the line gives a predicted life expectancy mx + b. The predicted value is written y, read "y-hat", so y = mx + b. For a country in the data we also know the actual life expectancy. The difference is the residual:

residual = actual y − predicted y = y − y.

A positive residual means the country lies above the line: it lives longer than the line predicts. A negative residual means it lies below the line. On a graph the residual is the vertical distance from the point to the line.

Task 3 (about 5 minutes, calculator)

One way to draw a line through Figure 1 is to join two of the points. Use Venezuela (5.3, 72.6) and Chile (22.7, 79.2).

  1. Find the slope of the line through these two points, as in week 2. Round it to two decimal places.
  2. Find the intercept b so that the line passes through Venezuela, using your rounded slope. Round b to two decimal places and write the equation y = mx + b.
  3. Use your line to find the predicted life expectancy and the residual for Bolivia (6.5, 67.4) and for Ecuador (10.1, 76.6). Say whether each country lies above or below the line.
Solution to Task 3

(a) Slope = (change in y) ÷ (change in x) = (79.2 − 72.6) ÷ (22.7 − 5.3) = 6.6 ÷ 17.4 = 0.3793…, which rounds to 0.38. In units: 0.38 years of life expectancy per $1,000 of GDP per person.

(b) The line passes through Venezuela, so 72.6 = 0.38 × 5.3 + b. Then b = 72.6 − 2.014 = 70.586, which rounds to 70.59. The line is y = 0.38x + 70.59.

(c) Bolivia: predicted = 0.38 × 6.5 + 70.59 = 2.47 + 70.59 = 73.06 years. Residual = 67.4 − 73.06 = −5.66 years. Bolivia lies 5.66 years below the line.
Ecuador: predicted = 0.38 × 10.1 + 70.59 = 3.838 + 70.59 = 74.428, about 74.43 years. Residual = 76.6 − 74.43 = 2.17 years. Ecuador lies 2.17 years above the line.

Sum of squared residuals

Many lines pass through the cloud of points, and each one gives ten residuals. To pick the best line we need one number that says how badly a line misses.

Adding up the residuals does not work, because positive and negative residuals cancel. A flat line at height 74.86, the mean life expectancy of the ten countries (the mean of lecture 8.1, written y for the y values), has residuals that add up to exactly 0, yet it ignores income completely.

Squaring each residual fixes this. A square is never negative, so misses above and below the line both count. Squaring also makes a large miss count much more than a small one: a residual of 4 years contributes 16, while a residual of 1 year contributes 1. The score of a line is the sum of squared residuals: square each of the ten residuals and add the squares. A smaller sum means a line that fits better.

For the Venezuela–Chile line of Task 3, the sum of squared residuals is 49.09. For the flat line at 74.86 it is 99.20. The line from Task 3 fits much better than the flat line. Task 4 asks whether any line does better still.

Task 4 (about 5 minutes, Figure 3)

Figure 3 starts at the line from Task 3, with a sum of squared residuals of 49.09. Move the two sliders to make the sum of squared residuals as small as you can. Solution to Task 4

The smallest possible sum of squared residuals is 41.54. It is reached by the line

y = 0.4348x + 69.04.

The sliders move in steps of 0.01, so the best they can reach is a sum of about 41.55, with slope 0.43 and intercept 69.11. Any answer close to these is a good fit. A sum near 41.5 can be reached with slightly different pairs of slope and intercept, because raising the slope and lowering the intercept together keeps the line in nearly the same place over the data.

Adjusting by hand is slow, since changing the slope moves the whole line and the intercept has to be corrected each time. A spreadsheet finds the best line directly (Section 5).

The least-squares regression line

The line with the smallest possible sum of squared residuals is called the least-squares regression line, or regression line for short. For the ten countries of Table 1 it is y = 0.4348x + 69.04. Spreadsheets compute its slope and intercept with the functions SLOPE and INTERCEPT (Section 5). We do not derive the formulas in this course, but they use the summary statistics of lecture 8.1:

  • The slope is r × (standard deviation of y) ÷ (standard deviation of x), with each standard deviation s computed as in lecture 8.1, dividing by n − 1. For Table 1 the standard deviation of GDP per person is 5.822 thousand dollars and that of life expectancy is 3.320 years, so the slope is 0.7624 × 3.320 ÷ 5.822 = 0.4348.
  • The regression line always passes through the point (x, y), the means of the x values and of the y values. Here x = 13.38 thousand dollars and y = 74.86 years, and 0.4348 × 13.38 + 69.04 = 74.86.

Notation. These symbols mean the same thing in this lecture and in lecture 8.3. The symbols n, x and s are those of lecture 8.1.

(x, y): one data point. x is the variable used to predict (here GDP per person) and y the variable predicted (here life expectancy).

n: the number of data points. Here n = 10.

x and y ("x-bar" and "y-bar"): the mean of the x values and the mean of the y values.

r: the correlation coefficient, between −1 and 1.

y = mx + b: the least-squares regression line, with slope m and intercept b, as in week 2.

y ("y-hat"): the predicted value, y = mx + b, the height of the line at x.

Residual: y − y, actual minus predicted. Positive above the line, negative below it.

6. Reading the slope and making predictions

The regression line is

predicted life expectancy = 0.4348 × (GDP per person in thousands of dollars) + 69.04.

Rounded, the slope is 0.43. As in week 2, a slope is a rate with units: units of y per unit of x. Here that is 0.43 years of life expectancy per $1,000 of GDP per person. Among these ten countries, a country with $1,000 more GDP per person has, on average, a life expectancy about 0.43 years longer. A difference of $5,000 goes with about 5 × 0.43 = 2.15, or roughly 2 years.

This describes a comparison between countries in 2022. It does not say that a country whose income rises by $1,000 will gain 0.43 years. Richer countries also differ from poorer ones in other ways that affect health, and the line does not separate these.

The intercept, 69.04, is the height of the line at x = 0, a country with no income at all. No country in the data is anywhere near $0; the poorest, Venezuela, has $5.3 thousand. So the intercept is a number that places the line, and it should not be read as a fact about very poor countries.

Task 6 (about 4 minutes, calculator)

Use the line y = 0.43x + 69.04, the regression line with its slope rounded to 0.43 for use on a calculator. For x from 5.3 to 22.7 its predictions differ from those of the regression line by at most 0.11 years. Two countries outside South America had these values in 2022 [1]:

  • Costa Rica: GDP per person 15.5 thousand dollars, life expectancy 79.3 years.
  • United States: GDP per person 58.5 thousand dollars, life expectancy 78.0 years.

For each country, compute the predicted life expectancy and the residual. Then write one sentence on which prediction you trust more, and why.

Solution to Task 6

Costa Rica: predicted = 0.43 × 15.5 + 69.04 = 6.665 + 69.04 = 75.705, about 75.7 years. Residual = 79.3 − 75.7 = 3.6 years.

United States: predicted = 0.43 × 58.5 + 69.04 = 25.155 + 69.04 = 94.195, about 94.2 years. Residual = 78.0 − 94.2 = −16.2 years.

The Costa Rica prediction is the more trustworthy one. Costa Rica's GDP per person, 15.5, lies inside the range of the data the line was fitted to (5.3 to 22.7), and the miss of 3.6 years is about the size of the residuals of the ten South American countries (−4.47 to 3.17, Table 2). The United States, at 58.5, is far outside that range, and the line misses by 16 years. Section 7 explains why.

7. How far to trust a prediction

Inside the range of the data. The line was fitted to GDP values from 5.3 to 22.7 thousand dollars. For those ten countries the residuals run from −4.47 (Bolivia) to 3.17 (Ecuador). A prediction for another country in that income range can reasonably be expected to miss by a few years, as Costa Rica's did. The correlation of 0.76 says the points are fairly close to the line, but not on it.

Outside the range of the data. Using a line to predict for x values outside the range it was fitted to is called extrapolation. The line itself says nothing about what happens there. To check, compare the line with the rest of the world's countries in Figure 4.

Across the world, life expectancy rises steeply at low incomes and then levels off. The 24 countries with GDP per person above $40,000 all have life expectancies between 77.3 and 83.5 years. The straight line keeps climbing. For Qatar, with GDP per person of 149.2 thousand dollars, the regression line predicts 0.4348 × 149.2 + 69.04 = 133.9 years. Qatar's actual life expectancy in 2022 was 81.9 years [1].

The world data also show why r alone is not enough. For all 164 countries in Figure 4, r = 0.70, which sounds like a fairly strong straight-line relationship. The scatterplot shows a curve, which no straight line can follow closely.
