Linear equations from a nutrition target

1. Five breakfast foods and their protein

A student wants about 30 grams of protein at breakfast. The 30 g is the student's own target. They want to reach it with foods they already buy: rolled oats, eggs, plain Greek yogurt, 2% milk and peanut butter. Two questions come up right away. How much of each food reaches 30 g? How many different combinations reach it?

Packaged foods sold in the US must carry a Nutrition Facts label, unless they qualify for an exemption [6]. The label lists a serving size and the grams of protein in one serving. The values here come from USDA FoodData Central, a public database run by the US Department of Agriculture [1–5]. The database gives each nutrient per 100 g of food, plus the weight of common servings such as "1 cup" or "1 large egg".

The peanut butter record is for unsalted peanut butter; if your jar has added salt, use the protein figure on its label. Using one set of average values is a simplification. The same method works with the numbers from your own labels.

2. Protein as a rate

"19.900 g of protein per container" is a rate: an amount of one thing (protein) for each unit of another thing (containers of yogurt). In week 1 you wrote rates as fractions, such as 19.900 g protein / 1 container. The same food can have its rate written per serving or per gram.

Reading a label as a rate

A Nutrition Facts label gives the protein in one serving and the weight of that serving in grams. Here is an example label line, made up for this example:

Serving size 1/2 cup (40 g) … Protein 5 g

The rate is protein divided by serving weight:

5 g protein / 40 g food = 5 ÷ 40 = 0.125 g protein per gram of food.

Labels give protein to the nearest gram [6]. A label that says 5 g could mean any amount from 4.5 g up to 5.5 g, so a rate worked out from a label is less exact than its three decimals suggest.

Rates per 100 g

To turn "13.2 g protein per 100 g oats" into a rate per gram, divide by 100:

13.2 g protein / 100 g oats = 0.132 g protein per gram of oats.

Then the protein in any amount of oats is

protein = amount × rate.

For 60 g of oats: 60 g × 0.132 g protein per g = 7.92 g protein. The grams of oats cancel, as in the unit conversions of week 1, and grams of protein are left.

When a breakfast has several foods, the protein from each food adds up to the total.

Task 1 (about 4 minutes, calculator)

  1. One container of Greek yogurt weighs 200 g and has 19.900 g of protein (Table 1). Write its protein rate in grams of protein per gram of yogurt. Use the rate to find the protein in 150 g of yogurt.
  2. Find the total protein in this breakfast: 1/2 cup of oats cooked in 1 cup of 2% milk, plus 1 large egg. Use Table 1. How many grams short of 30 g is it?
Solution to Task 1
  1. 19.900 g per 200 g is 19.900 ÷ 200 = 0.0995 g of protein per gram of yogurt. (Dividing the per-100 g value gives the same rate: 9.95 ÷ 100 = 0.0995.) For 150 g: 150 × 0.0995 = 14.925, about 14.9 g of protein.
  2. Oats 5.346 g + milk 8.052 g + egg 6.300 g = 19.698 g, about 19.7 g. That is 30 − 19.698 = 10.302 g, about 10.3 g, short of the target.

3. Reaching 30 grams by trial and error

One way to make overnight oats is to stir rolled oats into yogurt. Take half a cup of oats (40.5 g, 5.346 g of protein) and ask how much Greek yogurt brings the total to 30 g.

A first approach is to guess an amount of yogurt, compute the total, and adjust. With 200 g of yogurt the total is 5.346 + 0.0995 × 200 = 25.246 g, too little, so the next guess should be larger.

Task 2 (about 3 minutes, calculator)

Use 40.5 g of oats. Try amounts of yogurt in steps of 10 g (210 g, 220 g, …) until the total protein is between 29.5 g and 30.5 g. Write each guess and its total in a two-column list. Then say how you would get a total of exactly 30 g, with no rounding.

Two more rounds reach 247.8 g of yogurt, with a total of 30.0021 g. That shows as 30.0 to one decimal place, but it is still not exactly 30, and the search never ends on its own.

Trial and error found an answer, but slowly. It also has to start over for every new amount of oats. An equation sets up the calculation once, so it can be solved directly.

4. Writing the protein equation

A variable is a letter that stands for an amount we do not know yet, or an amount we want to change. Let

  • x = grams of rolled oats, dry,
  • y = grams of Greek yogurt.

Protein from oats is amount × rate = x × 0.132. Protein from yogurt is y × 0.0995. Together they must make 30 g:

0.132 × x + 0.0995 × y = 30

The usual short way to write a number times a variable is to put them side by side, so 0.132 × x is written 0.132x:

0.132x + 0.0995y = 30

This is an equation: a statement that two amounts are equal. Here the left side is the protein in the breakfast, in grams, and the right side is the target. A solution is a pair of amounts that makes the statement true. For example, x = 0 and y = 301.5 is very nearly a solution, because 0.132 × 0 + 0.0995 × 301.5 = 29.999. The pair x = 40.5, y = 250 from Task 2 is close but is not a solution, since it gives 30.221.

The student's target is about 30 g. Half a cup of oats with 250 g of yogurt is a perfectly good breakfast even though it is not a solution. The equation gives the exact amounts, and the student then rounds them to amounts that are easy to measure.

The same recipe works for any two foods:

(amount of food 1) × (rate of food 1) + (amount of food 2) × (rate of food 2) = target.

Each rate must match the unit of its amount. If the amount of yogurt is in grams, the rate must be per gram (0.0995). If it is in containers, the rate must be per container (19.900).

5. Solving for one amount when the other is fixed

Once you choose the amount of oats, the equation has only one unknown left, and you can find it exactly. The rule for solving is: do the same thing to both sides. If two amounts are equal and you subtract the same number from both, they are still equal. If you divide both by the same number (other than zero), they are still equal. Each step below uses one of these moves to get y alone on one side.

Worked example: half a cup of oats

Fix x = 40.5 g. Before solving, estimate: the oats give about 5 g, so the yogurt must give about 25 g. Yogurt has about 10 g of protein per 100 g, so the answer should be near 250 g.

0.132 × 40.5 + 0.0995y = 30Put 40.5 in place of x.
5.346 + 0.0995y = 30Multiply: 0.132 × 40.5 = 5.346 g of protein from oats.
0.0995y = 24.654Subtract 5.346 from both sides. The yogurt must supply 24.654 g.
y = 24.654 ÷ 0.0995Divide both sides by 0.0995.
y = 247.78…Calculator.

Check by putting the answer back into the original equation: 0.132 × 40.5 + 0.0995 × 247.78 = 5.346 + 24.654 = 30.000. The estimate of 250 g was close, so the answer is the right size.

Answer: half a cup of oats with about 248 g of Greek yogurt gives 30 g of protein. That is 247.78 ÷ 200 = 1.24 containers, a little more than one container.

The steps undo what the equation does to y. The equation multiplies y by 0.0995 and then adds 5.346. Solving takes these away in reverse order: subtract 5.346 first, then divide by 0.0995.

Task 3 (about 8 minutes, calculator)

For each part, solve the equation one step at a time and write the reason for each step. The equation is given for part a; write it yourself for parts b and c. Round final answers to two decimal places. Then check: put the rounded answer back into the equation. A total that differs from 30 by a few hundredths comes from the rounding.

  1. Eggs and milk. Drink 1 cup of 2% milk (8.052 g of protein). Let e be the number of large eggs (6.300 g each). Solve 6.30e + 8.052 = 30. What does the answer tell you, since eggs come whole?
  2. Yogurt and oats. Eat one full container of yogurt (200 g). How many grams of oats x reach 30 g? Convert your answer to cups, using 1 cup of dry oats = 81 g.
  3. If you finish early: 2 large eggs, 3/4 cup of 2% milk and t tablespoons of peanut butter. One tablespoon has half the protein of the 2-tablespoon serving in Table 1. Use Table 1 for the eggs and milk too. Find t.
Solution to Task 3

a.

6.30e + 8.052 = 30Protein from eggs plus protein from milk equals the target.
6.30e = 21.948Subtract 8.052 from both sides.
e = 21.948 ÷ 6.30 = 3.48Divide both sides by 6.30.

Check with the rounded answer: 6.30 × 3.48 + 8.052 = 29.976, about 29.98. The 0.02 gap comes from rounding e. Eggs come whole, so no real breakfast hits 30 g exactly with these two foods. The answer 3.48 says 3 eggs fall short (6.30 × 3 + 8.052 = 26.95 g) and 4 eggs go over (33.25 g).

b.

0.132x + 0.0995 × 200 = 30Put 200 in place of y.
0.132x + 19.9 = 30Multiply: one container has 19.9 g.
0.132x = 10.1Subtract 19.9 from both sides.
x = 10.1 ÷ 0.132 = 76.52Divide both sides by 0.132.

Check with the rounded answer: 0.132 × 76.52 + 19.9 = 30.0006, about 30.00. In cups: 76.52 g × (1 cup / 81 g) = 0.94 cup, a little less than 1 cup of dry oats.

c. One tablespoon of peanut butter has 7.104 ÷ 2 = 3.552 g of protein. Three quarters of a cup of milk has 3/4 × 8.052 = 6.039 g. Two eggs have 2 × 6.300 = 12.6 g.

12.6 + 6.039 + 3.552t = 30Eggs plus milk plus peanut butter equals the target.
18.639 + 3.552t = 30Add the known amounts.
3.552t = 11.361Subtract 18.639 from both sides.
t = 11.361 ÷ 3.552 = 3.20Divide both sides by 3.552.

Check with the rounded answer: 12.6 + 6.039 + 3.552 × 3.20 = 30.0054, about 30.01. The 0.01 gap comes from rounding t (before rounding, t is about 3.1985). About 3 1/5 tablespoons of peanut butter.

6. Many combinations by hand

To see the whole range of choices, repeat the calculation for several amounts of oats and put the results in a table. The steps are the same as in the worked example, so each row has three columns of arithmetic:

  • protein from oats = 0.132 × (oats),
  • protein still needed = 30 − (protein from oats),
  • yogurt needed = (protein still needed) ÷ 0.0995.

8. How many combinations reach 30 grams

For every amount of oats there is exactly one amount of yogurt that brings the total to 30 g. The amount of oats can be any number from 0 g up to the amount that gives 30 g by itself, 30 ÷ 0.132 = 227.27 g (about 2.8 cups). It does not have to be a whole number or a multiple of 10: 40 g, 40.5 g and 40.25 g all work, each with its own amount of yogurt. Between any two amounts of oats there is always another one, so there are infinitely many combinations that reach 30 g. No table, however long, lists them all. The upper end, 227.27 g, is a limit of the equation. At 379 kcal per 100 g [1], 227 g of dry oats is about 860 kcal, which is not a sensible breakfast; calories come back in lectures 2.2 and 2.3.

Every extra gram of oats needs 0.132 ÷ 0.0995 = 1.33 g less yogurt, wherever you start. That is why the yogurt column in Tasks 4 and 5 went down by the same amount in every row: 66.33 g per 50 g of oats, and 26.53 g per 20 g of oats.

Each combination is a pair of numbers (oats, yogurt), such as (0, 301.51), (40.5, 247.78) and (100, 168.84), with yogurt rounded to two decimal places. If each pair is plotted as a point on a graph, with oats across and yogurt up, the constant drop suggests the points lie on a straight line. Lecture 2.2 plots them and checks whether they do.
