Two equations in two unknowns

1. Two targets for one breakfast

In lecture 2.1 the target was 30 g of protein, and infinitely many combinations of oats and Greek yogurt reached it. A breakfast also has calories. Suppose the student wants 30 g of protein and 400 calories, from rolled oats and plain Greek yogurt. The 30 g is the target from lecture 2.1, and the 400 kcal is the student's own choice for this example.

The foods and values are the ones in Table 1 of lecture 2.1, from USDA FoodData Central [1–5].

Let x be the number of servings of oats (half-cups, dry) and y the number of containers of yogurt. Each target gives an equation, written the way lecture 2.1 wrote the protein target:

protein:   5.346x + 19.9y = 30 calories:   153x + 146y = 400

A breakfast meets both targets when the same pair (x, y) makes both equations true. Lecture 2.1 already gives a way to search: choose x, solve the protein equation for y, and see what the calories come to.

2. Two targets as two lines

Lecture 2.2 showed that all the solutions of a linear equation such as 5.346x + 19.9y = 30 form a straight line. The calorie equation also has a line. In Figure 1 the blue line holds every breakfast with exactly 30 g of protein, and the orange line every breakfast with exactly 400 calories.

A breakfast that meets both targets is a point on both lines. Two lines with different slopes cross at exactly one point. That crossing point is called the intersection of the lines. A pair of equations that must both be true at the same time is called a system of equations, and the coordinates of the intersection are the solution of the system.

Task 2 (about 4 minutes, Figure 1 on a phone or laptop, calculator)

Produce: (a) the protein and calories at the point (1, 1), and whether that point is above or below each line; (b) the amounts x and y, to two decimal places, at the point where you get closest to 30 g and 400 kcal at once; (c) the two intercepts of each line, to two decimals, and the slope of each line computed from its intercepts. Figure 1 shows roughly where the intercepts are; compute them by setting x = 0, then y = 0, as in lecture 2.2.

Solution to Task 2

(a) At (1, 1): protein 5.346 + 19.9 = 25.246 g, about 25.2 g; calories 153 + 146 = 299 kcal. At x = 1 the blue line is at y ≈ 1.24 and the orange line at y ≈ 1.69, so the point is below both lines. It is short on both targets.

(b) The lines cross near x = 1.58, y = 1.08. Answers within about 0.05 of these are as close as dragging allows on a phone; the arrow keys get closer. The exact values come from Section 3.

(c) Blue line: with x = 0, y = 30 ÷ 19.9 ≈ 1.51; with y = 0, x = 30 ÷ 5.346 ≈ 5.61. The intercepts are (0, 1.51) and (5.61, 0), so slope = (0 − 1.51) ÷ (5.61 − 0) ≈ −0.27 containers of yogurt per serving of oats. Each extra serving of oats lets you drop about 0.27 containers of yogurt and keep 30 g of protein. Orange line: 400 ÷ 146 ≈ 2.74 and 400 ÷ 153 ≈ 2.61, so the intercepts are (0, 2.74) and (2.61, 0), and slope = (0 − 2.74) ÷ (2.61 − 0) ≈ −1.05. Each extra serving of oats means about 1.05 fewer containers of yogurt to stay at 400 kcal. The slopes are different, so the lines cross.

Reading the intersection off a graph is called solving by graphing. It shows whether there is a solution and roughly where. Its accuracy is limited by how carefully you can read the graph, here about one or two hundredths.

3. Solving by substitution

Substitution finds the intersection exactly. One equation tells you y in terms of x. Put that expression in place of y in the other equation. The result is one equation with one unknown, which you solve as in lecture 2.1.

Worked example: oats and yogurt

Step 1. Solve one equation for y. Start from the protein equation and write it in the slope-intercept form y = mx + b from lecture 2.2.

5.346x + 19.9y = 30 19.9y = −5.346x + 30 subtract 5.346x from both sides y = −(5.346 ÷ 19.9)x + 30 ÷ 19.9 ≈ −0.2686x + 1.5075 divide both sides by 19.9

The slope is m ≈ −0.2686 and the y-intercept is b ≈ 1.5075, the blue line of Figure 1.

Step 2. Substitute into the other equation. In the calorie equation, replace y by −0.2686x + 1.5075. The parentheses matter: 146 multiplies the whole expression.

153x + 146(−0.2686x + 1.5075) = 400

Step 3. Solve for x.

153x − 39.22x + 220.10 = 400 146 × 0.2686 ≈ 39.22 and 146 × 1.5075 ≈ 220.10 113.78x + 220.10 = 400 153 − 39.22 = 113.78 113.78x = 179.90 subtract 220.10 from both sides x = 179.90 ÷ 113.78 ≈ 1.5811 divide both sides by 113.78

Step 4. Find y. Put x into the expression from Step 1.

y ≈ −0.2686 × 1.5811 + 1.5075 ≈ 1.0828

Step 5. Check in both equations. With x = 1.5811 and y = 1.0828:

protein: 5.346 × 1.5811 + 19.9 × 1.0828 = 8.4526 + 21.5477 = 30.0003 g calories: 153 × 1.5811 + 146 × 1.0828 = 241.9083 + 158.0888 = 399.9971 kcal

Both are within 0.01 of the targets (30 g and 400 kcal). Step 4 used only the protein equation, so a mistake in Step 2 or 3 shows up only in the calorie check. That is why the check uses both equations.

In the kitchen: about 1.58 servings of oats is 1.58 × ½ cup = 0.79 cup of dry oats (about 64 g), and 1.08 containers is about 216 g of yogurt. With these rounded amounts the totals are 29.94 g and 399.42 kcal. Allowing fractions of a serving is a simplification: a real breakfast uses amounts you can measure, and the totals are then close to the targets, not exact.

The method

  1. Write one equation per target, with the same two unknowns.
  2. Solve one equation for one unknown.
  3. Substitute that expression into the other equation and solve for the remaining unknown.
  4. Put the value back to find the first unknown.
  5. Check the pair in both original equations, and check that it makes sense (no negative amounts).

Either equation can be used in step 2, and you can solve for x instead of y; the answer is the same. Keep four decimals in the expression from step 2 and in the unknown you find in step 3, and at least two decimals in the numbers in between, as in the worked example. Round the answer only at the end. Another method, called elimination, adds or subtracts multiples of the two equations to remove one unknown. It gives the same answer; this course uses substitution.

Task 3 (about 7 minutes, calculator)

A different breakfast with the same targets: x large eggs and y cups of 2% milk, with 30 g of protein and 400 calories. Use Table 1.

Produce: (a) the two equations; (b) the solution by substitution, with each step written out, rounded to two decimals at the end; (c) the check of your rounded answer in both equations.

Solution to Task 3

(a) Protein: 6.3x + 8.052y = 30. Calories: 72x + 122y = 400.

(b) From the protein equation: 8.052y = −6.3x + 30, so y = −(6.3 ÷ 8.052)x + 30 ÷ 8.052 ≈ −0.7824x + 3.7258.

Substitute: 72x + 122(−0.7824x + 3.7258) = 400.

Multiply out: 72x − 95.45x + 454.55 = 400, so −23.45x + 454.55 = 400.

Subtract 454.55 from both sides: −23.45x = −54.55. Divide both sides by −23.45: x ≈ 2.3262 (a negative divided by a negative is positive).

Then y ≈ −0.7824 × 2.3262 + 3.7258 ≈ 1.9058. Rounded: x = 2.33 eggs and y = 1.91 cups of milk.

(c) Protein: 6.3 × 2.33 + 8.052 × 1.91 = 14.679 + 15.379 = 30.058 g ≈ 30 g. Calories: 72 × 2.33 + 122 × 1.91 = 167.76 + 233.02 = 400.78 kcal ≈ 400 kcal. Both are within rounding.

Eggs come whole, so no real breakfast of these two foods meets both targets exactly. With 2 eggs, 2.16 cups of milk give 30 g of protein and about 408 kcal, close to both.

4. When there is no solution, or infinitely many

Parallel lines: large eggs and extra-large eggs

Take large eggs and extra-large eggs as the two foods, with the same targets, 30 g of protein and 400 calories. This is really one food in two sizes, which is what makes it useful here. A large egg weighs 50 g and an extra-large egg 56 g [3], so an extra-large egg is 56 ÷ 50 = 1.12 large eggs. This uses the same nutrition per gram for both sizes, as USDA does; real eggs vary a little. So an extra-large egg has

1.12 × 6.3 = 7.056 g of protein   and   1.12 × 72 = 80.64 kcal.

(USDA's 143 kcal per 100 g gives 80.08 kcal for 56 g. Scaling the 72 kcal of Table 1 instead computes both sizes the same way.)

Task 4 (about 5 minutes, calculator)

Let x be the number of large eggs and y the number of extra-large eggs.

Produce: (a) the protein equation and the calorie equation; (b) each equation in the form y = mx + b, with m to three decimals; (c) the calories in a large egg for each gram of its protein, the same for an extra-large egg, and one sentence explaining why no mix of these eggs has 30 g of protein and 400 calories.

Solution to Task 4

(a) Protein: 6.3x + 7.056y = 30. Calories: 72x + 80.64y = 400.

(b) Protein line: y = −(6.3 ÷ 7.056)x + 30 ÷ 7.056 ≈ −0.893x + 4.252. Calorie line: y = −(72 ÷ 80.64)x + 400 ÷ 80.64 ≈ −0.893x + 4.960. The slopes are equal; both are −50 ÷ 56, the ratio of the egg weights. The y-intercepts are different.

(c) Large: 72 ÷ 6.3 ≈ 11.43 kcal per gram of protein. Extra-large: 80.64 ÷ 7.056 ≈ 11.43 kcal per gram of protein. Any mix of the eggs has 11.43 kcal for every gram of protein, so 30 g of protein always comes with 30 × 72 ÷ 6.3 ≈ 342.86 kcal, never 400.

Two lines with the same slope and different y-intercepts are parallel: they never cross. A system whose lines are parallel has no solution. Here the reason is in the food. One gram of egg has 6.3 ÷ 50 = 0.126 g of protein and 72 ÷ 50 = 1.44 kcal, whatever the size of the egg. Reaching 30 g of protein takes 30 ÷ 0.126 ≈ 238.1 g of egg; reaching 400 kcal takes 400 ÷ 1.44 ≈ 277.8 g of egg. No amount of egg is both.

Substitution shows the same thing in the algebra. Solving the protein equation for y and substituting into the calorie equation makes the x terms cancel, and what is left is the false statement 342.86 = 400 (to two decimals). When substitution removes the unknown and leaves a false statement, the system has no solution.

Identical lines: the same eggs with a different calorie target

Changing the calorie target moves the orange line in Figure 2 without changing its slope. With a target of 30 × 72 ÷ 6.3 ≈ 342.86 kcal, the orange line lands exactly on the blue one: the calorie equation 72x + 80.64y = 30 × 72 ÷ 6.3 is the protein equation with both sides multiplied by 72 ÷ 6.3 ≈ 11.43. Every pair that solves one equation solves the other. A system whose two equations give the same line has infinitely many solutions: every point on the line. Substitution shows this by leaving a statement that is always true, such as 342.86 = 342.86.

Lines that cross at a negative amount: oats and peanut butter

Oats and peanut butter [5] with the targets 30 g and 400 kcal give 5.346x + 7.104y = 30 and 153x + 191y = 400, where y counts 2-tablespoon servings of peanut butter. The slopes are −5.346 ÷ 7.104 ≈ −0.75 and −153 ÷ 191 ≈ −0.80. They differ, so the lines cross, but far off Figure 3: substitution gives x ≈ −43.88 and y ≈ 37.24. That pair satisfies both equations and is not a breakfast, because nobody eats −43.88 servings of oats.

The food explains it. Oats have 153 ÷ 5.346 ≈ 28.6 kcal per gram of protein and peanut butter 191 ÷ 7.104 ≈ 26.9. Any mix of the two has between 26.9 and 28.6 kcal per gram of protein, so 30 g of protein brings at least 30 × 191 ÷ 7.104 ≈ 807 kcal. The target asks for 400 ÷ 30 ≈ 13.3 kcal per gram of protein, which is outside that range. The algebra answers with a negative amount, and the check "no negative amounts" in step 5 of the method rejects it.

5. A third food, a third target, and targets out of reach

Add 2% milk (8.052 g of protein and 122 kcal per cup [4]) to the oats and yogurt, with the same targets of 30 g and 400 kcal. Now there are three unknowns and still two equations. Choose an amount of milk, and the oats and yogurt must cover the rest. With 1 cup of milk, the rest is 30 − 8.052 = 21.948 g of protein and 400 − 122 = 278 kcal, and substitution gives about 1.03 servings of oats and 0.83 containers of yogurt. With ½ cup of milk it gives about 1.30 servings of oats and 0.95 containers of yogurt. Each amount of milk from 0 to about 2.86 cups gives a different breakfast that meets both targets. With more milk than that, the oats would have to be negative. So a third food gives infinitely many breakfasts again, and the freedom to pick the one you like.

A third target, such as a fixed amount of fat, adds a third equation. Three equations in three unknowns usually have exactly one solution, found with the same substitution idea used twice. This course does not go further with them.

Some targets cannot be reached with a given set of foods. Write each food's nutrition as a pair (protein, calories). A breakfast's totals come from multiplying each food's pair by its amount and adding. The set of all totals you can make this way, with any amounts, even negative ones, is called the span of the foods.

  • The span of large and extra-large eggs is a single line of totals: every total has 11.43 kcal per gram of protein. The target (30 g, 400 kcal) is off that line, so no amounts of these eggs reach it, even negative ones.
  • The span of oats and yogurt is every (protein, calories) pair: their lines always have different slopes, so substitution always finds one solution. With these two foods the only obstacle is that real amounts cannot be negative, as with oats and peanut butter in Section 4.

Task 5 is about that second obstacle, for all five foods.

Task 5 (about 3 minutes, calculator)

Produce: (a) the calories per gram of protein for each of the five foods in Table 1, to two decimals; (b) the smallest number of calories a breakfast made from these foods can have if it has 30 g of protein; (c) a yes or no: can a breakfast from these five foods have 30 g of protein and 200 kcal?

Solution to Task 5

(a) Oats 153 ÷ 5.346 ≈ 28.62; yogurt 146 ÷ 19.9 ≈ 7.34; egg 72 ÷ 6.3 ≈ 11.43; milk 122 ÷ 8.052 ≈ 15.15; peanut butter 191 ÷ 7.104 ≈ 26.89.

(b) Every food has at least 7.34 kcal per gram of protein, so any mix does too. The least is 30 × 146 ÷ 19.9 ≈ 220 kcal, from 30 ÷ 19.9 ≈ 1.51 containers of yogurt and nothing else.

(c) No. 200 kcal is below the 220 kcal minimum, so the target is out of reach with these five foods in any non-negative amounts. Reaching it would need a food with fewer than 200 ÷ 30 ≈ 6.67 kcal per gram of protein.
